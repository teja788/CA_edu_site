A vegetable seller in a Pune mandi drops the price of tomatoes from ₹ 40 a kilo to ₹ 30 near closing
time. Within minutes the crate empties. She has just used, without a textbook, the single most
important idea in this chapter: when the price falls, buyers want more. Raise it again tomorrow and
the queue thins. The whole of demand theory is the careful statement of that everyday observation,
and the whole of supply theory is its mirror image on the seller's side.

Paper 4 is an objective paper, and this chapter carries two kinds of question. Most test whether you
can state a law cleanly, tell a movement from a shift, or name the good behind an exception. A large
minority ask you to **compute** — an elasticity from two prices and two quantities, an equilibrium
price from a demand and a supply equation, or the direction in which total revenue moves. The
computations are short. The marks are lost on the sign of an elasticity, on confusing a change in
quantity demanded with a change in demand, and on reading a different question from the one asked.

## 1. Demand: what it means

In economics **demand** is not a wish. It is the quantity of a good that a buyer is **willing and
able to buy at a given price in a given period**. Three conditions must all hold, or there is no
demand in the economic sense.

- **A desire for the good** — the buyer must want it.
- **The ability to pay** — the buyer must have the money. A desire without the means is only a want.
- **A willingness to spend** that money on the good.

Demand backed by the ability and willingness to pay is called **effective demand**, and it is
effective demand that economics studies. A student who longs for a motorcycle but cannot pay for one
adds nothing to its market demand.

Two more points fix the idea. Demand is always stated **at a price** — "the demand for sugar" is
meaningless until a price is named, because the quantity changes with the price. And demand is always
a **rate over a period** — so many units per day, per week, per month. A bare number of units with no
time attached is incomplete.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Examiners open with a definition question surprisingly often. The reliable answer names all three
    conditions — desire, ability to pay, willingness to pay — and adds "at a given price, over a
    given period". An option that says only "the desire to buy a good" is the classic trap; desire
    alone is a <strong>want</strong>, not demand.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. The law of demand and the demand curve

The **law of demand** states that, other things remaining equal, the quantity demanded of a good
rises when its price falls and falls when its price rises. Price and quantity demanded move in
**opposite** directions — an inverse relationship.

<div class="formula">Other things equal: price up → quantity demanded down; price down → quantity demanded up</div>

The phrase "other things remaining equal" is doing real work, and it has a Latin name, **ceteris
paribus**. It means the law holds only while every other influence on demand — income, tastes, the
prices of related goods, expectations — is held still. Change the price alone, and quantity demanded
moves the other way. Let income change at the same time and the simple inverse link can be masked.

A **demand schedule** is a table of prices against the quantity demanded at each. Plot it with price
on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis, and the points trace the **demand curve**.
Because price and quantity move in opposite directions, the curve slopes **downward from left to
right**.

| Price (₹ per unit) | Quantity demanded (units) |
|---|---|
| 50 | 10 |
| 40 | 20 |
| 30 | 30 |
| 20 | 40 |
| 10 | 50 |

Why does the curve slope down? Three reasons stand behind the law.

- **The law of diminishing marginal utility.** Each extra unit gives the buyer less added
  satisfaction than the one before, so the buyer will pay less for it — more is bought only at a
  lower price. Section 6 develops this.
- **The income effect.** A price fall leaves the buyer with more real purchasing power, some of which
  is spent on the now-cheaper good.
- **The substitution effect.** When one good becomes cheaper relative to its substitutes, buyers
  switch toward it.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    "Quantity demanded" and "demand" are not interchangeable, and the law is stated in terms of
    <strong>quantity demanded</strong>. The law of demand describes a movement <em>along one fixed
    curve</em> as the price changes. It does <strong>not</strong> say that demand rises when price
    falls in the sense of the whole curve moving. Keeping the two ideas apart is the subject of
    Section 4, and examiners test the difference constantly.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. Individual demand, market demand, and the determinants

An **individual demand** curve is one buyer's schedule. The **market demand** is the total quantity
all buyers together demand at each price. It is found by **adding the individual quantities
horizontally** — at each price, sum the quantity every buyer demands.

<div class="formula">Market quantity at a price = sum of every individual buyer's quantity at that price</div>

So if at ₹ 20 buyer A wants 8 units, B wants 14 and C wants 23, the market demands 8 + 14 + 23 = 45
units at ₹ 20. Repeat at every price and the market demand schedule is complete. The market curve is
flatter than any one buyer's curve, because a price cut pulls in extra units from every buyer at once
and, at low enough prices, brings new buyers into the market.

**The determinants of demand.** Price is the immediate influence, but demand for a good depends on a
list of factors held constant by the ceteris-paribus clause. When one of them changes, the whole
curve moves.

- **The good's own price** — the movement captured by the law of demand.
- **Income of the buyer.** For a **normal** good, demand rises with income; for an **inferior** good,
  demand falls as income rises, because the buyer switches to a better substitute.
- **Prices of related goods.** For **substitutes** (tea and coffee), a rise in the price of one
  raises the demand for the other. For **complements** (cars and petrol), a rise in the price of one
  lowers the demand for the other.
- **Tastes and preferences** — fashion, habit, advertising, season.
- **Expectations** about future prices and incomes.
- **Population and its composition**, and the **distribution of income**, for market demand.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The determinants list is worth memorising because a whole family of questions runs "which of the
    following will <strong>not</strong> shift the demand curve for good X?" — and the answer is almost
    always "a change in the price of X itself", because that produces a movement <em>along</em> the
    curve, not a shift <em>of</em> it. Every other item on the list shifts the curve.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Movement along a curve versus a shift of the curve

This distinction is the most heavily tested idea in the chapter, and it rests on one rule: **only the
good's own price moves you along the curve; every other change shifts the whole curve.**

**A movement along the demand curve** is caused by a change in the good's **own price**, with
everything else held constant. It is called a **change in quantity demanded**. A fall in price gives
an **extension** (downward along the curve to a larger quantity); a rise gives a **contraction**
(upward along the curve to a smaller quantity).

**A shift of the whole demand curve** is caused by a change in any determinant **other than the
good's own price** — income, tastes, the price of a related good, expectations. It is called a
**change in demand**. The curve moving **rightward** is an **increase in demand** (more is demanded
at every price); moving **leftward** is a **decrease in demand** (less at every price).

| What changed | What happens on the diagram | The name |
|---|---|---|
| The good's own price | movement **along** the fixed curve | change in **quantity demanded** (extension or contraction) |
| Income, tastes, related prices, expectations | the whole curve **shifts** | change in **demand** (increase or decrease) |

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    A rise in consumers' income raises demand for a normal good — but this is an <strong>increase in
    demand</strong>, a rightward shift, <em>not</em> a movement along the curve. Describing it as
    "quantity demanded rises because price fell" is doubly wrong: the price did not fall, and the
    effect is a shift, not a movement. Match the cause to the vocabulary: own price → movement;
    anything else → shift.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Exceptions to the law of demand

For a handful of goods the demand curve slopes **upward** over some range: more is bought at a higher
price. These are the exceptions to the law of demand.

- **Giffen goods.** Named after Robert Giffen, these are strongly **inferior** staples that take a
  large share of a poor household's budget. When the price of the staple (the classic example is a
  cheap food grain) rises, the household becomes so much poorer in real terms that it can no longer
  afford the costlier foods it used to add, and buys **more** of the cheap staple instead. Here the
  negative income effect outweighs the substitution effect, and quantity demanded rises with price. A
  Giffen good is the one genuine theoretical exception to the law.
- **Veblen or conspicuous goods.** Named after Thorstein Veblen, these are luxury goods — diamonds,
  premium brands — bought partly to display status. A higher price can make them **more** desirable,
  because the price itself signals prestige. Demand rises with price over a range.
- **Goods bought on speculation / expectation of further price change.** When buyers expect a price
  that is already rising to rise **further**, they buy more now to beat the increase — as with shares
  in a rising market. The current higher price is met with larger, not smaller, purchases.
- **Articles of necessity and emergencies.** In a crisis, or for a bare necessity, buyers may buy
  more even as the price climbs, fearing worse to come.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A common MCQ asks you to pick the good that is a <strong>genuine</strong> exception in the strict
    theoretical sense. That answer is the <strong>Giffen good</strong>: an inferior good whose income
    effect is strong enough to reverse the law. Veblen goods and speculative buying are real
    exceptions too, but if the question stresses "inferior good" and "income effect outweighs the
    substitution effect", it is pointing straight at Giffen.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. The utility basis of demand

Why is the demand curve downward-sloping? The older, **cardinal** explanation measures satisfaction
in imaginary units called **utils** and rests on one law.

**Total utility (TU)** is the whole satisfaction from consuming a quantity of a good. **Marginal
utility (MU)** is the addition to total utility from consuming **one more** unit.

<div class="formula">Marginal utility of the nth unit = TU of n units − TU of (n − 1) units</div>

The **law of diminishing marginal utility** states that, as a person consumes more of a good, the
marginal utility of each successive unit **falls**. The first glass of water on a hot day is worth a
great deal; the fifth adds little. Because each extra unit is worth less to the buyer, the buyer will
pay less for it — which is exactly why more is bought only at a lower price, and why the demand curve
slopes down.

The relationship between the two curves is worth stating precisely:

- While **MU is positive**, TU is still **rising**.
- When **MU is zero**, TU is at its **maximum** — the point of satiation.
- When **MU is negative**, TU **falls** — an extra unit now reduces total satisfaction.

**Consumer equilibrium (cardinal).** A buyer spending on one good stops where the marginal utility of
the last rupee spent equals the price — where MU equals price (both in money terms). Spending across
**two** goods, X and Y, the buyer is in equilibrium when the **marginal utility per rupee is equal**
across the goods:

<div class="formula">MU of X ÷ price of X = MU of Y ÷ price of Y</div>

This is the **law of equi-marginal utility**. If a rupee spent on X yielded more utility than a rupee
spent on Y, the buyer would move spending toward X until the two ratios came back into balance.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Total utility is greatest not where marginal utility is greatest but where marginal utility is
    <strong>zero</strong>. A rising total utility and a falling marginal utility sit together
    perfectly: each extra unit still adds something (MU positive, so TU rising) but adds
    <em>less</em> than the unit before (MU falling). Reading "MU falling" as "TU falling" is the trap.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. Consumer surplus

**Consumer surplus** is the gap between what a buyer **would have been willing to pay** for a good
and what the buyer **actually pays**. It measures the benefit the buyer gets over and above the
price.

<div class="formula">Consumer surplus = what the buyer was willing to pay − what the buyer actually paid</div>

Suppose a buyer would have paid ₹ 50 for the first unit, ₹ 40 for the second and ₹ 30 for the third,
but the market price is ₹ 30 and all three are bought at ₹ 30 each. The buyer pays 3 × 30 = ₹ 90 but
values the three units at 50 + 40 + 30 = ₹ 120. The consumer surplus is 120 − 90 = ₹ 30. On a
diagram it is the area **below the demand curve and above the price line**. Consumer surplus is large
for a good the buyer values far above its price — necessities like salt — and small for a good bought
close to the most the buyer would pay.

## 8. The indifference-curve approach

The newer, **ordinal** theory does not measure satisfaction in utils. It only asks the buyer to
**rank** bundles of goods, and it derives demand from that ranking.

An **indifference curve** shows all the combinations of two goods that give the buyer the **same**
satisfaction — the buyer is indifferent between every point on one curve. A set of such curves is an
**indifference map**; a curve **further from the origin** represents a higher level of satisfaction,
because it carries more of both goods.

The properties worth knowing:

- **Indifference curves slope downward** from left to right. To keep satisfaction constant, giving up
  some of one good must be compensated by more of the other.
- **They are convex to the origin.** The rate at which the buyer will swap one good for the other —
  the **marginal rate of substitution** — falls as you move along the curve, because the more you
  hold of a good the less you will give up of the other to get one more unit of it.
- **They never intersect.** Two curves crossing would mean one bundle gave two different levels of
  satisfaction, a contradiction.
- **Higher curves mean higher satisfaction.**

The **budget line** shows every combination of the two goods the buyer can **just afford** given
income and the two prices. Its slope is the ratio of the two prices. A rise in income shifts the
budget line outward parallel to itself; a change in one price swings the line about its intercept on
the other axis.

**Consumer equilibrium (ordinal).** The buyer reaches the highest attainable satisfaction where the
**budget line is tangent to the highest indifference curve it can touch** — the point where the two
just meet. At that point the slope of the indifference curve equals the slope of the budget line, so
the marginal rate of substitution equals the price ratio.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Two facts about indifference curves are asked again and again: they are <strong>convex to the
    origin</strong> and they <strong>cannot intersect</strong>. Equilibrium is the point of
    <strong>tangency</strong> between the budget line and the highest reachable indifference curve —
    not the point where the budget line crosses a curve, and not the highest curve on the map
    regardless of the budget.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Elasticity of demand: the price elasticity

The law of demand says quantity demanded moves against price, but not by **how much**.
**Elasticity** measures the size of the response. **Price elasticity of demand** is the
responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in the good's own price, and it is the ratio of two
**percentage** changes.

<div class="formula">Price elasticity e = (percentage change in quantity demanded) ÷ (percentage change in price)</div>

Because quantity moves opposite to price, the ratio is negative; by convention it is usually quoted
as an **absolute value**, ignoring the minus sign. So an elasticity written as "2" means "2 in
magnitude". Keep this convention in mind: for price elasticity a bare number nearly always means the
absolute value, while for income and cross elasticity the **sign is kept**, because the sign carries
the meaning (Section 13).

The percentage changes are computed from the base values:

<div class="formula">Percentage change in quantity = (change in quantity ÷ original quantity) × 100</div>
<div class="formula">Percentage change in price = (change in price ÷ original price) × 100</div>

## 10. Measuring price elasticity: percentage, arc and point

There are three ways to put a number on price elasticity, and the exam uses all three.

**The percentage (proportionate) method.** Compute each percentage change from the **original**
values and divide. This is the workhorse for a single "from-to" change.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — price ₹ 20 → ₹ 25, quantity 400 → 340</div>
  Percentage change in quantity = (60 ÷ 400) × 100 = 15% fall<br />
  Percentage change in price = (5 ÷ 20) × 100 = 25% rise<br />
  e = 15 ÷ 25 = <strong>0.6</strong> (in magnitude)<br />
  Since e is below 1, demand for this good is <strong>inelastic</strong> — quantity moved
  proportionately less than price.
</div>

**The arc method.** When the change in price is large, the answer differs depending on which end you
call the "original" value. The arc method removes the ambiguity by using the **average** of the two
prices and the **average** of the two quantities as the base.

<div class="formula">Arc e = (change in quantity ÷ average quantity) ÷ (change in price ÷ average price)</div>
<div class="formula">= [ (Q₂ − Q₁) ÷ (Q₁ + Q₂) ] ÷ [ (P₂ − P₁) ÷ (P₁ + P₂) ]  (the ×2 in each average cancels)</div>

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — price ₹ 8 → ₹ 12, quantity 200 → 120</div>
  Change in quantity = 80 (fall); sum of quantities = 320<br />
  Change in price = 4 (rise); sum of prices = 20<br />
  Arc e = (80 ÷ 320) ÷ (4 ÷ 20) = 0.25 ÷ 0.20 = <strong>1.25</strong> (in magnitude)<br />
  Above 1, so demand is <strong>elastic</strong> over this arc.
</div>

**The point method.** For the elasticity at a single point on the curve, use the slope of the demand
curve at that point together with the price and quantity there.

<div class="formula">Point e = (change in quantity ÷ change in price) × (price ÷ quantity), taken in magnitude</div>

For a straight-line demand equation such as Q = a − bP, the term (change in quantity ÷ change in
price) is simply the slope −b, so point e = b × (P ÷ Q) in magnitude.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — for Q = 300 − 6P, find e at P = 20</div>
  Slope: change in quantity per unit change in price = −6<br />
  At P = 20, quantity Q = 300 − 6(20) = 180<br />
  Point e = 6 × (20 ÷ 180) = 120 ÷ 180 = <strong>2/3 ≈ 0.67</strong> (in magnitude)<br />
  Below 1, so demand at this price is inelastic.
</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The percentage method gives a different number depending on whether you treat the low price or
    the high price as "original" — that is exactly the ambiguity the arc method exists to cure. If a
    question gives two widely separated prices and simply says "find the elasticity", the arc method
    (average base) is the safe reading. If it says "at a price of ₹ 20", it wants the point method.
    Match the method to the wording.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 11. The five degrees of price elasticity

The number e falls into five named bands. Learn the boundary values, because most concept questions
turn on them.

| Value of e (magnitude) | Name | What it means | Demand curve |
|---|---|---|---|
| e = 0 | perfectly inelastic | quantity does not change at all when price changes | vertical straight line |
| e &lt; 1 (between 0 and 1) | inelastic | quantity changes proportionately **less** than price | steep |
| e = 1 | unit (unitary) elastic | quantity changes in the **same** proportion as price | rectangular hyperbola |
| e &gt; 1 | elastic | quantity changes proportionately **more** than price | flat |
| e = ∞ | perfectly elastic | buyers take any amount at one price, none above it | horizontal straight line |

Two boundary cases anchor the scale. **Perfectly inelastic** demand (e = 0) is drawn as a
**vertical** line — the same quantity whatever the price, as for a life-saving medicine over a range.
**Perfectly elastic** demand (e = ∞) is drawn as a **horizontal** line — the good sells in any
quantity at the going price and none at all above it, the theoretical case of a single seller in a
perfectly competitive market.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The two extremes are the ones students swap. <strong>Perfectly inelastic = vertical</strong> (e =
    0); <strong>perfectly elastic = horizontal</strong> (e = ∞). A memory hook: an inelastic buyer
    stands firm at one quantity (a firm, upright vertical line), while a perfectly elastic buyer is
    completely flexible on quantity (a flat, horizontal line).
  </p>
</Callout>

## 12. Elasticity and total revenue

**Total revenue (TR)** is the seller's receipts: price multiplied by quantity sold.

<div class="formula">Total revenue = price × quantity sold</div>

Price elasticity tells the seller what a price change does to total revenue, because a price cut has
two opposing effects — a lower price per unit, but more units sold. Which effect wins depends on
elasticity.

| Demand is | A price **fall** does | A price **rise** does |
|---|---|---|
| elastic (e &gt; 1) | TR **rises** (the quantity gain outweighs the lower price) | TR **falls** |
| unit elastic (e = 1) | TR **unchanged** | TR **unchanged** |
| inelastic (e &lt; 1) | TR **falls** (the quantity gain is too small) | TR **rises** |

The link is the fastest way to read elasticity off a real situation. If a shop cuts its price and
finds its takings **rise**, demand for its goods must be **elastic**. If a bus fare rises and total
collections **rise**, demand for the ride is **inelastic**.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — price ₹ 40 → ₹ 30, quantity 500 → 800</div>
  Total revenue before = 40 × 500 = ₹ 20,000<br />
  Total revenue after = 30 × 800 = ₹ 24,000<br />
  The price fell yet total revenue <strong>rose by ₹ 4,000</strong>, so demand over this range is
  <strong>elastic</strong> — consistent with the percentage elasticity, which here exceeds 1.
</div>

## 13. Income elasticity and cross elasticity

Price is not the only thing quantity demanded responds to. Two further elasticities matter, and for
both of them the **sign is kept**, because the sign classifies the good.

**Income elasticity of demand** measures the response of quantity demanded to a change in the buyer's
**income**.

<div class="formula">Income elasticity = (percentage change in quantity demanded) ÷ (percentage change in income)</div>

The sign and size classify the good:

- **Positive** income elasticity → a **normal** good (demand rises with income). Within this,
  income elasticity **greater than 1** marks a **luxury** (demand rises faster than income), and
  between 0 and 1 a **necessity**.
- **Negative** income elasticity → an **inferior** good (demand falls as income rises).

**Cross elasticity of demand** measures the response of the quantity demanded of one good, X, to a
change in the **price of another** good, Y.

<div class="formula">Cross elasticity of X = (percentage change in quantity of X) ÷ (percentage change in price of Y)</div>

The sign classifies the relationship:

- **Positive** cross elasticity → the goods are **substitutes** (tea dearer, so more coffee bought).
- **Negative** cross elasticity → the goods are **complements** (petrol dearer, so fewer cars bought).
- **Zero** cross elasticity → the goods are **unrelated**.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — income and cross elasticity</div>
  Income rises 20%, quantity of good X rises 30% → income elasticity = 30 ÷ 20 = <strong>+1.5</strong>
  → normal good, and a luxury (above 1).<br />
  Income rises 25%, quantity of good Y falls 15% → income elasticity = −15 ÷ 25 = <strong>−0.6</strong>
  → inferior good.<br />
  Price of tea rises 20%, quantity of coffee rises 10% → cross elasticity = +10 ÷ 20 =
  <strong>+0.5</strong> → substitutes.<br />
  Price of a printer rises 10%, quantity of its cartridges falls 25% → cross elasticity =
  −25 ÷ 10 = <strong>−2.5</strong> → complements.
</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    For income and cross elasticity, dropping the sign discards the answer. A cross elasticity of
    <strong>+0.5</strong> means substitutes; <strong>−0.5</strong> means complements — same
    magnitude, opposite economic meaning. Only for <em>price</em> elasticity is it safe to quote the
    magnitude alone, because the sign is always negative there. Read what the question asks: if it
    asks whether two goods are substitutes or complements, the sign <em>is</em> the answer.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 14. What determines price elasticity

Whether a good's demand is elastic or inelastic is not random; it follows a few factors.

- **Availability of close substitutes.** The more (and closer) the substitutes, the **more elastic**
  the demand — buyers switch away easily. A single brand of soap has elastic demand; salt as a whole
  has inelastic demand.
- **Necessity or luxury.** Necessities have **inelastic** demand (bought whatever the price);
  luxuries have **elastic** demand.
- **Share of income spent on the good.** A good taking a large share of the budget has **more
  elastic** demand, because a price change is felt keenly; a trifling item (a box of matches) has
  inelastic demand.
- **Time.** Demand is **more elastic in the long run**, because buyers have time to find substitutes
  and change habits.
- **Number of uses.** A good with many uses has more elastic demand, because at a high price it is
  reserved for its most important use only.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The single most testable determinant is <strong>substitutes</strong>: more and closer substitutes
    mean more elastic demand. If an MCQ asks which good is most price-elastic, pick the one with the
    readiest substitutes (one brand among many); if it asks which is most inelastic, pick the
    necessity with no good substitute (salt, a life-saving drug).
  </p>
</Callout>

## 15. Supply: the law and the curve

**Supply** is the quantity of a good that sellers are **willing to offer for sale at a given price**
in a given period. Like demand, it is stated at a price and over a period, and it means quantity
actually offered, not merely produced or held in stock.

The **law of supply** states that, other things remaining equal, the quantity supplied of a good
**rises** when its price rises and **falls** when its price falls. Price and quantity supplied move in
the **same** direction — a direct relationship — because a higher price makes production more
profitable and draws out more output.

<div class="formula">Other things equal: price up → quantity supplied up; price down → quantity supplied down</div>

Plot price against quantity supplied and the **supply curve** slopes **upward from left to right**.
As with demand, a change in the good's **own price** produces a **movement along** the supply curve
(an extension when price rises, a contraction when it falls), while a change in any other determinant
**shifts** the whole curve.

**The determinants of supply**, held constant along one curve, are: the **prices of inputs** (higher
costs shift supply left), the **state of technology** (better technology shifts supply right), the
**prices of other goods** the firm could make instead, **taxes and subsidies** (a tax shifts supply
left, a subsidy right), the **number of sellers**, and **expectations** about future prices. A
**rightward** shift is an **increase in supply** (more offered at every price); a **leftward** shift
is a **decrease in supply**.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    A fall in the price of a raw material lowers costs and <strong>increases supply</strong> — the
    whole curve shifts right. This is not a movement along the supply curve, because the good's
    <em>own</em> price has not changed. As on the demand side: only the good's own price moves you
    along the curve; a change in costs, technology or taxes shifts it.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 16. Elasticity of supply

**Price elasticity of supply** measures how responsive the quantity supplied is to a change in the
good's price. Its form mirrors price elasticity of demand, but it is normally **positive**, because
quantity supplied and price move together.

<div class="formula">Elasticity of supply = (percentage change in quantity supplied) ÷ (percentage change in price)</div>

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — two supply-elasticity calculations</div>
  Quantity supplied rises 30% when price rises 12% → elasticity = 30 ÷ 12 = <strong>2.5</strong>
  (elastic supply).<br />
  Price ₹ 50 → ₹ 60 (a 20% rise); quantity supplied 300 → 345 (a 15% rise) →
  elasticity = 15 ÷ 20 = <strong>0.75</strong> (inelastic supply).
</div>

The same five degrees apply: supply is **perfectly inelastic** (e = 0, a vertical line) when quantity
cannot change with price — the fixed supply of a rare painting, or of any good in the very short run;
and **perfectly elastic** (e = ∞, a horizontal line) when any quantity is offered at one price and
none below it. **Time** is the chief influence: supply is more elastic the longer the period, because
firms have time to vary their output.

## 17. Market equilibrium

A market brings buyers and sellers together. **Equilibrium** is the price at which the quantity
buyers demand exactly equals the quantity sellers supply — the price clears the market, leaving no
unsatisfied buyer and no unsold unit. On a diagram it is the point where the demand curve and the
supply curve **cross**.

<div class="formula">At equilibrium: quantity demanded = quantity supplied</div>

To find it from equations, set the demand quantity equal to the supply quantity, solve for the
**equilibrium price**, then put that price back into either equation to get the **equilibrium
quantity**.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — Qd = 200 − 4P and Qs = 6P − 100</div>
  Set demand equal to supply: 200 − 4P = 6P − 100<br />
  200 + 100 = 6P + 4P → 300 = 10P → <strong>equilibrium price P = ₹ 30</strong><br />
  Quantity from demand: 200 − 4(30) = 200 − 120 = <strong>80 units</strong><br />
  Check in supply: 6(30) − 100 = 180 − 100 = 80 ✓ — both sides give the same quantity, and that
  agreement is the whole point of checking.
</div>

Away from equilibrium the market corrects itself. **Above** the equilibrium price, quantity supplied
exceeds quantity demanded — a **surplus** — and sellers cut the price to clear stock, pushing the
price down toward equilibrium. **Below** the equilibrium price, quantity demanded exceeds quantity
supplied — a **shortage** — and competing buyers bid the price up toward equilibrium.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — surplus at a price above equilibrium</div>
  With Qd = 200 − 4P and Qs = 6P − 100, take a price of ₹ 40, above the equilibrium of ₹ 30.<br />
  Quantity demanded = 200 − 4(40) = 40 · Quantity supplied = 6(40) − 100 = 140<br />
  Supply exceeds demand by 140 − 40 = <strong>100 units — a surplus</strong>, which pushes the price
  back down toward ₹ 30.
</div>

## 18. Shifts in demand and supply

Equilibrium moves when a curve shifts. The four basic cases are worth holding as ready results,
because "what happens to price and quantity if …" questions are common.

| The change | Effect on equilibrium **price** | Effect on equilibrium **quantity** |
|---|---|---|
| **Demand increases** (curve shifts right) | rises | rises |
| **Demand decreases** (curve shifts left) | falls | falls |
| **Supply increases** (curve shifts right) | falls | rises |
| **Supply decreases** (curve shifts left) | rises | falls |

The pattern is easy to hold: a **demand** shift moves price and quantity in the **same** direction;
a **supply** shift moves them in **opposite** directions.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — a demand increase, computed</div>
  Start from Qd = 200 − 4P and Qs = 6P − 100, equilibrium price ₹ 30.<br />
  Demand rises by 50 units at every price, so the new demand is Qd = 250 − 4P.<br />
  New equilibrium: 250 − 4P = 6P − 100 → 350 = 10P → <strong>P = ₹ 35</strong>, up from ₹ 30.<br />
  New quantity: 6(35) − 100 = 110, up from 80 — both price and quantity rose, as the table predicts
  for a rightward demand shift.
</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    When <strong>both</strong> curves shift at once, one of price or quantity is predictable and the
    other is ambiguous without the sizes of the shifts. If demand and supply both increase, quantity
    definitely <strong>rises</strong> but the price could go either way. Examiners lean on this: read
    whether the question asks for the certain effect (usually quantity) or claims a certain effect on
    the ambiguous one (usually the trap).
  </p>
</Callout>

## 19. Negative marking and when to leave a question

Paper 4 is entirely objective and carries **negative marking of 0.25 mark for each wrong answer**. A
correct answer earns 1 mark, a wrong one costs 0.25, and an unattempted one scores 0.

The arithmetic is worth holding calmly. A blind guess among four options is right one time in four:
across four such guesses you expect 1 mark gained and 3 × 0.25 = 0.75 lost, a net of +0.25 for four
questions — very slightly positive but close to neutral, with a wide spread. What changes the picture
is **elimination**. Rule out one option and a guess among three is worth about 0.5 marks per four
attempts; rule out two and the last guess is worth about 1.5 marks per four.

In this chapter elimination is unusually easy, because the distractors are built from named errors —
a cross elasticity with the sign dropped, a shift described as a movement, perfectly elastic swapped
with perfectly inelastic. A sensible rule: **answer when you can eliminate at least one option;
consider leaving it only when you cannot narrow the field at all.** Nothing about that rule needs to
be followed anxiously — the marks at stake either way are small, and time spent on a question you can
actually finish is worth more than either choice.

## Fully worked examples

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — market demand and reading the law (easy)">
  <p>
    At a price of ₹ 15 per unit, three buyers demand 8, 14 and 23 units respectively. (a) Find the
    market demand at ₹ 15. (b) The price then falls to ₹ 12 and the same buyers demand 11, 19 and 30.
    Is this a change in demand or a change in quantity demanded?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) add the individual quantities horizontally</div>
    Market demand at ₹ 15 = 8 + 14 + 23 = <strong>45 units</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) name the effect correctly</div>
    Only the good's own price changed (₹ 15 → ₹ 12), so this is a <strong>movement along</strong> the
    demand curve — a <strong>change in quantity demanded</strong> (an extension), not a change in
    demand. Market quantity rises to 11 + 19 + 30 = 60 units, consistent with the law of demand.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — price elasticity three ways (easy-moderate)">
  <p>
    A good's price falls from ₹ 12 to ₹ 10 and the quantity demanded rises from 250 to 300.
    (a) Find the price elasticity by the percentage method using the original values. (b) State
    whether demand is elastic or inelastic, and what that implies for total revenue.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) percentage method</div>
    Percentage change in quantity = (50 ÷ 250) × 100 = 20% rise<br />
    Percentage change in price = (2 ÷ 12) × 100 = 16.67% fall<br />
    e = 20 ÷ 16.67 = <strong>1.2</strong> in magnitude
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) elastic, so revenue rises on a price cut</div>
    e = 1.2 is above 1, so demand is <strong>elastic</strong>. For elastic demand a price fall raises
    total revenue: before, TR = 12 × 250 = ₹ 3,000; after, TR = 10 × 300 = ₹ 3,000 — here it is
    exactly equal, a reminder that near e = 1 the revenue effect is small. (Push the elasticity
    clearly above 1 and the cut would raise TR.)
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — the arc method for a large price change (moderate)">
  <p>
    When the price of a commodity rises from ₹ 8 to ₹ 12, the quantity demanded falls from 200 units
    to 120 units. Find the price elasticity by the arc method, and explain why the arc method is the
    right choice here.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — set up the averages</div>
    Change in quantity = 200 − 120 = 80 (a fall); sum of quantities = 200 + 120 = 320<br />
    Change in price = 12 − 8 = 4 (a rise); sum of prices = 8 + 12 = 20
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — divide the proportionate changes</div>
    Arc e = (80 ÷ 320) ÷ (4 ÷ 20) = 0.25 ÷ 0.20 = <strong>1.25</strong> in magnitude → elastic.<br />
    The price change is large (a full 50% of the lower price), so the percentage method would give
    different answers depending on the direction. The arc method uses the average base and gives one
    figure either way — which is exactly why it is used for large changes.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — point elasticity from a demand equation (moderate)">
  <p>
    The demand for a good is Q = 300 − 6P, where P is the price in rupees. Find the price elasticity
    of demand at P = 20, and say whether a small price rise would raise or lower total revenue there.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — slope, then price and quantity at the point</div>
    From Q = 300 − 6P, the quantity changes by −6 for each ₹ 1 of price, so the slope term is 6 in
    magnitude.<br />
    At P = 20, Q = 300 − 6(20) = 180
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — apply the point formula</div>
    Point e = 6 × (20 ÷ 180) = 120 ÷ 180 = <strong>2/3 ≈ 0.67</strong> in magnitude → inelastic.<br />
    Because demand is inelastic at this price, a small price <strong>rise</strong> would <strong>raise
    total revenue</strong> — the quantity lost is proportionately smaller than the price gained.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — income and cross elasticity, and what they classify (moderate)">
  <p>
    (a) A household's income rises from ₹ 16,000 to ₹ 20,000 a month, and its monthly demand for a
    coarse grain falls from 40 kg to 34 kg. Find the income elasticity and classify the good.
    (b) When the price of a printer rises 10%, the quantity of its ink cartridges demanded falls 25%.
    Find the cross elasticity and classify the relationship.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) income elasticity, sign kept</div>
    Percentage change in quantity = (6 ÷ 40) × 100 = 15% fall → −15%<br />
    Percentage change in income = (4,000 ÷ 16,000) × 100 = 25% rise → +25%<br />
    Income elasticity = −15 ÷ 25 = <strong>−0.6</strong><br />
    The sign is negative, so the grain is an <strong>inferior good</strong> — demand falls as income
    rises.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) cross elasticity, sign kept</div>
    Cross elasticity = (−25%) ÷ (+10%) = <strong>−2.5</strong><br />
    The sign is negative, so the printer and its cartridges are <strong>complements</strong> — a
    dearer printer drags down the demand for its cartridges. Dropping the sign here would lose the
    whole answer.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 6 — elasticity read from total revenue (moderate)">
  <p>
    A bus operator raises the fare on a route from ₹ 20 to ₹ 25. Daily ridership falls from 1,000 to
    900. (a) What happens to total revenue? (b) Is demand for this route elastic or inelastic, and
    confirm it with the percentage elasticity.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) compare total revenue before and after</div>
    Before: TR = 20 × 1,000 = ₹ 20,000<br />
    After: TR = 25 × 900 = ₹ 22,500<br />
    The fare rose and total revenue <strong>rose by ₹ 2,500</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) a price rise that raises TR means inelastic</div>
    When a price rise raises total revenue, demand is <strong>inelastic</strong>.<br />
    Check: percentage change in quantity = (100 ÷ 1,000) × 100 = 10% fall; percentage change in price
    = (5 ÷ 20) × 100 = 25% rise; e = 10 ÷ 25 = 0.4, which is below 1 — inelastic, as the revenue test
    said.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 7 — market equilibrium from equations (moderate)">
  <p>
    In a market the demand is Qd = 500 − 10P and the supply is Qs = 40P − 500, with P in rupees.
    Find the equilibrium price and quantity, and state what would exist at a price of ₹ 15.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — set demand equal to supply</div>
    500 − 10P = 40P − 500<br />
    500 + 500 = 40P + 10P → 1,000 = 50P → <strong>P = ₹ 20</strong><br />
    Quantity: 500 − 10(20) = <strong>300 units</strong> · Check in supply: 40(20) − 500 = 300 ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — a price below equilibrium</div>
    At ₹ 15 (below ₹ 20): quantity demanded = 500 − 150 = 350; quantity supplied = 600 − 500 = 100.<br />
    Demand exceeds supply by 350 − 100 = <strong>250 units — a shortage</strong>, which would push the
    price up toward ₹ 20.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 8 — a shift in demand, computed and named (moderate-hard)">
  <p>
    A market has Qd = 200 − 4P and Qs = 6P − 100. Incomes rise and demand increases by 50 units at
    every price. (a) Find the old and new equilibrium. (b) Describe the effect on price and quantity,
    and say whether this is a movement or a shift.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) old equilibrium</div>
    200 − 4P = 6P − 100 → 300 = 10P → P = ₹ 30, quantity = 200 − 120 = 80 units
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (a) new equilibrium after the demand shift</div>
    New demand: Qd = 250 − 4P<br />
    250 − 4P = 6P − 100 → 350 = 10P → <strong>P = ₹ 35</strong>, quantity = 6(35) − 100 =
    <strong>110 units</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (b) name it</div>
    Both the equilibrium price (₹ 30 → ₹ 35) and quantity (80 → 110) <strong>rose</strong>, exactly
    what a rightward <strong>shift</strong> of demand does. Because the cause was income, not the
    good's own price, this is a <strong>change in demand (a shift)</strong>, not a movement along the
    curve.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 9 — consumer surplus from a willingness-to-pay schedule (easy-moderate)">
  <p>
    A buyer would pay at most ₹ 50, ₹ 45, ₹ 38 and ₹ 30 for the first, second, third and fourth unit
    of a good. The market price is ₹ 30, and the buyer buys all four units at ₹ 30 each. Find the
    consumer surplus.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — value placed on the units versus amount paid</div>
    Total willingness to pay = 50 + 45 + 38 + 30 = ₹ 163<br />
    Amount actually paid = 4 × 30 = ₹ 120
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — the surplus is the gap</div>
    Consumer surplus = 163 − 120 = <strong>₹ 43</strong><br />
    The fourth unit adds nothing to the surplus, because the buyer values it at exactly the ₹ 30
    paid — the marginal unit is where willingness to pay meets the price.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes and examiner traps

- **Movement versus shift.** A change in the good's **own price** is a movement along the curve (a
  change in quantity demanded); a change in income, tastes, related prices or expectations shifts the
  whole curve (a change in demand). Matching the cause to the correct name is the single most tested
  point.
- **Dropping the sign of income or cross elasticity.** The sign classifies the good: negative income
  elasticity means inferior; positive cross elasticity means substitutes, negative means
  complements. Only price elasticity is safely quoted as a magnitude.
- **Perfectly elastic and perfectly inelastic swapped.** Perfectly inelastic (e = 0) is **vertical**;
  perfectly elastic (e = ∞) is **horizontal**.
- **Total utility versus marginal utility.** Total utility is maximum where marginal utility is
  **zero**, not where it is greatest. MU falling while TU still rises is normal.
- **The wrong base for a percentage change.** The percentage method divides the change by the
  **original** value. For a large price change use the arc method (average base) to avoid a
  direction-dependent answer.
- **Confusing the two equilibrium unknowns.** Solve demand = supply for the **price** first, then
  substitute to get the **quantity**. Answering the price when the quantity was asked, or vice versa,
  is a common slip.
- **Demand shift versus supply shift on price.** A demand shift moves price and quantity the **same**
  way; a supply shift moves them **opposite** ways. When both shift, only one of the two is
  predictable.
- **Giffen versus Veblen.** A Giffen good is an **inferior** good whose income effect reverses the
  law; a Veblen good is a **luxury** bought for status. If the question stresses "inferior" and
  "income effect", it means Giffen.
- **Elastic and inelastic total-revenue direction.** For **elastic** demand, price and total revenue
  move **opposite** ways; for **inelastic** demand, they move the **same** way; at **unit** elastic,
  revenue does not change.

## One-page revision summary

**Demand** = quantity a buyer is willing and able to buy at a given price over a period (effective
demand). **Law of demand:** price and quantity demanded move in **opposite** directions, ceteris
paribus; the demand curve slopes **downward**.

**Market demand** = the horizontal sum of individual demands. **Determinants:** own price, income,
prices of related goods, tastes, expectations, population.

**Movement vs shift:** own price changes → movement along the curve (change in **quantity
demanded**); any other determinant changes → shift of the curve (change in **demand**).

**Exceptions:** Giffen goods (inferior, income effect dominates), Veblen/conspicuous goods,
speculative buying, emergencies. The Giffen good is the strict theoretical exception.

**Utility:** MU falls as more is consumed (diminishing marginal utility); TU is maximum when MU = 0.
Consumer equilibrium: MUx ÷ Px = MUy ÷ Py. **Consumer surplus** = willingness to pay − amount paid.

**Indifference curves:** downward-sloping, convex to the origin, never intersect, higher = better.
Equilibrium at the **tangency** of the budget line with the highest reachable curve.

**Price elasticity** e = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔP (quoted in magnitude). Percentage method (original base), arc
method (average base, for large changes), point method (slope × P ÷ Q).

<div class="formula">Five degrees: e = 0 perfectly inelastic (vertical) · e &lt; 1 inelastic · e = 1 unit elastic · e &gt; 1 elastic · e = ∞ perfectly elastic (horizontal)</div>

**Elasticity and total revenue (TR = P × Q):** elastic → price and TR move opposite ways; unit
elastic → TR unchanged; inelastic → price and TR move the same way.

**Income elasticity** = %ΔQ ÷ %ΔincomeI (sign kept): positive → normal (above 1 luxury, 0 to 1
necessity); negative → inferior. **Cross elasticity** = %ΔQ of X ÷ %ΔP of Y (sign kept): positive →
substitutes; negative → complements; zero → unrelated.

**Determinants of elasticity:** more/closer substitutes, luxuries, large budget share, longer time,
more uses → more elastic.

**Supply** = quantity offered at a given price. **Law of supply:** price and quantity supplied move
in the **same** direction; the supply curve slopes **upward**. **Elasticity of supply** = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP,
normally positive; more elastic over a longer period.

**Equilibrium:** set quantity demanded = quantity supplied, solve for **price**, substitute for
**quantity**. Above equilibrium → surplus (price falls); below → shortage (price rises).

**Shifts:** demand increase → price and quantity rise; demand decrease → both fall; supply increase →
price falls, quantity rises; supply decrease → price rises, quantity falls. Demand shift moves price
and quantity together; supply shift moves them oppositely.

**Marking:** 1 mark correct, −0.25 wrong, 0 unattempted. Guessing among four is close to neutral;
eliminating even one option makes an attempt clearly worthwhile.
