A dosa cart on a Bengaluru street corner runs on four things at once: the stall and griddle it stands
on, the cook who works it, the batter and gas it turns into food, and the owner who decided to set it
up and carries the risk of an empty morning. Add a second cook to one small griddle and the first
few extra hands help a great deal, but pile on a fifth and they only jostle for space — output still
rises, but by less each time. Rent a bigger cart with two griddles and the whole operation scales up
differently again. Those two everyday facts — that adding more of one input to a fixed setup gives
diminishing extra output, and that enlarging the whole setup is a separate question — are the twin
engines of this chapter. Production theory studies what comes out; cost theory puts a rupee figure on
what goes in.

Paper 4 is an objective paper, and this chapter carries two kinds of question. Most test whether you
can state a law cleanly, name a stage, or tell one cost concept from another. A large minority ask
you to **compute** — a marginal product from a total-product schedule, a marginal cost from a cost
table, an average variable cost, or a marginal revenue from a demand schedule. The computations are
short. The marks are lost on confusing average with marginal, on forgetting that fixed cost does not
enter marginal cost, and on reading a different row of the table from the one asked.

# Unit 1 — Theory of Production

## 1. The factors of production

**Production** is the act of creating goods and services that satisfy human wants — more exactly, of
transforming inputs into output that has greater want-satisfying power. The inputs a firm uses are
grouped into four **factors of production**, and each earns its own kind of income.

- **Land.** All the free gifts of nature used in production — the ground itself, and also minerals,
  water, forests and climate. Land is a natural, largely **fixed** factor. Its reward is **rent**.
- **Labour.** Every human effort, mental or physical, directed at production. Labour cannot be stored
  and is inseparable from the worker. Its reward is **wages**.
- **Capital.** The produced means of production — the machines, tools, buildings and stock of raw
  materials a firm uses to produce further goods. Capital is a **man-made** factor, itself the result
  of past production. Its reward is **interest**.
- **Entrepreneur.** The person who brings the other three factors together, decides what and how to
  produce, and bears the **uncertainty and risk** of the venture. The reward is **profit** — what is
  left after the other three factors are paid, and the only reward that can be negative.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Match each factor to its reward, because a one-line MCQ often asks exactly that: land → rent,
    labour → wages, capital → interest, entrepreneur → profit. The entrepreneur is the factor that
    bears <strong>risk and uncertainty</strong> and receives the <strong>residual</strong>, so it is
    the only reward that can be a loss.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. The production function, short run and long run

The **production function** is the technical relationship between the inputs a firm uses and the
maximum output it can produce from them, given the state of technology. Written in general form for
two inputs, labour (L) and capital (K):

<div class="formula">Q = f(L, K) — output is the maximum obtainable from the given quantities of labour and capital</div>

The function says nothing about prices or costs; it is purely a statement of physical possibilities —
so many units of output from so much labour and capital. Two time horizons matter, and they are
defined by which inputs the firm can vary, **not** by any fixed number of days or months.

- **The short run** is a period in which **at least one factor is fixed** (typically capital — the
  plant, the premises, the machines) while others (typically labour and materials) can be varied. The
  firm changes its output only by working the fixed plant more or less intensively. The short run is
  the setting for the **law of variable proportions**.
- **The long run** is a period long enough that **all factors can be varied** — the firm can build a
  bigger plant, install more machines, change everything. There is no fixed factor in the long run.
  The long run is the setting for **returns to scale**.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The short run is not "a few months" and the long run is not "a few years". The distinction is
    about <strong>which inputs can change</strong>: if even one factor is fixed it is the short run;
    when every factor can be varied it is the long run. For a small tea stall the long run might be a
    fortnight; for a steel plant it might be years. Never define the two by clock time in an exam
    answer.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. The law of variable proportions

The **law of variable proportions** — also called the **law of returns to a factor** — describes
what happens to output in the **short run** when one variable factor (say labour) is added in
successive units to a **fixed** factor (say a fixed plot of land or a fixed plant). It is the central
result of short-run production. Three product measures are needed.

- **Total product (TP)** — the total output produced by all the units of the variable factor
  together.
- **Average product (AP)** — output per unit of the variable factor.
- **Marginal product (MP)** — the addition to total product from employing **one more** unit of the
  variable factor.

<div class="formula">Average product AP = TP ÷ L (total product divided by the units of the variable factor)</div>
<div class="formula">Marginal product MP = ΔTP ÷ ΔL = TP of n units − TP of (n − 1) units</div>

The schedule below adds workers one at a time to a fixed plot of land. Read across it slowly — every
relationship in this section is visible in the numbers.

| Workers (L) | Total product TP | Marginal product MP | Average product AP |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10 | 10 | 10.00 |
| 2 | 24 | 14 | 12.00 |
| 3 | 39 | 15 | 13.00 |
| 4 | 52 | 13 | 13.00 |
| 5 | 60 | 8 | 12.00 |
| 6 | 66 | 6 | 11.00 |
| 7 | 70 | 4 | 10.00 |
| 8 | 70 | 0 | 8.75 |

Three relationships between MP and AP are worth stating precisely, because the exam turns on them.

- **When MP is above AP, AP rises; when MP is below AP, AP falls; when MP equals AP, AP is at its
  maximum.** In the table AP peaks at 13 (the 4th worker), and it is exactly there that MP has fallen
  to meet AP at 13. The marginal always pulls the average toward itself.
- **MP reaches its maximum before AP does.** MP peaks at the 3rd worker (15); AP peaks at the 4th.
- **When MP is zero, TP is at its maximum**; if MP turns negative, TP actually falls. Here TP tops out
  at 70 when MP hits 0 (the 8th worker adds nothing).

**The three stages of production.** The behaviour of the three curves divides the short run into three
stages.

| Stage | What is happening | The signal |
|---|---|---|
| **Stage I — increasing returns** | TP rises at an increasing rate, then a decreasing rate; AP rises throughout and reaches its maximum at the end | AP rising to its peak; MP has already peaked and begun to fall |
| **Stage II — diminishing returns** | TP rises but at a decreasing rate; both AP and MP fall, but MP stays positive | from AP maximum up to where MP = 0 |
| **Stage III — negative returns** | TP falls; MP is negative | MP below zero |

A rational firm always operates in **Stage II**. Stage I is wasteful because the fixed factor is
underused (more of the variable factor would still raise average product), and Stage III is
irrational because adding the variable factor actually cuts total output. Stage II — from the AP
maximum to the point where MP reaches zero — is where the firm settles; the exact point within it
depends on factor prices.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The single most tested fact here is the meeting point: <strong>AP is at its maximum where MP
    equals AP</strong>, and a rational producer operates in <strong>Stage II</strong>. If a schedule
    asks "at which unit is average product maximum?", find the unit where MP has fallen to equal AP —
    that is the boundary between Stage I and Stage II.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Diminishing returns under this law is a <strong>short-run</strong> idea — it comes from adding a
    variable factor to a <strong>fixed</strong> factor, so the proportions between them change. Do not
    confuse it with decreasing <em>returns to scale</em> (Section 4), where <strong>all</strong>
    factors change together and no factor is fixed. Same word "returns", entirely different setting.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Returns to scale

**Returns to scale** is a **long-run** idea. It asks what happens to output when **all** factors are
increased together in the **same proportion** — the whole scale of production expands, and no factor
is held fixed. There are three possibilities.

- **Increasing returns to scale.** Output rises **more than in proportion** to the inputs — double all
  inputs and output more than doubles. This is common at low scales, driven by the internal economies
  of Section 7 (specialisation, indivisible equipment used more fully).
- **Constant returns to scale.** Output rises **exactly in proportion** — double the inputs and output
  exactly doubles.
- **Decreasing returns to scale.** Output rises **less than in proportion** — double the inputs and
  output less than doubles. This sets in at large scales, driven by the internal diseconomies of
  management and coordination.

<div class="formula">Increase all inputs by a factor t: output up by more than t → increasing; exactly t → constant; less than t → decreasing returns to scale</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Returns to scale (long run, all factors vary) and the law of variable proportions (short run, one
    factor fixed) answer different questions. If the stem says "all factors are increased in the same
    proportion", it is testing returns to <strong>scale</strong>; if it says "more of one factor is
    added to a fixed factor", it is the law of <strong>variable proportions</strong>. Read the setup
    before choosing the vocabulary.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Isoquants and the iso-cost line

The long-run producer chooses a **combination** of two factors, and two tools describe that choice.

An **isoquant** (equal-quantity curve, also called an iso-product curve) shows all the combinations of
two factors — labour and capital — that produce the **same** level of output. It is the producer's
counterpart of the consumer's indifference curve. Its properties mirror those of the indifference
curve.

- **Isoquants slope downward** from left to right. To keep output constant, using less of one factor
  must be made up by more of the other.
- **They are convex to the origin**, because the **marginal rate of technical substitution (MRTS)** —
  the rate at which labour can replace capital while holding output constant — **diminishes** as more
  labour is used. Each extra worker substitutes for less and less capital.
- **A higher isoquant means more output.** A curve further from the origin uses more of both factors
  and yields a larger output.
- **Isoquants never intersect.** Two crossing isoquants would mean one input combination produced two
  different outputs, a contradiction.

<div class="formula">MRTS of labour for capital = ΔK ÷ ΔL = the capital given up per extra unit of labour, output held constant</div>

The **iso-cost line** (equal-cost line) shows all the combinations of the two factors the firm can
buy for a **given total outlay**, at the given factor prices. It is the producer's budget line. Its
**slope is the ratio of the factor prices** — the wage rate divided by the price (rental) of capital.

<div class="formula">Slope of the iso-cost line = wage rate of labour ÷ price of capital = w ÷ r</div>

A larger budget shifts the iso-cost line outward parallel to itself; a change in one factor price
swings the line about its intercept on the other axis.

## 6. Producer equilibrium and the expansion path

The producer is in equilibrium — producing a given output at **least cost**, or the maximum output
for a given outlay — where the **iso-cost line is tangent to the highest attainable isoquant**. At
that tangency the slope of the isoquant equals the slope of the iso-cost line, so:

<div class="formula">At the least-cost combination: MRTS of labour for capital = w ÷ r (ratio of factor prices)</div>

Read in the more useful form, this is the condition that the **marginal product per rupee is equal
across factors** — the last rupee spent on labour adds as much output as the last rupee spent on
capital:

<div class="formula">MP of labour ÷ price of labour = MP of capital ÷ price of capital</div>

If a rupee spent on labour yielded more output than a rupee spent on capital, the firm would shift
spending toward labour until the two ratios came back into balance. This is the exact producer
analogue of the consumer's equi-marginal rule.

The **expansion path** joins the successive least-cost (tangency) points as the firm raises its
output level with factor prices unchanged. It shows how the cheapest factor combination changes as the
scale of production grows, and it is the route the firm follows when it expands.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The producer-equilibrium condition is asked in two equivalent forms: <strong>MRTS = ratio of
    factor prices (w ÷ r)</strong>, and <strong>MP of labour ÷ wage = MP of capital ÷ price of
    capital</strong>. Both say the same thing — the tangency of the iso-cost line and the isoquant.
    Learn the tangency picture and either algebraic form falls out of it.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. Economies and diseconomies of scale

As a firm grows, its **cost per unit** first falls and later rises. The forces that pull it down are
**economies of scale**; the forces that push it back up at large size are **diseconomies of scale**.
Each comes in two kinds — **internal** (arising within the firm from its own growth) and **external**
(arising outside the firm from the growth of the whole industry).

**Internal economies** accrue to a single firm as it expands its own scale.

- **Technical economies** — larger, more efficient, indivisible machines become worth installing; the
  larger plant uses them more fully.
- **Managerial economies** — division of managerial work and specialisation of function as the firm
  grows.
- **Marketing (commercial) economies** — bulk buying of raw materials at a discount and bulk selling
  at lower cost per unit.
- **Financial economies** — a large firm borrows more easily and on better terms.
- **Risk-bearing economies** — a large firm spreads its risks across more products and markets.

**External economies** accrue to **all** firms in an industry as the industry grows — the benefit is
not the firm's own doing. They include a pool of skilled labour in the locality, specialised
ancillary and repair firms springing up, shared infrastructure, and shared information and research.

**Internal diseconomies** set in when a firm grows **too** large — chiefly **managerial**: control
becomes unwieldy, decisions slow, coordination and supervision falter, and cost per unit rises.
**External diseconomies** arise when an industry grows too large in one place — competition for scarce
inputs bids up the price of local labour, land and raw materials for every firm.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Internal and external must not be swapped. <strong>Internal</strong> economies come from the
    <strong>firm's own</strong> expansion and are enjoyed by that firm alone; <strong>external</strong>
    economies come from the <strong>whole industry's</strong> growth and are shared by every firm in
    it. Bulk buying a discount is internal; a pool of trained local labour is external.
  </p>
</Callout>

# Unit 2 — Theory of Cost

## 8. Cost concepts

Before drawing a single cost curve, the exam wants the cost **concepts** kept straight, because a
whole family of questions turns on the definitions.

- **Accounting cost versus economic cost.** **Accounting cost** counts only the **explicit**, paid-out
  money expenses recorded in the books — wages, rent, raw materials. **Economic cost** adds the
  **implicit** costs as well, so economic cost = explicit cost + implicit cost.
- **Explicit versus implicit cost.** **Explicit costs** are actual cash payments to outsiders for
  hired factors. **Implicit costs** are the costs of the firm's **own** factors, for which no payment
  is made — the salary the owner-entrepreneur could have earned elsewhere, the rent the firm's own
  building could have fetched, the interest the owner's capital could have earned.
- **Opportunity cost.** The value of the **next-best alternative forgone** when a resource is put to a
  given use. It is the master idea behind implicit cost and behind economic cost generally — the true
  cost of any choice is what had to be given up.
- **Sunk cost.** A cost **already incurred that cannot be recovered** whatever the firm now does — a
  specialised machine with no resale value, past spending on research. Because a sunk cost cannot be
  changed by any current decision, it is **irrelevant** to that decision.

<div class="formula">Economic cost = explicit (accounting) cost + implicit cost · Economic profit = revenue − economic cost</div>

Because economic cost includes the implicit costs that accounting cost ignores, **economic profit is
smaller than accounting profit**. A firm earning a positive accounting profit may be making zero or
negative economic profit once the owner's forgone salary and forgone interest are counted.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Three definition traps recur. <strong>Opportunity cost</strong> = the next-best alternative
    forgone. <strong>Implicit cost</strong> = the cost of the firm's own (unpaid) factors, so it is
    <em>excluded</em> from accounting cost but included in economic cost. <strong>Sunk cost</strong> =
    already spent and unrecoverable, so it is irrelevant to any present decision. If a question asks
    which cost should be ignored when deciding, the answer is the <strong>sunk</strong> cost.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Short-run costs

In the short run some factors are fixed and some are variable, so total cost splits into a fixed part
and a variable part. Six short-run cost measures matter, and the exam computes all of them.

- **Total fixed cost (TFC)** — the cost of the fixed factors; it does **not** change with output, and
  is positive even at zero output (rent, insurance, the salary of permanent staff).
- **Total variable cost (TVC)** — the cost of the variable factors; it is **zero at zero output** and
  rises as output rises (raw materials, power, wages of casual labour).
- **Total cost (TC)** — the sum of the two: TC = TFC + TVC.
- **Average fixed cost (AFC)** — fixed cost per unit; it **falls continuously** as output rises,
  because a fixed sum is spread over more units. Its curve is a rectangular hyperbola that approaches
  but never touches the axes.
- **Average variable cost (AVC)** — variable cost per unit; it is **U-shaped**, falling then rising.
- **Average total cost (ATC or AC)** — total cost per unit; it too is **U-shaped**, and equals AFC +
  AVC at every output.
- **Marginal cost (MC)** — the addition to total cost from producing **one more** unit; also U-shaped.

<div class="formula">AFC = TFC ÷ Q · AVC = TVC ÷ Q · ATC = TC ÷ Q = AFC + AVC</div>
<div class="formula">MC = ΔTC ÷ ΔQ = change in total cost from one more unit (= ΔTVC, since fixed cost does not change)</div>

The table below builds every measure from a total-cost schedule with a fixed cost of ₹ 100. Averages
are rounded to two decimals; the marginal-cost column is the change in total cost from the previous
row.

| Output Q | TFC | TVC | TC | AFC | AVC | ATC | MC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100 | 50 | 150 | 100.00 | 50.00 | 150.00 | 50 |
| 2 | 100 | 80 | 180 | 50.00 | 40.00 | 90.00 | 30 |
| 3 | 100 | 100 | 200 | 33.33 | 33.33 | 66.67 | 20 |
| 4 | 100 | 120 | 220 | 25.00 | 30.00 | 55.00 | 20 |
| 5 | 100 | 150 | 250 | 20.00 | 30.00 | 50.00 | 30 |
| 6 | 100 | 200 | 300 | 16.67 | 33.33 | 50.00 | 50 |
| 7 | 100 | 280 | 380 | 14.29 | 40.00 | 54.29 | 80 |

Read the shapes off the columns. **AFC** (100 → 14.29) falls the whole way. **AVC** falls to a minimum
of 30 around the 4th–5th unit, then rises. **ATC** falls to a minimum of 50 at the 5th–6th unit, then
rises. **MC** falls to 20, then climbs steeply. The vertical gap between ATC and AVC at any output is
exactly AFC, and because AFC keeps shrinking, the ATC and AVC curves draw closer together as output
rises without ever meeting.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Fixed cost does <strong>not</strong> enter marginal cost. Because TFC is the same at Q and at
    Q − 1, it cancels in ΔTC, so MC = ΔTVC. Adding fixed cost into a marginal-cost calculation is a
    frequent slip. Likewise TVC — not TC — is zero at zero output; TC at zero output equals TFC.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. The relationship between average cost and marginal cost

The link between marginal cost and the two average curves is the same "marginal pulls the average"
logic met in Section 3, and it produces the chapter's most tested cost result.

- **When MC is below AC, AC is falling.** The extra unit costs less than the running average, so it
  drags the average down.
- **When MC is above AC, AC is rising.** The extra unit costs more than the average, so it pulls the
  average up.
- **MC cuts AC at the minimum point of AC**, where MC = AC. The same holds for AVC: **MC cuts AVC at
  the minimum point of AVC**.

<div class="formula">MC = AC at the minimum of AC · MC = AVC at the minimum of AVC · MC below the average → average falling; MC above → average rising</div>

In the Section 9 table this is exactly what happens. AVC reaches its minimum of 30 around the 4th–5th
unit; MC (30 at the 5th unit) meets AVC there. ATC reaches its minimum of 50 at the 5th–6th unit; MC
(50 at the 6th unit) meets ATC there. Because MC cuts AVC before it cuts ATC, and both at their lows,
the **MC curve passes through the lowest point of both the AVC and the ATC curves, cutting AVC first**.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The result examiners reach for is: <strong>MC cuts AC (and AVC) at its minimum point</strong>. A
    corollary they also test — if MC is <em>less</em> than AC, AC must be <em>falling</em>; if MC is
    <em>greater</em> than AC, AC must be <em>rising</em>. So an option claiming "AC is rising while MC
    is below AC" is self-contradictory and wrong.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 11. Long-run cost and the envelope curve

In the long run there is no fixed factor, so there is no fixed cost and no distinction between fixed
and variable — every cost is variable. The firm can build any size of plant, and each possible plant
has its own short-run average cost (SAC) curve.

The **long-run average cost (LAC) curve** is drawn from all these short-run curves. For each level of
output the firm picks the plant that produces it most cheaply, so the LAC is the **lowest** average
cost attainable at each output. Geometrically the LAC is the **envelope** of the short-run average
cost curves — it touches each SAC curve but lies **on or below** every one of them, never cutting
through them. For this reason the LAC is often called the **envelope curve** or, informally, the
**planning curve**.

The LAC is itself **U-shaped**, but for a different reason from the short-run U. Its downward stretch
is caused by **economies of scale** (Section 7) as the firm builds larger, more efficient plants; its
upward stretch is caused by **diseconomies of scale**, chiefly managerial, once the firm grows too
large. The lowest point of the LAC is the **minimum efficient scale** — the plant size that produces
at the least possible cost per unit.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The LAC is the envelope of the short-run curves — it is <strong>tangent</strong> to each SAC and
    lies on or below them; it is <strong>not</strong> drawn through their minimum points. Only at the
    single plant that is optimal for the whole industry does the LAC touch an SAC at that SAC's own
    minimum; for every other plant the tangency is to the left or right of the SAC minimum. And the
    U-shape of LAC comes from <strong>economies and diseconomies of scale</strong>, not from the law
    of variable proportions, which is a short-run idea.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 12. Revenue: total, average and marginal

Cost is one side of the firm's accounts; **revenue** is the other. Three revenue measures parallel the
three product and cost measures.

- **Total revenue (TR)** — the firm's total receipts from selling its output: price times quantity.
- **Average revenue (AR)** — revenue per unit sold, which is simply the **price** of the good.
- **Marginal revenue (MR)** — the addition to total revenue from selling **one more** unit.

<div class="formula">TR = price × quantity · AR = TR ÷ Q = price · MR = ΔTR ÷ ΔQ</div>

Because average revenue is just the price, the **AR curve is the firm's demand curve**. The
relationship between AR and MR depends on the market the firm sells in.

- **Under perfect competition** the firm is a price-taker: it sells any quantity at the one going
  price, so the price does not fall as it sells more. Then **AR = MR = price**, and both curves are
  the same horizontal line. Total revenue rises in a straight line from the origin.
- **Under imperfect competition** (monopoly, monopolistic competition, oligopoly) the firm faces a
  **downward-sloping** demand curve: to sell more it must lower the price on all units. Then the price
  (AR) falls as output rises, and **MR falls faster, so MR is below AR** (MR &lt; AR) at every output
  beyond the first.

The two revenue schedules below make the contrast concrete. On the left, price is fixed at ₹ 20
(perfect competition); on the right, price falls as more is sold (imperfect competition).

| Q | Price (AR) — perfect | TR — perfect | MR — perfect | Price (AR) — imperfect | TR — imperfect | MR — imperfect |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20 | 20 | 20 | 20 | 20 | 20 |
| 2 | 20 | 40 | 20 | 18 | 36 | 16 |
| 3 | 20 | 60 | 20 | 16 | 48 | 12 |
| 4 | 20 | 80 | 20 | 14 | 56 | 8 |
| 5 | 20 | 100 | 20 | 12 | 60 | 4 |

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The examiner's one-liner is: under <strong>perfect competition AR = MR</strong> (a horizontal
    line, the price); under <strong>imperfect competition AR &gt; MR</strong> and both slope down.
    Remember also that <strong>AR is the price</strong> and the <strong>AR curve is the demand
    curve</strong>. If asked why MR falls faster than AR, it is because to sell the extra unit the
    firm must cut the price on the earlier units too.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 13. Negative marking and when to leave a question

Paper 4 is entirely objective and carries **negative marking of 0.25 mark for each wrong answer**. A
correct answer earns 1 mark, a wrong one costs 0.25, and an unattempted one scores 0.

The arithmetic is worth holding calmly. A blind guess among four options is right one time in four:
across four such guesses you expect 1 mark gained and 3 × 0.25 = 0.75 lost, a net of +0.25 for four
questions — very slightly positive but close to neutral. What changes the picture is **elimination**.
Rule out one option and a guess among three is worth about 0.5 marks per four attempts; rule out two
and the last guess is worth about 1.5 marks per four.

In this chapter elimination is unusually easy, because the distractors are built from named errors — a
marginal cost that wrongly includes fixed cost, an average confused with a marginal, internal and
external economies swapped, AR set equal to MR under imperfect competition. A sensible rule: **answer
when you can eliminate at least one option; consider leaving it only when you cannot narrow the field
at all.** Nothing about that rule needs to be followed anxiously — the marks at stake either way are
small, and time spent on a question you can actually finish is worth more than either choice.

## Fully worked examples

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — marginal and average product from a schedule (easy)">
  <p>
    A farm employs workers on a fixed plot. Total product is 24 units with 2 workers, 39 with 3, and
    52 with 4. (a) Find the marginal product of the 3rd worker. (b) Find the average product of 4
    workers.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) marginal product is the change in total product</div>
    MP of the 3rd worker = TP(3) − TP(2) = 39 − 24 = <strong>15 units</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) average product is total product per worker</div>
    AP of 4 workers = TP(4) ÷ 4 = 52 ÷ 4 = <strong>13 units per worker</strong>
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — reading the three stages (moderate)">
  <p>
    Using the schedule of Section 3 (TP = 10, 24, 39, 52, 60, 66, 70, 70 for 1 to 8 workers): (a) at
    which worker is average product at its maximum, and what is special about marginal product there?
    (b) At which worker does total product reach its maximum, and what is marginal product at that
    point?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) AP maximum and the MP = AP meeting</div>
    AP = 10, 12, 13, 13, 12, 11, 10, 8.75. The maximum AP is 13, reached at the <strong>4th
    worker</strong>. MP at the 4th worker = 52 − 39 = 13, so <strong>MP equals AP</strong> at the AP
    maximum — the boundary between Stage I and Stage II.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) TP maximum where MP = 0</div>
    TP is highest at 70, first reached at the <strong>7th worker and unchanged at the 8th</strong>.
    MP of the 8th worker = 70 − 70 = <strong>0</strong>. When MP is zero, TP is at its maximum; a 9th
    worker (negative MP) would push the farm into Stage III.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — completing a short-run cost table (moderate)">
  <p>
    A firm's fixed cost is ₹ 100. At an output of 4 units its total variable cost is ₹ 120; at 5 units
    its total variable cost is ₹ 150. For an output of 4 units, find (a) total cost, (b) average fixed
    cost, (c) average variable cost and average total cost, and (d) the marginal cost of the 5th unit.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) total cost and (b) average fixed cost at Q = 4</div>
    TC = TFC + TVC = 100 + 120 = <strong>₹ 220</strong><br />
    AFC = TFC ÷ Q = 100 ÷ 4 = <strong>₹ 25</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (c) average variable and average total cost at Q = 4</div>
    AVC = TVC ÷ Q = 120 ÷ 4 = <strong>₹ 30</strong><br />
    ATC = TC ÷ Q = 220 ÷ 4 = ₹ 55 (check: AFC + AVC = 25 + 30 = <strong>₹ 55</strong> ✓)
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (d) marginal cost of the 5th unit</div>
    TC at 5 units = 100 + 150 = ₹ 250; TC at 4 units = ₹ 220.<br />
    MC of the 5th unit = ΔTC = 250 − 220 = <strong>₹ 30</strong> (equal to ΔTVC = 150 − 120 = 30, since
    fixed cost does not change).
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — where MC cuts ATC (moderate)">
  <p>
    From the Section 9 schedule the total cost is ₹ 250 at 5 units and ₹ 300 at 6 units, with a fixed
    cost of ₹ 100. (a) Find the average total cost at 5 and at 6 units. (b) Find the marginal cost of
    the 6th unit. (c) What relationship does this confirm?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) average total cost at the two outputs</div>
    ATC at 5 units = 250 ÷ 5 = ₹ 50 · ATC at 6 units = 300 ÷ 6 = ₹ 50 — the ATC is at its
    <strong>minimum of ₹ 50</strong> over this range.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) marginal cost of the 6th unit</div>
    MC of the 6th unit = TC(6) − TC(5) = 300 − 250 = <strong>₹ 50</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (c) MC = ATC at the ATC minimum</div>
    MC (₹ 50) equals ATC (₹ 50) exactly where ATC is at its lowest. This confirms the rule: the
    <strong>marginal cost curve cuts the average total cost curve at the ATC minimum</strong>.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — average variable cost and its minimum (moderate)">
  <p>
    A firm's total variable cost is ₹ 120 at 4 units and ₹ 150 at 5 units. (a) Find the average
    variable cost at each of these outputs. (b) The marginal cost of the 5th unit is ₹ 30. What does
    the comparison of MC with AVC tell you about the AVC curve here?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) average variable cost at 4 and 5 units</div>
    AVC at 4 units = 120 ÷ 4 = ₹ 30 · AVC at 5 units = 150 ÷ 5 = <strong>₹ 30</strong> — AVC is at its
    <strong>minimum of ₹ 30</strong> across this range.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) MC meets AVC at the AVC minimum</div>
    The MC of the 5th unit (₹ 30) equals the AVC (₹ 30) exactly at the AVC minimum. To the left MC was
    below AVC (AVC falling); beyond this point MC rises above AVC (AVC rising). So <strong>MC cuts AVC
    at the lowest point of AVC</strong>.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 6 — total, average and marginal revenue under imperfect competition (moderate)">
  <p>
    A firm can sell 2 units at ₹ 18 each and 3 units at ₹ 16 each. (a) Find total revenue at 2 and at
    3 units. (b) Find the marginal revenue of the 3rd unit. (c) Find the average revenue at 3 units,
    and state how AR and MR compare.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) total revenue = price × quantity</div>
    TR at 2 units = 18 × 2 = ₹ 36 · TR at 3 units = 16 × 3 = <strong>₹ 48</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) marginal revenue is the change in total revenue</div>
    MR of the 3rd unit = TR(3) − TR(2) = 48 − 36 = <strong>₹ 12</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (c) average revenue is the price</div>
    AR at 3 units = TR ÷ Q = 48 ÷ 3 = ₹ 16, which is just the price.<br />
    Here AR (₹ 16) is <strong>above</strong> MR (₹ 12): under imperfect competition MR &lt; AR, because
    selling the extra unit needed a price cut on the earlier units too.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 7 — revenue under perfect competition (easy-moderate)">
  <p>
    A price-taking firm sells its output at a fixed market price of ₹ 20 per unit, whatever quantity
    it sells. (a) Find total revenue at 3 and at 4 units. (b) Find the marginal revenue of the 4th
    unit and the average revenue at 4 units. (c) What is the relationship between AR and MR here?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) total revenue at a constant price</div>
    TR at 3 units = 20 × 3 = ₹ 60 · TR at 4 units = 20 × 4 = <strong>₹ 80</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) marginal and average revenue</div>
    MR of the 4th unit = 80 − 60 = <strong>₹ 20</strong> · AR at 4 units = 80 ÷ 4 = <strong>₹ 20</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (c) AR = MR = price</div>
    Both AR and MR equal the price of ₹ 20, and neither changes with output. Under perfect competition
    <strong>AR = MR = price</strong>, a single horizontal line — the firm's demand curve.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 8 — least-cost factor combination (moderate-hard)">
  <p>
    A firm uses labour and capital. The wage rate is ₹ 200 per unit of labour and the price of capital
    is ₹ 100 per unit. (a) At the least-cost combination, what must the marginal rate of technical
    substitution of labour for capital equal? (b) At its current mix the marginal product of labour is
    40 units and the marginal product of capital is 25 units. Is the firm at the least-cost
    combination, and if not, which factor should it use more of?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) the equilibrium condition</div>
    At the least-cost combination MRTS of labour for capital = ratio of factor prices = w ÷ r =
    200 ÷ 100 = <strong>2</strong>. Each extra unit of labour should replace 2 units of capital.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) compare marginal product per rupee</div>
    MP of labour ÷ wage = 40 ÷ 200 = 0.20 units per rupee.<br />
    MP of capital ÷ price of capital = 25 ÷ 100 = 0.25 units per rupee.<br />
    The two are <strong>not equal</strong>, so the firm is not at least cost. A rupee spent on capital
    buys more output (0.25) than a rupee spent on labour (0.20), so the firm should use <strong>more
    capital and less labour</strong> until the two ratios come into balance.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes and examiner traps

- **Average confused with marginal.** Average product/cost/revenue is a total divided by quantity;
  marginal is the change from one more unit. AP = TP ÷ L but MP = ΔTP; ATC = TC ÷ Q but MC = ΔTC.
  Mixing the two is the most common computational error.
- **Fixed cost slipping into marginal cost.** MC = ΔTC = ΔTVC, because fixed cost is unchanged from
  one unit to the next and cancels. Never add TFC into an MC calculation.
- **TFC versus TVC at zero output.** Total variable cost is zero at zero output; total fixed cost is
  positive at zero output. TC at zero output equals TFC, not zero.
- **Short-run law of variable proportions versus long-run returns to scale.** Diminishing returns to a
  factor is short run (one factor fixed); decreasing returns to scale is long run (all factors vary).
  Read whether one factor is fixed or all vary.
- **AP maximum and the MP = AP point.** Average product is at its maximum where marginal product
  equals it, not where marginal product is greatest. MP peaks one unit earlier.
- **MC and the average curves.** MC cuts AVC and ATC at their minimum points. If MC is below the
  average the average is falling; if MC is above, the average is rising. An "AC rising while MC below
  AC" option is contradictory.
- **AR and MR by market type.** Under perfect competition AR = MR = price (horizontal); under
  imperfect competition AR &gt; MR and both slope down. AR is the price, and the AR curve is the
  demand curve.
- **Internal versus external economies.** Internal economies come from the firm's own growth; external
  economies come from the whole industry's growth. Managerial diseconomies are the classic internal
  diseconomy.
- **Accounting versus economic cost and profit.** Economic cost = explicit + implicit cost, so it
  exceeds accounting cost, and economic profit is smaller than accounting profit. Sunk cost is
  irrelevant to any present decision.
- **The envelope curve.** LAC is tangent to the short-run curves and lies on or below them; it does
  not join their minimum points. Its U-shape comes from economies and diseconomies of scale.

## One-page revision summary

**Factors of production:** land (rent), labour (wages), capital (interest), entrepreneur (profit — the
residual, bears risk). **Production function** Q = f(L, K): the maximum output from given inputs.
**Short run** = at least one factor fixed; **long run** = all factors variable — defined by inputs,
not clock time.

**Law of variable proportions (short run):** add a variable factor to a fixed factor.

<div class="formula">AP = TP ÷ L · MP = ΔTP ÷ ΔL · AP is maximum where MP = AP · TP is maximum where MP = 0</div>

Three stages: **Stage I** AP rising to its peak; **Stage II** AP and MP both falling but MP positive
(the rational stage); **Stage III** MP negative, TP falling.

**Returns to scale (long run, all factors change together):** increasing (output up more than in
proportion), constant (in proportion), decreasing (less than in proportion).

**Isoquant** = combinations giving equal output; downward-sloping, convex (diminishing MRTS), never
intersect, higher = more output. **Iso-cost line** slope = w ÷ r. **Producer equilibrium** at the
tangency: MRTS = w ÷ r, equivalently MP of labour ÷ w = MP of capital ÷ r. The **expansion path**
joins the least-cost points.

**Economies of scale:** internal (technical, managerial, marketing, financial, risk-bearing — from
the firm's own growth) and external (from the industry's growth). Diseconomies: internal (mainly
managerial) and external (competition for scarce inputs).

**Cost concepts:** accounting/explicit (paid out) versus economic = explicit + implicit; opportunity
cost = next-best forgone; sunk cost = unrecoverable, irrelevant to decisions. Economic profit &lt;
accounting profit.

<div class="formula">TC = TFC + TVC · AFC = TFC ÷ Q · AVC = TVC ÷ Q · ATC = TC ÷ Q = AFC + AVC · MC = ΔTC ÷ ΔQ</div>

**Short-run shapes:** AFC falls throughout; AVC, ATC and MC are U-shaped; the ATC–AVC gap is AFC and
shrinks. **MC cuts AVC and ATC at their minimum points;** MC below the average → average falling; MC
above → average rising.

**Long-run cost:** LAC is the **envelope** of the short-run average cost curves — lowest cost at each
output, tangent to each SAC. Its U-shape comes from **economies then diseconomies of scale**; the
lowest point is the minimum efficient scale.

<div class="formula">TR = price × Q · AR = TR ÷ Q = price · MR = ΔTR ÷ ΔQ</div>

**Revenue:** AR is the price and the AR curve is the demand curve. **Perfect competition:** AR = MR =
price (horizontal). **Imperfect competition:** AR &gt; MR, both slope down (MR falls faster).

**Marking:** 1 mark correct, −0.25 wrong, 0 unattempted. Guessing among four is close to neutral;
eliminating even one option makes an attempt clearly worthwhile.
