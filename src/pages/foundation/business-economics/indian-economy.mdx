Three people leave their homes at dawn in three different corners of India. In a village in Bihar a
farmer walks to his field to transplant paddy. In a plant on the outskirts of Chennai a machine
operator clocks in to press car-body panels. In a glass tower in Bengaluru a graduate puts on a
headset to answer calls from customers a continent away. None of them has met the others, and yet the
three together are a working portrait of the whole Indian economy: the farmer belongs to the
**primary** sector, the machine operator to the **secondary**, and the call-centre worker to the
**tertiary**. Trace how the weight of the economy has shifted across those three over the decades, and
you have traced the single most important story this chapter tells.

Paper 4 is entirely objective, and this chapter is almost wholly conceptual. It carries no arithmetic
worth the name; the marks are won by **recognising** things — the features that make India a
developing mixed economy, which activity belongs to which sector, what each letter of the "LPG"
reforms stood for, which institution replaced which and in what year, and which type of unemployment
a described case illustrates. The traps are built from confusions: a sector misclassified,
privatization mistaken for globalization, disguised unemployment read as open unemployment, the
Planning Commission confused with NITI Aayog. Read every stem for the exact word it turns on, and the
distractors fall away.

## 1. The Indian economy: a developing, mixed economy

Economies are often sorted, roughly, into **developed** and **developing** (the older word was
"underdeveloped", and "emerging" is the newer, more hopeful term for the faster-growing ones). A
**developed economy** has a high average income, a large stock of capital, advanced technology, most
of its output and workforce in industry and services, and high standards of health and education. A
**developing economy** is one still in the process of getting there: average income is lower, a large
share of people still depend on agriculture, capital and infrastructure are relatively scarce, and
human-development indicators lag. India is described as a **developing** — or **emerging** — economy
on exactly these grounds.

India is also a **mixed economy**. As Chapter 1 set out, a mixed economy is one in which the
**private and public sectors coexist**: markets and private enterprise run much of the economy for
profit, while the government owns key industries, regulates private activity, provides public goods,
and steps in to reduce inequality and correct market failures. India chose this middle path
deliberately after independence, keeping private enterprise but giving the state a large, planned role
— the arrangement the rest of this chapter describes.

Put the two labels together and the standard one-line description of India follows: a **developing
(emerging) mixed economy** — still building up income and capital, and doing so through a blend of
market forces and state direction rather than through pure capitalism or pure central planning.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Two facts anchor a whole family of easy questions: India is a <strong>developing / emerging</strong>
    economy (not a developed one), and it is a <strong>mixed</strong> economy (private and public
    sectors together, not pure capitalism and not pure socialism). An option that calls India a
    "developed economy" or a "purely socialist economy" is wrong on the face of it.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. Features of the Indian economy

A handful of structural features recur whenever the Indian economy is described. They are timeless
features — traits of the system, not this year's numbers — and the paper tests them as recognition
points.

- **A mixed economic system.** Private and public ownership sit side by side, with the government
  playing a planning and regulating role alongside private enterprise. This is the defining
  institutional feature.
- **A large but shrinking role for agriculture.** Agriculture and allied activities still support a
  large share of the **workforce** and of rural livelihoods, even though their share of total
  **output** has fallen steadily over time. This gap — a big share of workers producing a smaller
  share of output — points to relatively low productivity in the sector and is itself a feature.
- **A distinctive demographic profile.** India has a very large population and, unlike many developed
  countries, a **young** age structure — a large share of people of working age. A young, growing
  workforce is called a **demographic dividend** when it can be productively employed, and a burden
  when it cannot. Rapid population growth and rising urbanisation are part of the same profile.
- **Economic dualism.** The economy runs on two tracks at once. A **modern** sector — organised
  industry, formal services, up-to-date technology — coexists with a **traditional** sector —
  subsistence farming, handicrafts, small unorganised units using old methods. This coexistence of a
  modern and a traditional economy side by side is called **dualism**, and it is a classic mark of a
  developing economy.
- **A large and growing services sector.** Services — trade, transport, finance, communication,
  software, tourism — have grown to become the **largest** contributor to national output, and India
  is often noted for the unusual weight of its services sector for a country at its income level.
- **A large informal (unorganised) sector.** A very large share of workers are employed outside the
  organised, regulated sector — in small farms, tiny enterprises and casual work — without formal
  contracts or social security. The size of this informal sector is a persistent structural feature.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    "Agriculture is the largest part of the Indian economy" is a half-truth that the examiner exploits.
    Agriculture still employs a very large <strong>share of the workforce</strong>, but in terms of
    <strong>share of output</strong> the <strong>services</strong> sector is the largest. Keep the two
    measures apart: workforce share and output share tell different stories, and the gap between them is
    itself a feature of the economy.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. The three sectors of the economy

All the productive activity of an economy is conventionally grouped into three broad sectors,
according to the kind of work done. Every job and every rupee of output belongs to one of them.

- **The primary sector** covers activities that draw directly on **nature** — agriculture, animal
  husbandry, forestry, fishing, and mining and quarrying. These are the "primary" activities because
  they produce the raw materials on which everything else is built. In India the primary sector is
  dominated by **agriculture and allied** activities.
- **The secondary sector** covers **industry** — activities that take the raw materials of the primary
  sector and **transform** them into manufactured goods. Manufacturing, construction, electricity, gas
  and water supply belong here. Because it makes finished goods out of raw materials, the secondary
  sector is also called the **manufacturing** or **industrial** sector.
- **The tertiary sector** covers **services** — activities that do not produce goods but provide
  **services** that support production and consumption. Trade, transport, storage, communication,
  banking and finance, insurance, education, health, public administration, software and tourism all
  belong here. The tertiary sector is also called the **services** sector.

| Sector | Also called | What it does | Everyday Indian examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary | agriculture and allied | draws goods directly from nature | farming, dairy, fishing, forestry, mining |
| Secondary | industry / manufacturing | transforms raw materials into goods | a car plant, a textile mill, construction, a power station |
| Tertiary | services | provides services, not goods | banking, transport, a call centre, retail shops, teaching |

A fourth grouping is sometimes carved out of the tertiary sector — a **quaternary** sector of
knowledge-based services (information technology, research, consultancy) — but at Foundation level the
three-sector classification is the one that matters. Note that the same commodity can pass through all
three: cotton grown on a farm (primary) is woven into cloth in a mill (secondary) and sold to you in a
shop with the help of a bank loan and a delivery van (tertiary).

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The reliable test for classifying an activity is to ask what it does to the good.
    <strong>Takes it from nature</strong> → primary. <strong>Transforms it into a manufactured
    good</strong> → secondary. <strong>Provides a service around it</strong> → tertiary. Mining is the
    one students misplace: it takes minerals directly from the earth, so it is <strong>primary</strong>,
    not secondary, even though it feels "industrial".
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. The structural shift across the sectors

As an economy develops, the **relative weight** of the three sectors changes in a regular pattern, and
tracing that pattern is the heart of this chapter. The pattern is usually described as a two-stage
shift.

In a poor, early-stage economy the **primary** sector dominates: most people farm, and agriculture
produces the largest share of output. As the economy industrialises, resources and workers move into
the **secondary** sector, and industry's share of output rises. As incomes rise further and the
economy matures, demand swings toward services, and the **tertiary** sector grows to become the
largest of the three. The centre of gravity moves, over time, **primary → secondary → tertiary**.

India's own experience is a variation on this theme, and the paper describes it as a **trend**, not
with pinned figures:

- The **share of the primary sector in output** has fallen steadily over the decades since
  independence, even though it still employs a large part of the workforce.
- The **secondary sector** has grown, but industry's share of output has not risen as sharply as the
  textbook pattern would predict.
- The **tertiary (services) sector** has grown the fastest and has become the **largest** contributor
  to national output — India is often noted for having a services-led structure at a relatively early
  stage of development, in a sense skipping some of the industrial phase.

The key structural fact to carry away is the **direction** of the shift: **away from the primary
sector and toward the tertiary sector**, with services now the largest part of output. Because the
workforce has moved more slowly than output, a disproportionate share of workers is still left in
low-productivity agriculture — which is why raising farm productivity and moving surplus farm labour
into industry and services is a recurring policy theme.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Do not read the structural shift as "agriculture has disappeared". Its <strong>share of output</strong>
    has fallen, but agriculture still supports a very large share of the <strong>population and the
    workforce</strong> and remains vital for food and raw materials. The shift is about relative weight
    over time, not about one sector vanishing. And the shift runs <strong>primary → secondary →
    tertiary</strong>, not the reverse.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Economic planning in India

After independence India chose to guide its development through **economic planning** rather than
leave it wholly to the market. Planning means the **conscious, coordinated direction of the economy by
a central authority** toward chosen goals over a fixed period, using targets and the allocation of
public resources.

The **objectives of planning** in India are conventionally stated as four, and they are a favourite
list to examine:

- **Growth.** Raising the national output and the average standard of living — increasing the size of
  the economic pie.
- **Equity (social justice).** Reducing poverty and the inequalities of income and wealth, so that
  growth reaches the weaker sections — dividing the pie more fairly.
- **Self-reliance.** Reducing dependence on foreign aid and imports by building the country's own
  productive capacity, especially in key industries.
- **Modernization.** Upgrading technology, diversifying the structure of production, and changing
  outdated social and institutional arrangements that hold back growth.

The chief instrument of planning was the **Five-Year Plan**: a document setting out, for a five-year
block, the economy's goals, the sectors to be developed, and the public investment to be made. The
Five-Year Plans ran, one after another (with a few gaps and "plan holidays"), for decades and shaped
the direction of public investment across agriculture, heavy industry, infrastructure and social
services. Early plans leaned heavily toward **self-reliance** and the building of **heavy industry** in
the public sector; later plans gave more weight to poverty reduction, agriculture and, eventually, to
opening the economy.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The four objectives of Indian planning — <strong>growth, equity, self-reliance, modernization</strong>
    — are the reliable list. If a stem asks which of the following was NOT a stated objective, the odd
    one out is usually something like "maximising private profit" or "complete free trade", neither of
    which belongs. Growth and equity are the two most frequently named; equity is also phrased as
    "social justice" or "reducing inequality".
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. From the Planning Commission to NITI Aayog

Planning needed a body to prepare the plans, and India created one soon after independence: the
**Planning Commission**. It was the central agency that formulated the Five-Year Plans, set targets,
and allocated resources to the states and to sectors. Crucially, the Planning Commission worked in a
**top-down** way — it decided plans centrally and passed them down — and it was not a body created by
the Constitution or by an Act of Parliament, but by a government resolution.

In **2015** the Planning Commission was **replaced** by a new body, the **NITI Aayog** — the acronym
stands for the **National Institution for Transforming India**. The change was institutional and is a
plain historical fact worth memorising precisely. NITI Aayog is designed as a **policy think-tank and
advisory body** rather than a resource-allocating planning authority. Its emphasis is on:

- **Cooperative federalism** — working *with* the states as partners rather than handing plans down to
  them, a shift from the old top-down style to a more **bottom-up** approach.
- **Policy advice and long-term strategy** — acting as a think-tank that advises the government and
  designs strategic and long-term programmes, rather than fixing five-year targets and dividing up
  funds.

The end of the Planning Commission also brought the era of the numbered **Five-Year Plans** to a
close. The key facts for the exam are the pairing and the year: the **Planning Commission (old,
resource-allocating, top-down) → NITI Aayog (new, think-tank, cooperative federalism), in 2015**.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    NITI Aayog did not simply rename the Planning Commission with the same powers. The Planning
    Commission <strong>allocated resources</strong> and prepared the Five-Year Plans in a
    <strong>top-down</strong> way; NITI Aayog is a <strong>think-tank and advisory body</strong> built
    around <strong>cooperative federalism</strong> and does not hand down five-year targets. Confusing
    the two bodies, or misdating the change (it was <strong>2015</strong>), is the classic trap here.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. The 1991 reforms: the crisis and the LPG response

For its first four decades independent India ran a heavily controlled, inward-looking economy: private
industry needed government **licences** to set up or expand (the so-called "Licence Raj"), imports were
tightly restricted, and the public sector was reserved a commanding role. By 1991 this model, together
with heavy borrowing and external shocks, had brought the country to a severe **balance-of-payments
crisis** — foreign-exchange reserves fell so low that India could barely pay for a few weeks of
imports and was close to defaulting on its external obligations.

The response was a decisive change of direction: the **1991 economic reforms**, universally summarised
by the initials **LPG** — **Liberalization, Privatization and Globalization**. Each strand loosened a
different set of controls.

| Strand | What it means | The kind of measure it involved |
|---|---|---|
| **Liberalization** | freeing domestic business from state controls | abolishing most industrial **licensing** (delicensing), removing controls on production, prices and investment, and reducing the number of industries reserved for the public sector |
| **Privatization** | reducing the state's ownership role and widening the private sector's | **disinvestment** — selling part of the government's stake in public-sector undertakings — and opening to private firms areas once reserved for the state |
| **Globalization** | integrating the Indian economy with the world economy | reducing import tariffs and quantitative restrictions on **trade**, making the currency convertible on the trade account, and opening the door to **foreign investment** (both direct and portfolio) |

The three strands work together. Liberalization freed firms **inside** the country to invest and
produce as they judged best; privatization shifted the **ownership** balance from the state toward
private enterprise; and globalization opened the economy **outward** to foreign goods, capital and
competition. Together they marked the shift from a controlled, self-reliant model toward a more
market-oriented, open economy — while keeping India a mixed economy rather than a purely capitalist
one.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Fix one keyword to each letter and the reform questions answer themselves.
    <strong>Liberalization = removing internal controls / delicensing</strong> (freeing domestic
    business). <strong>Privatization = disinvestment / more private ownership</strong> (shrinking the
    state's stake). <strong>Globalization = opening to trade and foreign investment</strong> (linking to
    the world). A measure that "removes industrial licensing" is L; one that "sells a government stake"
    is P; one that "cuts import tariffs" is G.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. Poverty and its measurement

The reforms lifted growth, but the deep development challenges remained, and the paper expects you to
recognise them. The first is **poverty**.

**Poverty** is the condition of being unable to afford the basic minimum needs of life — food,
clothing, shelter, and other essentials. It is usually measured against a **poverty line**: a
threshold level of consumption or income below which a person is counted as poor. The commonest method
defines the line by the cost of a **minimum basket** of goods — often built around a minimum level of
**nutrition (calories)** plus an allowance for basic non-food needs — and then counts the share of the
population whose consumption falls below it.

Two ideas of poverty are worth holding apart:

- **Absolute poverty** counts people against a fixed minimum standard — the poverty line — regardless
  of how others live. It measures deprivation in an absolute sense.
- **Relative poverty** compares people with others in the same society — for example, those in the
  bottom slice of the income distribution — so it measures inequality more than bare deprivation. By
  this measure some poverty exists even in rich countries.

The simplest summary measure is the **head-count ratio**: the **proportion of the population below the
poverty line**. It is easy to understand but crude, because it counts how many are poor without saying
how far below the line they fall. Poverty is described here as a concept and a method of measurement;
the chapter deliberately avoids quoting a current poverty figure, because that number changes and is
not what the paper tests.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The poverty line is not the same as the average income, and the head-count ratio is not a rupee
    amount — it is a <strong>proportion of people</strong> below the line. Note too that
    <strong>absolute</strong> poverty (against a fixed minimum) and <strong>relative</strong> poverty
    (against how others live) are different ideas; a country can reduce absolute poverty while relative
    poverty persists, because relative poverty is really a statement about inequality.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Unemployment and its types

The second great challenge is **unemployment**. A person is **unemployed** in the economic sense when
they are **willing and able to work at the going wage but cannot find work**. Someone who chooses not
to work, or who cannot work, is not counted as unemployed. What the paper tests is the **classifying**
of unemployment into its recognised types from a described situation.

| Type | Meaning | A typical case |
|---|---|---|
| **Structural** | joblessness caused by a mismatch between workers' skills or location and the jobs available, as the structure of the economy changes | a mill closes as the industry declines and its weavers' skills are no longer in demand |
| **Cyclical** | joblessness that rises in a downturn (a recession) when total demand falls, and falls again in a recovery | workers laid off across many firms during a general slump in the business cycle |
| **Frictional** | short-term joblessness while a person moves between jobs or searches for a first job | a graduate between quitting one job and starting the next, spending a few weeks job-hunting |
| **Disguised** | where more people work on a task than are really needed, so some contribute little or nothing to output and could be removed without reducing output | five family members working a small farm that three could manage equally well |
| **Seasonal** | joblessness during the off-season of an activity that only needs labour at certain times of year | a farm labourer with no work between the sowing and the harvest seasons |

Two of these deserve extra care, because they are the ones examiners love. **Disguised unemployment**
is the subtle one: the people are visibly "working", but their **marginal productivity is nil or near
nil** — removing them would not cut output — so they are unemployed in disguise. It is common in Indian
**agriculture**, where family farms absorb more hands than the land needs. **Seasonal unemployment**,
by contrast, is about the calendar: work exists only in certain seasons, and workers are idle in
between. A further useful split is **open** unemployment (a person has no work at all and is visibly
without a job) versus disguised unemployment (a person appears employed but adds nothing).

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Match the cue word in the stem to the type. "Skills no longer needed / mismatch" → <strong>structural</strong>.
    "Recession / downturn / falling demand" → <strong>cyclical</strong>. "Between jobs / searching" →
    <strong>frictional</strong>. "More workers than the job needs / could be removed without cutting
    output" → <strong>disguised</strong>. "Off-season / only part of the year" → <strong>seasonal</strong>.
    Disguised unemployment is the one tied to <strong>zero marginal productivity</strong> and to Indian
    agriculture.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. Inflation, inequality, infrastructure and other challenges

Beyond poverty and unemployment, a cluster of further challenges is regularly listed. You should be
able to recognise each by its definition.

- **Inflation.** A **sustained rise in the general level of prices**, which erodes the purchasing power
  of money — the same rupee buys less over time. Some inflation accompanies a growing economy, but high
  or volatile inflation hurts the poor most, because they hold little that keeps pace with prices.
- **Income inequality.** Income and wealth are shared unevenly, both between persons and between
  regions and between the rural and urban parts of the country. High inequality is a challenge because
  growth that flows mainly to those already well off does little to reduce poverty — which is why
  **equity** is a stated objective of planning. Inequality is often pictured by the **Lorenz curve** and
  summarised by the **Gini coefficient** (0 = perfect equality, 1 = perfect inequality).
- **Infrastructure.** The basic physical and institutional facilities on which the rest of the economy
  rests — power, roads, railways, ports, water, telecommunications, and financial and educational
  services. Inadequate infrastructure raises the cost of doing everything and holds back growth in all
  three sectors, so building infrastructure is a central development task.
- **The informal (unorganised) sector.** A very large share of India's workers are in the unorganised
  sector — small unregistered units, self-employment and casual labour — without written contracts, job
  security or social protection. Its size means that much of the workforce lies outside formal
  regulation and benefits, a structural challenge in its own right.
- **Human development.** Development is not only about income. **Human development** looks at whether
  people can lead long, healthy and educated lives, and is commonly summarised by the **Human
  Development Index (HDI)**, which combines three dimensions — a **long and healthy life** (life
  expectancy), **knowledge** (education) and **a decent standard of living** (income). Weak health and
  education outcomes hold back both welfare and future productivity.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Inflation is a <strong>sustained rise in the general price level</strong>, not a one-off jump in a
    single good's price, and not a rise in the quantity of goods. And human development is broader than
    income: the <strong>HDI combines health, education and income</strong>, so a country can have a
    middling income yet a lower HDI if its health and education outcomes are weak. Reading HDI as "income
    per head only" is the trap.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 11. The role of the three sectors in growth

Each sector contributes to growth in its own way, and the paper sometimes asks about the **role** of a
sector rather than its definition.

- **Agriculture (primary).** Even as its share of output falls, agriculture remains vital. It **feeds
  the population**, supplies **raw materials** to industry (cotton, sugarcane, oilseeds), provides a
  **market** for industrial goods (fertiliser, machinery, consumer goods bought by rural households),
  and still **employs** a very large share of the workforce. Raising farm **productivity** — so that
  fewer workers can feed the country and surplus labour can move to industry and services — is a
  standing goal.
- **Industry (secondary).** Industry is the classic **engine of modernisation**. It raises
  productivity, creates better-paid jobs, produces the capital goods and manufactures the economy
  needs, absorbs surplus labour from agriculture, and reduces dependence on imports. A strong
  manufacturing base is usually seen as necessary for sustained, broad-based growth and for
  productive employment.
- **Services (tertiary).** Services have become the **largest** contributor to India's output and its
  fastest-growing sector, led by areas such as information technology, finance, trade and
  communication. Services support the other two sectors (a factory needs banking, transport and
  telecom) and have become a major source of **exports** and of skilled employment.

Underpinning all three is **infrastructure**: power, transport, communication and finance are the
common foundation without which no sector can grow. Because the sectors are **linked** — farms supply
mills, mills need services, services serve both — balanced growth across all three, resting on good
infrastructure, is the usual policy aim rather than growth in one sector alone.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Hold the sector roles as short tags. Agriculture: <strong>food, raw materials, employment, a rural
    market</strong>. Industry: <strong>modernisation, capital goods, productive jobs, less import
    dependence</strong>. Services: <strong>the largest and fastest-growing part of output, and a major
    exporter</strong>. Infrastructure is the <strong>common foundation</strong> under all three, not a
    fourth sector.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 12. India and the world economy

Since the 1991 reforms India has become steadily more **integrated with the world economy** — the
"globalization" strand of LPG carried on and deepened. Integration shows up in a few plain ways that
the paper describes structurally rather than with figures.

- **Trade.** Import tariffs and quantitative restrictions were cut, so Indian firms both **export**
  more (notably services such as software, and a range of manufactures and farm goods) and face more
  **import** competition. The economy's **openness** — the weight of trade in the economy — has risen.
- **Foreign investment.** The economy was opened to **foreign direct investment (FDI)** — where a
  foreign firm sets up or takes a lasting stake in a business — and to **foreign portfolio investment**
  in shares and bonds. Foreign capital brings funds, technology and management practices.
- **Global institutions.** India takes part in the multilateral trading system through the **World
  Trade Organization (WTO)** and works with bodies such as the International Monetary Fund and the
  World Bank; this is the institutional side of integration.

Integration brings gains — larger markets, cheaper inputs, new technology, more competition and choice
— and risks — greater exposure to global downturns and to volatile capital flows. The balanced
Foundation view is that India moved, after 1991, from a **relatively closed, inward-looking** economy
toward an **open, outward-oriented** one, while remaining a developing mixed economy.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Globalization does not mean India stopped being a mixed economy or became purely capitalist. It
    means the economy became more <strong>open</strong> — more trade, more foreign investment, more
    exposure to world markets. And <strong>foreign direct investment (a lasting stake in a business)</strong>
    is not the same as <strong>portfolio investment (buying shares and bonds)</strong>; the paper
    sometimes separates the two.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 13. Negative marking and when to leave a question

Paper 4 is entirely objective and carries **negative marking of 0.25 mark for each wrong answer**. A
correct answer earns 1 mark, a wrong one costs 0.25, and an unattempted one scores 0.

The arithmetic is the same as for any objective paper. A blind guess among four options is right one
time in four: across four such guesses you expect 1 mark gained and 3 × 0.25 = 0.75 lost, a net of
0.25 marks for four questions — very slightly positive, with a wide spread. What changes the picture is
**elimination**. Rule out one option and a guess among the remaining three is worth about 0.5 marks per
four attempts; rule out two and the guess between the last two is worth about 1.5 marks per four.

This chapter is unusually kind to elimination, because almost every distractor is a **named confusion**
— a sector misplaced, a reform strand swapped, the Planning Commission mistaken for NITI Aayog,
disguised unemployment read as seasonal. A definition you know lets you strike two options on sight. A
calm working rule: **answer whenever you can eliminate at least one option, and consider leaving a
question only when you cannot narrow the field at all.** The marks at stake either way are small, so
nothing here needs to be done anxiously.

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — classifying activities into sectors (easy)">
  <p>
    Place each activity in the primary, secondary or tertiary sector: (a) a fisherman catching fish in
    the sea; (b) a company assembling refrigerators in a factory; (c) a bank clerk processing a loan;
    (d) coal being dug from a mine.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — ask what the activity does to the good</div>
    (a) Fishing draws a good directly from nature → <strong>primary</strong>.<br />
    (b) Assembling refrigerators transforms materials into a manufactured good → <strong>secondary</strong>.<br />
    (c) Processing a loan provides a service, not a good → <strong>tertiary</strong>.<br />
    (d) Mining takes minerals directly from the earth → <strong>primary</strong> (the classic
    misplacement — it feels industrial but it is a primary activity).
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — reading the structural shift (easy-moderate)">
  <p>
    Over the decades since independence, which sector's share of India's output has fallen the most, and
    which sector has become the largest contributor to output? What is the overall direction of the
    shift?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — direction, not numbers</div>
    The <strong>primary sector's</strong> share of output has fallen the most over time, even though it
    still employs a large share of the workforce.<br />
    The <strong>tertiary (services) sector</strong> has grown fastest and is now the <strong>largest</strong>
    contributor to output.<br />
    The overall direction is a shift <strong>away from the primary sector and toward the tertiary
    sector</strong> — the classic primary → secondary → tertiary movement, with India notable for its
    services-led structure.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — matching a measure to an LPG strand (moderate)">
  <p>
    For each 1991-reform measure, say whether it is Liberalization (L), Privatization (P) or
    Globalization (G): (a) abolishing the industrial licensing requirement for most industries; (b)
    selling part of the government's shareholding in a public-sector undertaking; (c) cutting import
    tariffs and easing restrictions on foreign trade; (d) allowing foreign direct investment into new
    sectors.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — internal control, ownership, or opening outward?</div>
    (a) Removing licensing frees domestic business from internal controls → <strong>Liberalization (L)</strong>.<br />
    (b) Selling a government stake (disinvestment) shifts ownership toward the private sector →
    <strong>Privatization (P)</strong>.<br />
    (c) Cutting import tariffs opens the economy to world trade → <strong>Globalization (G)</strong>.<br />
    (d) Opening to foreign investment links India to world capital → <strong>Globalization (G)</strong>.<br />
    L works inside the country, P shifts ownership, G opens the economy outward.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — identifying the type of unemployment (moderate)">
  <p>
    Name the type of unemployment in each case: (a) six members of a family work a small plot that four
    could farm just as well; (b) a textile worker loses his job for three months every year when the
    unit shuts in the lean season; (c) engineers are laid off across many firms during a general
    economic slump; (d) a technician's old skill is no longer wanted after the industry modernises.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — match the cue to the type</div>
    (a) More workers than the land needs; removing two would not cut output → <strong>disguised
    unemployment</strong> (zero marginal productivity, common in agriculture).<br />
    (b) Idle only in the lean season, work returns each year → <strong>seasonal unemployment</strong>.<br />
    (c) Job losses across many firms in a slump → <strong>cyclical unemployment</strong>.<br />
    (d) Skills no longer match the jobs after the economy's structure changes → <strong>structural
    unemployment</strong>.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — the Planning Commission and NITI Aayog (moderate)">
  <p>
    A student writes: "NITI Aayog is the new name of the Planning Commission; it allocates resources to
    the states and prepares Five-Year Plans, and it was set up in 1991." Identify every error and give
    the correct position.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — three errors to correct</div>
    Error 1 — it is not merely a renaming. NITI Aayog <strong>replaced</strong> the Planning Commission
    as a body of a different kind: a <strong>policy think-tank and advisory body</strong>, not a
    resource-allocating planning authority.<br />
    Error 2 — NITI Aayog does <strong>not</strong> allocate resources or prepare numbered Five-Year Plans
    in the old top-down way; it advises and builds strategy around <strong>cooperative federalism</strong>
    (working with the states).<br />
    Error 3 — the year is wrong: NITI Aayog was set up in <strong>2015</strong>, not 1991 (1991 is the
    year of the LPG reforms).
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 6 — features of a developing mixed economy (moderate)">
  <p>
    Which of these are features of the Indian economy, and which are not: (a) private and public sectors
    coexist; (b) the economy is fully centrally planned with no private sector; (c) a modern sector and a
    traditional sector operate side by side; (d) services form the largest part of output. Name the two
    described in (a) and (c).
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — sort feature from non-feature</div>
    (a) True — coexistence of private and public sectors is the defining feature of a <strong>mixed
    economy</strong>.<br />
    (b) False — India is a mixed economy, not a fully centrally planned one with no private sector.<br />
    (c) True — a modern and a traditional economy side by side is <strong>economic dualism</strong>, a
    mark of a developing economy.<br />
    (d) True — the <strong>services (tertiary)</strong> sector is the largest contributor to output.<br />
    So (a) describes a mixed economy and (c) describes dualism; (b) is the non-feature.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 7 — the 1991 crisis and the reform response (moderate-hard)">
  <p>
    In 1991 India faced a crisis in which its foreign-exchange reserves fell so low it could barely pay
    for a few weeks of imports. (a) Name the crisis. (b) Name the three-strand package that followed and
    say, in one line each, what each strand did. (c) Did the reforms make India a purely capitalist
    economy?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) and (b), the crisis and the package</div>
    (a) It was a <strong>balance-of-payments crisis</strong> — India was close to being unable to meet
    its external payments.<br />
    (b) The response was the <strong>LPG reforms</strong>: <strong>Liberalization</strong> freed domestic
    business from internal controls (delicensing); <strong>Privatization</strong> reduced the state's
    ownership role (disinvestment); <strong>Globalization</strong> opened the economy to world trade and
    foreign investment.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (c) the balanced answer</div>
    No. The reforms moved India toward a more <strong>market-oriented and open</strong> economy, but the
    public and private sectors still coexist, so India remained a <strong>mixed economy</strong> — more
    liberal and more open than before, not purely capitalist.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes and examiner traps

- **Output share versus workforce share.** Agriculture still employs a large share of the workforce,
  but the **services** sector is the largest share of **output**. "Agriculture is the largest part of
  the economy" is true only of employment, not of output.
- **Misplacing mining.** Mining and quarrying take goods directly from nature, so they are
  **primary**, not secondary — despite feeling industrial.
- **The direction of the structural shift.** The shift runs **primary → secondary → tertiary** as an
  economy develops; India has moved away from the primary sector and toward services. Do not reverse
  it, and do not read it as agriculture "disappearing".
- **Planning Commission versus NITI Aayog.** The Planning Commission (old) allocated resources and made
  Five-Year Plans top-down; NITI Aayog (new, **2015**) is a think-tank built on cooperative federalism
  and does not hand down five-year targets. Do not treat them as the same body, and do not misdate the
  change.
- **The three LPG strands.** Liberalization = removing internal controls (delicensing); Privatization =
  disinvestment and more private ownership; Globalization = opening to trade and foreign investment.
  Swapping any two is the commonest reform error.
- **Absolute versus relative poverty; the head-count ratio.** Absolute poverty is measured against a
  fixed minimum (the poverty line); relative poverty compares people with others (it is really about
  inequality). The head-count ratio is a **proportion of people** below the line, not a rupee amount.
- **The types of unemployment.** Disguised = more workers than needed, zero marginal productivity
  (Indian agriculture); seasonal = idle in the off-season; structural = skills/location mismatch;
  cyclical = a downturn; frictional = between jobs. Disguised and seasonal are the two most confused.
- **Inflation and human development.** Inflation is a **sustained rise in the general price level**, not
  a one-off price jump. Human development (the HDI) combines **health, education and income**, not
  income alone.
- **What globalization did and did not do.** It made India more **open** (trade, foreign investment);
  it did not turn India into a purely capitalist economy — it remains a mixed economy.

## One-page revision summary

**India in one line.** A **developing (emerging) mixed economy** — lower average income and a large
farm-dependent workforce (developing), with private and public sectors coexisting (mixed).

**Features.** Mixed system; a large but shrinking role for agriculture (big workforce share, smaller
output share); a large, young population (demographic dividend); **dualism** (a modern and a
traditional sector side by side); a large and growing **services** sector; a large **informal**
sector.

**Three sectors.** **Primary** = agriculture and allied, mining — draws goods from nature.
**Secondary** = industry/manufacturing — transforms raw materials into goods. **Tertiary** = services —
banking, transport, trade, IT. Mining is primary.

**Structural shift.** As an economy develops, weight moves **primary → secondary → tertiary**. In
India the primary share of output has fallen, and **services are now the largest** part of output,
though agriculture still employs a large workforce.

**Planning.** Objectives: **growth, equity, self-reliance, modernization**. Instrument: the **Five-Year
Plans**, prepared by the **Planning Commission** (top-down, resource-allocating).

**Planning Commission → NITI Aayog (2015).** NITI Aayog (National Institution for Transforming India)
is a **policy think-tank** built on **cooperative federalism**; it replaced the Planning Commission and
does not hand down five-year targets.

**1991 reforms.** Triggered by a **balance-of-payments crisis**. The **LPG** package:
**Liberalization** (delicensing, removing internal controls), **Privatization** (disinvestment, more
private ownership), **Globalization** (opening to trade and foreign investment). India became more
open and market-oriented, but stayed a mixed economy.

**Development challenges.** **Poverty** (measured by a poverty line and the head-count ratio; absolute
versus relative). **Unemployment** types: structural, cyclical, frictional, **disguised** (zero
marginal productivity, farming), seasonal. **Inflation** (sustained rise in the general price level).
**Income inequality** (Lorenz curve, Gini). **Infrastructure** (power, transport, communication,
finance). The large **informal sector**. **Human development** (HDI = health + education + income).

**Roles.** Agriculture: food, raw materials, employment, a rural market. Industry: modernisation,
capital goods, productive jobs, less import dependence. Services: largest and fastest-growing output,
a major exporter. Infrastructure is the common foundation.

**India and the world.** Since 1991 more **open**: more trade, more **FDI** and portfolio investment,
membership of the **WTO**; gains (bigger markets, technology, competition) and risks (exposure to
global shocks).

**Marking.** 1 mark correct, −0.25 wrong, 0 unattempted. Guessing among four is close to neutral;
eliminating even one option makes an attempt clearly worthwhile.
