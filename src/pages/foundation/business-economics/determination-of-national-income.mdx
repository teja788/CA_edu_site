Every year the newspapers report that India's GDP "grew by 7 per cent". A shopkeeper in Nagpur, a
software engineer in Bengaluru and a farmer in Punjab all add to that single number, each time they
produce something and earn from it. National income accounting is the careful bookkeeping that adds
up the whole country's production and income into a handful of totals — GDP, GNP, national income —
and the second half of the chapter asks the deeper question: what fixes the level of that income in
the first place, and why does it rise or fall? The answer, in Keynes's framework, turns on how much
households choose to spend and how much firms choose to invest.

Paper 4 is an objective paper, and this chapter carries two kinds of question. The first tests
whether you can state a definition cleanly — what GNP adds to GDP, why factor cost differs from
market price, what personal income leaves out. The second asks you to **compute** — to walk GDP at
market price down to national income, to total up the expenditure or income method, to read a GDP
deflator, or to work an average and marginal propensity and the multiplier that follows from it. The
computations are short. Marks are lost on the direction of an adjustment (add depreciation or
subtract it?), on confusing market price with factor cost, and on reading the multiplier off the
wrong propensity.

## 1. National income: what it measures

**National income** is the money value of all the final goods and services produced by the normal
residents of a country during an accounting year. It is a **flow**, measured over a period (a year),
not a stock held at a point in time, and it is valued in money so that unlike things — wheat, haircuts
and steel — can be added into one total.

Three words in that definition do real work.

- **Final goods.** Only goods that reach their final user are counted. The flour a bakery buys is an
  **intermediate good**; counting both the flour and the bread would count the flour twice. Section 9
  makes this the central rule of the product method.
- **Produced during the year.** Only current production counts. The resale of an old house or a
  second-hand car is not new production, so it is excluded — though the broker's fee on the sale is,
  because that service is produced this year.
- **Normal residents.** National income belongs to the residents of the country, wherever they earn
  it. This is the point at which "national" parts company with "domestic", and Section 2 turns on it.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A national income aggregate is meaningless until three things are fixed: <strong>whose</strong>
    income (national = residents, domestic = within the territory), at <strong>which valuation</strong>
    (market price or factor cost), and <strong>before or after</strong> providing for the wearing-out
    of capital (gross or net). Almost every conversion question in this chapter is really a question
    about which of these three you are being asked to change.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. The aggregates: GDP, GNP, NDP and NNP

The four core aggregates are built from two choices laid on top of one another: **domestic versus
national**, and **gross versus net**. Get those two axes straight and the whole family follows.

**Domestic versus national.** **Gross Domestic Product (GDP)** is the value of output produced
**within the geographical territory** of a country, no matter who owns the factors that produced it.
**Gross National Product (GNP)** is the value of output produced by the **normal residents** of the
country, wherever in the world they earn it. The bridge between the two is the **net factor income
from abroad (NFIA)** — the factor income (wages, interest, rent, profit) that residents earn abroad,
minus the factor income foreigners earn inside the country.

<div class="formula">GNP = GDP + net factor income from abroad (NFIA)</div>

NFIA can be positive or negative. If residents earn more abroad than foreigners earn at home, NFIA is
positive and GNP exceeds GDP; if the reverse, NFIA is negative and GNP is smaller than GDP. For most
developing economies NFIA is slightly negative.

**Gross versus net.** Producing output wears out machines and buildings — this using-up of capital is
**depreciation** (also called consumption of fixed capital). A **gross** measure ignores it; a
**net** measure subtracts it. Subtracting depreciation from a gross aggregate gives the matching net
aggregate.

<div class="formula">Net product = Gross product − depreciation</div>

Combine the two axes and the four aggregates appear. Each also comes **at market price** or **at
factor cost**, which Section 3 handles; the table below states them at market price.

| Aggregate | Full name | How it is built |
|---|---|---|
| GDP | Gross Domestic Product | output within the territory, before depreciation |
| GNP | Gross National Product | GDP + NFIA |
| NDP | Net Domestic Product | GDP − depreciation |
| NNP | Net National Product | GNP − depreciation |

<div class="formula">NDP = GDP − depreciation · NNP = GNP − depreciation · GNP = GDP + NFIA</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The two adjustments run in opposite directions and are easy to swap. Moving from
    <strong>domestic to national</strong> you <strong>add</strong> NFIA; moving from
    <strong>gross to net</strong> you <strong>subtract</strong> depreciation. So NNP = GDP + NFIA −
    depreciation. Adding depreciation, or subtracting NFIA, is the classic sign slip that turns a
    right method into a wrong number.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. Market price and factor cost: net indirect taxes

An aggregate can be valued two ways. **At market price** counts each good at the price the buyer
actually pays — a price that includes indirect taxes (GST, excise) and is reduced by any subsidy. **At
factor cost** counts output at what the factors of production actually received — the payment to land,
labour, capital and enterprise, with no tax added and no subsidy taken away. The gap between the two
valuations is **net indirect taxes**.

<div class="formula">Net indirect taxes = indirect taxes − subsidies</div>
<div class="formula">Market price = Factor cost + net indirect taxes</div>
<div class="formula">Factor cost = Market price − net indirect taxes</div>

Indirect taxes push the market price **above** factor cost (the government takes a slice the factors
never see); subsidies pull it **below** (the buyer pays less than the factors received). The net of
the two is what separates the two valuations. To go from a market-price aggregate to its factor-cost
twin, **subtract** net indirect taxes; to go the other way, **add** them.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — factor cost from market price</div>
  NDP at market price = ₹ 4,600 crore; indirect taxes = ₹ 700 crore; subsidies = ₹ 150 crore<br />
  Net indirect taxes = 700 − 150 = ₹ 550 crore<br />
  NDP at factor cost = 4,600 − 550 = <strong>₹ 4,050 crore</strong>
</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Hold one sentence: <strong>market price is the bigger, tax-inclusive figure; factor cost is what
    the factors kept</strong>. So market price = factor cost + net indirect taxes. When a question
    gives indirect taxes and subsidies separately, the quantity you need is nearly always their
    difference, <strong>net</strong> indirect taxes — not the indirect taxes alone.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. National income, personal income and disposable income

The headline figure called **National Income (NI)** is one specific aggregate: **Net National Product
at factor cost (NNP at factor cost)**. It is the total factor income — rent, wages, interest and
profit — earned by the normal residents of the country in the year.

<div class="formula">National Income = NNP at factor cost = NNP at market price − net indirect taxes</div>

From national income two further "who actually receives it" measures are derived. Not all of national
income reaches households, and households receive some income that was not earned in production.

**Personal Income (PI)** is the income actually **received** by households, from all sources. It
starts from national income, **removes** the parts earned but not received by households — corporate
(undistributed) profits kept back by companies, corporate profit taxes, and social security
contributions — and **adds** transfer payments households receive from the government (pensions,
scholarships, unemployment relief), which are income received but not earned in current production.

<div class="formula">Personal Income = National Income − undistributed profits − corporate taxes − social security contributions + transfer payments</div>

**Disposable Personal Income (DPI)** is what households can actually **spend or save** — personal
income after direct (personal) taxes are paid.

<div class="formula">Disposable Personal Income = Personal Income − direct (personal) taxes</div>
<div class="formula">Disposable Personal Income = Consumption + Saving</div>

The last identity matters for Unit 2: a household does only two things with its disposable income —
spend it or save it — so consumption plus saving must equal disposable income.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Transfer payments</strong> — pensions, scholarships, unemployment benefit, gifts — are
    payments made without any good or service produced in return. They are <strong>excluded</strong>
    from national income (nothing was produced), but they are <strong>added</strong> when moving from
    national income to personal income, because a household really does receive them. Treating a
    transfer payment as production, or forgetting it at the personal-income stage, are opposite errors
    the examiner sets side by side.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. The aggregates at a glance: the key relationships

Every conversion in Unit 1 is one of a small set of moves. This table is the whole map; read a
question as "which move, and in which direction?"

| To go from | to | do this |
|---|---|---|
| GDP | GNP | **add** net factor income from abroad |
| Gross (GDP or GNP) | Net (NDP or NNP) | **subtract** depreciation |
| Market price | Factor cost | **subtract** net indirect taxes |
| NNP at market price | National Income | **subtract** net indirect taxes (this gives NNP at factor cost) |

<div class="formula">GNP at market price = GDP at market price + NFIA</div>
<div class="formula">NNP at market price = GNP at market price − depreciation</div>
<div class="formula">National Income (NNP at factor cost) = NNP at market price − (indirect taxes − subsidies)</div>

The full walk from the broadest gross-and-market figure down to national income chains these moves:

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — GDP at market price down to National Income</div>
  Given: GDP at market price = ₹ 8,000 crore; NFIA = −₹ 100 crore; depreciation = ₹ 700 crore;
  indirect taxes = ₹ 900 crore; subsidies = ₹ 200 crore.<br />
  GNP at market price = 8,000 + (−100) = ₹ 7,900 crore<br />
  NNP at market price = 7,900 − 700 = ₹ 7,200 crore<br />
  Net indirect taxes = 900 − 200 = ₹ 700 crore<br />
  National Income (NNP at factor cost) = 7,200 − 700 = <strong>₹ 6,500 crore</strong>
</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A long "find national income" question is never hard — it is the same three moves in a fixed
    order: <strong>add NFIA, subtract depreciation, subtract net indirect taxes</strong>. Do them one
    line at a time and label each line with the aggregate it produces. Most wrong answers come from
    stopping one line early (quoting NNP at market price as if it were national income) or getting one
    sign backwards.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. Nominal GDP, real GDP and the GDP deflator

A rise in GDP can mean two very different things: the country produced **more**, or the same output
simply cost **more**. Separating the two is the job of the real–nominal distinction.

**Nominal GDP** (GDP at current prices) values this year's output at this year's prices, so it rises
both when output rises and when prices rise. **Real GDP** (GDP at constant prices) values output at
the prices of a fixed **base year**, so it rises only when output actually rises. Real GDP is the
honest measure of growth, because it strips inflation out.

The ratio of the two, scaled by 100, is the **GDP deflator** — a price index for the whole economy.

<div class="formula">GDP deflator = (Nominal GDP ÷ Real GDP) × 100</div>
<div class="formula">Real GDP = (Nominal GDP ÷ GDP deflator) × 100</div>

A deflator of 100 means prices are unchanged from the base year; above 100 means the general price
level has risen; below 100 means it has fallen. In the base year itself, nominal and real GDP are
equal by construction and the deflator is exactly 100.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — the deflator and back again</div>
  Nominal GDP = ₹ 6,000 crore; Real GDP = ₹ 5,000 crore<br />
  GDP deflator = (6,000 ÷ 5,000) × 100 = <strong>120</strong> — prices are 20% above the base year<br />
  Check the inverse: with nominal ₹ 7,200 crore and a deflator of 120, Real GDP = (7,200 ÷ 120) × 100
  = <strong>₹ 6,000 crore</strong>
</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The deflator is <strong>nominal over real</strong>, then ×100 — not real over nominal. Flipping
    the ratio gives 83.3 instead of 120 and reverses the story (it would say prices fell when they
    rose). A quick sanity check: when the economy has inflation, nominal GDP is the larger number, so
    the deflator must come out <strong>above 100</strong>.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. Per capita income

**Per capita income** is national income divided by the population — the income per head. It is the
common way to compare living standards across countries or across years, because a large national
income shared among a large population can still mean modest incomes per person.

<div class="formula">Per capita income = National Income ÷ Population</div>

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — income per head</div>
  National Income = ₹ 5,00,000 crore; Population = 25 crore<br />
  Per capita income = 5,00,000 ÷ 25 = <strong>₹ 20,000</strong> per person
</div>

Per capita income is only an **average**, so it hides the distribution of income: two countries with
the same per capita income can have very different degrees of inequality. That limitation carries into
Section 13, where national income is judged as a measure of welfare.

## 8. The circular flow of income

The **circular flow of income** shows how income moves round the economy in a never-ending loop.
Households own the factors of production and sell their services to firms; firms pay households
**factor incomes** (rent, wages, interest, profit) for them; households spend that income buying the
goods and services firms produce; and that spending returns to firms as **receipts**, which they pay
out again as factor incomes. Production, income and expenditure are three views of the **same** flow —
which is exactly why the three methods of Sections 9 to 11 must give the same national income total.

The model is built up in stages by adding sectors.

- **Two-sector economy** — households and firms only. Income flows from firms to households as factor
  payments and back as consumption spending.
- **Three-sector economy** — add the **government**, which withdraws income as taxes and injects it as
  government spending and transfer payments.
- **Four-sector economy** — add the **foreign sector** (the rest of the world), which withdraws income
  through imports and injects it through exports.

Anything that takes income **out** of the circular flow is a **leakage** (saving, taxes, imports);
anything that puts income **in** is an **injection** (investment, government spending, exports). The
flow is in balance — income is at its equilibrium level — when total leakages equal total injections.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The circular flow is the reason the three measurement methods agree: <strong>value of output =
    income paid out = expenditure received</strong>, because every rupee of output is someone's income
    and someone else's spending. Learn the leakage/injection pairing — saving–investment, taxes–government
    spending, imports–exports — because Unit 2 finds equilibrium exactly where the leakages match the
    injections (saving = investment in the simple model).
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. The value added (product) method

The three methods measure the same national income from three sides. The first is the **value added**
or **product method**, which totals the **value added** by every producing unit in the economy.

**Value added** by a firm is the value of its output minus the value of the **intermediate goods** it
bought from other firms. Summing value added across all firms avoids **double counting** — the error
of adding the full value of output at every stage, which would count intermediate goods again and
again.

<div class="formula">Value added = Value of output − Value of intermediate consumption</div>
<div class="formula">GDP at market price = Sum of value added by all producing units</div>

A wheat-to-bread example makes the point. A farmer grows ₹ 100 of wheat (no inputs), sells it to a
miller who turns it into ₹ 170 of flour, who sells to a baker who bakes ₹ 300 of bread. The value
added is 100, then 170 − 100 = 70, then 300 − 170 = 130 — a total of ₹ 300, exactly the value of the
final good, the bread. Adding the three outputs (100 + 170 + 300 = 570) would double-count the wheat
and the flour.

**Precautions.** Count the value added of each firm, not its whole output; exclude the sale and
purchase of **second-hand goods** (they were counted when first produced), though commissions on their
sale are included; and value own-account production (a farmer's self-consumed grain, the imputed rent
of an owner-occupied house) at market prices.

## 10. The income method

The **income method** totals the **factor incomes** paid out in producing the output — the payments to
the factors of production. Because every rupee of value added is paid to some factor, the sum of factor
incomes equals the sum of value added.

<div class="formula">Domestic income = Rent + Wages (compensation of employees) + Interest + Profit + Mixed income</div>

The components are the rewards of the factors: **rent** for land, **wages and salaries** (compensation
of employees) for labour, **interest** for capital, and **profit** for enterprise. **Mixed income** is
the income of the self-employed — a shopkeeper, a farmer, a doctor in private practice — where wages,
rent, interest and profit cannot be separated, so they are lumped together.

This sum gives **domestic** income (net domestic product at factor cost). Adding net factor income
from abroad turns it into **national** income.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — income method total</div>
  Compensation of employees = ₹ 2,500 crore; rent = ₹ 400 crore; interest = ₹ 300 crore;
  profit = ₹ 600 crore; mixed income = ₹ 700 crore<br />
  Domestic income (NDP at factor cost) = 2,500 + 400 + 300 + 600 + 700 = <strong>₹ 4,500 crore</strong>
</div>

**Precautions.** Include only **factor** incomes, never **transfer payments** (pensions, gifts,
scholarships) — they are received without production. Exclude income from the sale of second-hand goods
and from financial transactions (buying shares or bonds is a transfer of ownership, not production).
Include the imputed value of own-account services.

## 11. The expenditure method

The **expenditure method** totals the spending on the economy's **final** goods and services by the
four groups of buyers — households, firms, government and the rest of the world.

<div class="formula">GDP at market price = C + I + G + (X − M)</div>

The four components are: **private final consumption expenditure (C)** by households; **gross domestic
investment (I)**, the spending of firms on capital goods and additions to stock; **government final
consumption expenditure (G)**; and **net exports (X − M)**, exports minus imports. Imports are
subtracted because they are produced abroad, not part of domestic output, yet they are already buried
inside C, I and G.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — expenditure method total</div>
  C = ₹ 3,000 crore; I = ₹ 800 crore; G = ₹ 1,200 crore; exports X = ₹ 500 crore; imports M = ₹ 400 crore<br />
  Net exports (X − M) = 500 − 400 = ₹ 100 crore<br />
  GDP at market price = 3,000 + 800 + 1,200 + 100 = <strong>₹ 5,100 crore</strong>
</div>

**Precautions.** Count only spending on **final** goods, never on intermediate goods (that is the
double-counting error again, from the spending side). Exclude spending on **second-hand goods** and on
**financial assets** (shares, bonds), and exclude **transfer payments** by the government, because none
of these is expenditure on current output.

## 12. Precautions and reconciling the three methods

The three methods are three windows on one flow, so — properly measured — they give the **same** GDP.
The circular flow is the reason: the value of output equals the income paid to produce it equals the
expenditure that buys it.

| Method | What it sums | Chief precaution |
|---|---|---|
| Value added (product) | value added of every firm | count value added, not whole output; no double counting |
| Income | factor incomes (rent, wages, interest, profit, mixed) | include factor incomes only; **exclude transfer payments** |
| Expenditure | C + I + G + (X − M) | spend on **final** goods only; exclude second-hand goods and financial assets |

Three threads run through every method's precautions, and every one is an examiner favourite:

- **No double counting** — exclude intermediate goods (product side) and intermediate spending
  (expenditure side).
- **No transfer payments** — they are income received without production, so they enter none of the
  three totals.
- **No second-hand goods or financial transactions** — these are transfers of existing assets, not
  current production; only the **commission or service fee** on such deals is included, because that
  service is produced now.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The purchase of <strong>shares or bonds</strong> is not investment in the national-income sense. In
    economics "investment" (I) means spending on <strong>new capital goods</strong> — plant, machinery,
    buildings, additions to stock. Buying a share is a <strong>financial</strong> transaction, a change
    of ownership of an existing asset, and is excluded from every method. The everyday sense of
    "investing in shares" is the trap.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 13. National income as a measure of welfare

National income (and per capita income) is the usual proxy for a country's economic welfare, but it is
an imperfect one, and the exam asks you to name the gaps.

- **Distribution is hidden.** Per capita income is an average; the same average can sit on top of very
  different degrees of inequality, and rising national income can coexist with the poor becoming worse
  off.
- **Non-market production is left out.** The unpaid work of a homemaker, or subsistence output consumed
  by the grower, adds to welfare but is largely missed by the accounts.
- **The composition of output is ignored.** A rupee of output is counted the same whether it is
  medicine or armaments; more guns and less food can still raise national income.
- **Externalities and the value of leisure are not counted.** The pollution and resource depletion that
  production causes reduce welfare but are not netted off, and extra leisure raises welfare without
  raising measured income.
- **Non-monetary and black-economy transactions** escape measurement.

For these reasons a rise in national income is a **necessary but not sufficient** signal of rising
welfare — a signpost, not a full picture.

## 14. Aggregate demand and aggregate supply

Unit 2 turns from measuring income to explaining what **determines** it. Keynes's answer is that the
level of income and employment is fixed by **aggregate demand** and **aggregate supply**, and in the
short run — with spare capacity and unemployed resources — it is **aggregate demand** that does the
work.

**Aggregate demand (AD)** is the total planned spending on the economy's output at a given level of
income. In the simple model it is consumption plus investment; adding the other sectors it is
C + I + G + (X − M). **Aggregate supply (AS)** is the total value of output producers plan to supply —
which, since producing it pays out exactly that value in incomes, equals the income of the economy.

Equilibrium income is where planned spending matches output — where **aggregate demand equals aggregate
supply**. Because Keynes assumed idle resources, an increase in aggregate demand raises **output and
employment** rather than simply raising prices: firms meet the extra demand by producing more. The
components of aggregate demand — chiefly consumption and investment — are therefore the levers that
move national income, and the next sections examine each.

## 15. The consumption function: APC and MPC

The **consumption function** states how planned consumption depends on income: as income rises,
consumption rises, but by **less** than the rise in income. Keynes wrote it as a straight line.

<div class="formula">C = a + bY</div>

Here **C** is consumption, **Y** is income, **a** is **autonomous consumption** (the consumption that
takes place even at zero income, met by past savings or borrowing), and **b** is the **marginal
propensity to consume**, the slope of the line. Two ratios describe the function.

**Average propensity to consume (APC)** is the share of total income that is consumed.

<div class="formula">APC = Consumption ÷ Income = C ÷ Y</div>

**Marginal propensity to consume (MPC)** is the fraction of any **extra** income that is consumed — the
change in consumption divided by the change in income.

<div class="formula">MPC = Change in consumption ÷ Change in income = ΔC ÷ ΔY = b</div>

MPC lies between 0 and 1: an extra rupee of income raises consumption, but by less than a rupee,
because part of it is saved. As income rises, APC **falls** (the fixed autonomous consumption `a`
becomes a smaller share of a bigger income) and stays **above** MPC.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — APC and MPC from a schedule</div>
  When income Y = ₹ 1,000 crore, consumption C = ₹ 800 crore → APC = 800 ÷ 1,000 = <strong>0.8</strong><br />
  When income rises to ₹ 1,100 crore, consumption rises to ₹ 860 crore →
  ΔC = 60, ΔY = 100 → MPC = 60 ÷ 100 = <strong>0.6</strong><br />
  APC (0.8) is above MPC (0.6), as it must be while autonomous consumption is positive.
</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>APC and MPC are different questions.</strong> APC is consumption as a share of
    <em>total</em> income (C ÷ Y); MPC is the share of <em>extra</em> income consumed (ΔC ÷ ΔY). A
    stem that gives one income–consumption pair is asking for APC; a stem that gives a <em>change</em>
    in each is asking for MPC. Reading "change in" is the whole difference between the two.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 16. The saving function: APS and MPS

Whatever income is not consumed is **saved**, so the saving function is the mirror image of the
consumption function. Since Y = C + S, saving is income minus consumption.

<div class="formula">S = Y − C = −a + (1 − b)Y</div>

The intercept is **−a**: at zero income the household must still consume `a`, so it **dis-saves** by
that amount. The slope, **(1 − b)**, is the **marginal propensity to save**. Two ratios match those of
consumption.

**Average propensity to save (APS)** is the share of income saved; **marginal propensity to save
(MPS)** is the fraction of extra income saved.

<div class="formula">APS = Saving ÷ Income = S ÷ Y</div>
<div class="formula">MPS = Change in saving ÷ Change in income = ΔS ÷ ΔY</div>

Because income is either consumed or saved, the two pairs of ratios each sum to one.

<div class="formula">MPC + MPS = 1 · APC + APS = 1</div>

So MPS = 1 − MPC and APS = 1 − APC. If the marginal propensity to consume is 0.75, the marginal
propensity to save is 0.25; if 80 paise of an extra rupee is spent, 20 paise is saved.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — APS and MPS</div>
  Income Y = ₹ 2,000 crore, consumption C = ₹ 1,500 crore → saving S = 2,000 − 1,500 = ₹ 500 crore<br />
  APS = 500 ÷ 2,000 = <strong>0.25</strong> — and indeed APC = 1,500 ÷ 2,000 = 0.75, so APC + APS = 1<br />
  If MPC = 0.75, then MPS = 1 − 0.75 = <strong>0.25</strong>
</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The identities <strong>MPC + MPS = 1</strong> and <strong>APC + APS = 1</strong> turn half the
    Unit 2 numericals into one-step subtractions. Given any one propensity you have its partner for
    free, and the multiplier of Section 18 can be read off either the MPC or the MPS. Whenever a
    question gives MPC, write MPS = 1 − MPC in the margin before you start.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 17. Investment and the equilibrium level of income

**Investment (I)** is spending on new capital goods and additions to stock. In the simple Keynesian
model investment is taken as **autonomous** — fixed independently of the level of income, decided by
the rate of interest and the expected profitability of capital (the marginal efficiency of capital).

**Equilibrium income** is the level of income at which planned spending equals output. With only
households and firms, aggregate demand is C + I, so equilibrium is where income equals consumption plus
investment.

<div class="formula">Equilibrium: Y = C + I</div>

There is an equivalent way to state the same condition. Since Y = C + S always holds, setting
Y = C + I gives C + S = C + I, so **saving equals investment**.

<div class="formula">Equilibrium (equivalent form): S = I</div>

The saving–investment form is the leakage–injection idea from Section 8: income is at rest when the
leakage (saving) exactly matches the injection (investment). To find equilibrium income from a
consumption function and an investment figure, substitute C = a + bY into Y = C + I and solve for Y.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — equilibrium income</div>
  Consumption function C = 100 + 0.8Y; autonomous investment I = ₹ 100 crore<br />
  Y = C + I = (100 + 0.8Y) + 100<br />
  Y − 0.8Y = 200 → 0.2Y = 200 → <strong>Y = ₹ 1,000 crore</strong><br />
  Check with S = I: S = −100 + 0.2(1,000) = 100 = I ✓
</div>

## 18. The investment multiplier

An increase in investment raises income by a **multiple** of itself. The extra investment is income to
those who receive it; they spend a fraction (the MPC) of it, which is income to the next set of
people, who spend a fraction of that, and so on — a diminishing chain of extra spending that sums to
more than the original injection. The **investment multiplier (k)** is the ratio of the change in
income to the change in investment that caused it.

<div class="formula">k = ΔY ÷ ΔI = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ MPS</div>
<div class="formula">Change in income: ΔY = k × ΔI</div>

Because MPS = 1 − MPC, the multiplier can be read off either propensity — one over (one minus MPC), or
one over MPS. The **larger the MPC** (the smaller the MPS), the larger the multiplier, because more of
each round is passed on as further spending. With MPC = 0.8 the multiplier is 5; with MPC = 0.75 it is
4; with MPC = 0.5 it is only 2.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — the multiplier and the change in income</div>
  MPC = 0.75, so MPS = 1 − 0.75 = 0.25<br />
  Multiplier k = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.75) = 1 ÷ 0.25 = <strong>4</strong><br />
  If investment rises by ΔI = ₹ 200 crore, then ΔY = k × ΔI = 4 × 200 = <strong>₹ 800 crore</strong>
</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The multiplier is <strong>1 ÷ (1 − MPC)</strong>, which is the same as <strong>1 ÷ MPS</strong> —
    never 1 ÷ MPC. With MPC = 0.8, k = 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5, not 1 ÷ 0.8 = 1.25. A quick check: a high MPC means
    a <em>large</em> multiplier, so if your answer for a high MPC comes out small, you have divided by
    the wrong propensity.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 19. The paradox of thrift

The **paradox of thrift** is Keynes's counter-intuitive result that if **everyone** tries to save more
at once, the community may end up saving **no more**, or even less, and with a lower income. The
attempt to save more means spending less; lower spending is lower aggregate demand; through the
multiplier working in reverse, income falls; and out of the lower income the same or a smaller total is
actually saved. What is prudent for one household — saving more — can be self-defeating for the economy
as a whole when all households do it together.

The paradox holds in the Keynesian world of **idle resources**, where output is demand-determined. It
is a caution against reading a household truth as an economy-wide truth: a virtue in the part can be a
vice in the whole. It also shows the multiplier cuts both ways — a fall in planned spending contracts
income by a multiple, just as a rise expands it.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The paradox of thrift is a favourite one-line MCQ: an attempt by <strong>all</strong> households to
    save more reduces aggregate demand and, through the multiplier, lowers income, so total saving does
    not rise (and may fall). The key words the examiner watches for are "<strong>everyone/simultaneously</strong>"
    and "<strong>total</strong> saving" — the result is about the whole economy, not one saver.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 20. Negative marking and when to leave a question

Paper 4 is entirely objective and carries **negative marking of 0.25 mark for each wrong answer**. A
correct answer earns 1 mark, a wrong one costs 0.25, and an unattempted one scores 0.

The arithmetic is worth holding calmly. A blind guess among four options is right one time in four:
across four such guesses you expect 1 mark gained and 3 × 0.25 = 0.75 lost, a net of +0.25 for four
questions — very slightly positive but close to neutral. What changes the picture is **elimination**.
Rule out one option and a guess among three is worth about 0.5 marks per four attempts; rule out two
and the last guess is worth about 1.5 marks per four.

In this chapter elimination is unusually easy, because the distractors are built from named errors — a
depreciation added instead of subtracted, a deflator taken upside down, a multiplier read off MPC
rather than MPS. A sensible rule: **answer when you can eliminate at least one option; consider leaving
it only when you cannot narrow the field at all.** The marks at stake either way are small, so a
question you can actually finish is worth more than either choice.

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — building the aggregates from GDP at market price (easy-moderate)">
  <p>
    For an economy: GDP at market price = ₹ 9,000 crore, net factor income from abroad = ₹ 300 crore,
    depreciation = ₹ 600 crore, indirect taxes = ₹ 800 crore, subsidies = ₹ 100 crore. Find (a) GNP at

  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (c) subtract net indirect taxes to reach factor cost</div>

    National Income (NNP at factor cost) = 8,700 − 700 = <strong>₹ 8,000 crore</strong>
  </div>

    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) net indirect taxes</div>

  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) add net indirect taxes to move up to market price</div>
    NDP at market price = NDP at factor cost + net indirect taxes = 5,200 + 650 =
    <strong>₹ 5,850 crore</strong><br />
    Market price is the larger, tax-inclusive figure, so we <strong>add</strong> the net indirect taxes.
  </div>
</Callout>

    <strong>₹ 7,000 crore</strong><br />

  <p>
    From the following, find (a) domestic income (NDP at factor cost) and (b) National Income:
    compensation of employees ₹ 3,000 crore, rent ₹ 500 crore, interest ₹ 400 crore, profit ₹ 800
    crore, mixed income ₹ 900 crore, and net factor income from abroad ₹ 200 crore. (Note: pensions of

  </p>
  <div class="working-note">

  <p>
    In a year, nominal GDP is ₹ 12,000 crore and real GDP (at base-year prices) is ₹ 10,000 crore.

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 6 — APC, MPC, APS and MPS from a schedule (moderate)">
  <p>
    At an income of ₹ 2,000 crore a household consumes ₹ 1,700 crore. When income rises to ₹ 2,500
    crore, consumption rises to ₹ 2,050 crore. Find (a) APC at each income, (b) MPC, and (c) MPS.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) average propensity to consume</div>
    At Y = 2,000: APC = 1,700 ÷ 2,000 = <strong>0.85</strong><br />
    At Y = 2,500: APC = 2,050 ÷ 2,500 = <strong>0.82</strong> — APC falls as income rises
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) marginal propensity to consume</div>
    ΔC = 2,050 − 1,700 = 350; ΔY = 2,500 − 2,000 = 500<br />
    MPC = 350 ÷ 500 = <strong>0.7</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (c) marginal propensity to save</div>
    MPS = 1 − MPC = 1 − 0.7 = <strong>0.3</strong>
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 7 — equilibrium income from the consumption function (moderate)">
  <p>
    In a two-sector economy the consumption function is C = 200 + 0.75Y and autonomous investment is
    I = ₹ 300 crore. Find (a) the equilibrium level of income and (b) confirm it with S = I.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) set Y = C + I</div>
    Y = (200 + 0.75Y) + 300<br />
    Y − 0.75Y = 500 → 0.25Y = 500 → <strong>Y = ₹ 2,000 crore</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) check with the saving = investment form</div>
    Saving function: S = −200 + 0.25Y; at Y = 2,000, S = −200 + 0.25(2,000) = −200 + 500 = ₹ 300 crore<br />
    S = ₹ 300 crore = I ✓ — the two forms of the equilibrium condition agree.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 8 — the multiplier and a change in income (moderate-hard)">
  <p>
    In an economy the marginal propensity to consume is 0.8. (a) Find the investment multiplier.
    (b) Investment rises by ₹ 250 crore; find the change in income. (c) If instead the MPC were 0.6,
    what would the multiplier be?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) multiplier from the MPC</div>
    MPS = 1 − 0.8 = 0.2<br />
    k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ 0.2 = <strong>5</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) apply ΔY = k × ΔI</div>
    ΔY = k × ΔI = 5 × 250 = <strong>₹ 1,250 crore</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (c) a lower MPC gives a smaller multiplier</div>
    With MPC = 0.6, MPS = 0.4, so k = 1 ÷ 0.4 = <strong>2.5</strong> — a lower propensity to consume
    means a smaller multiplier, because less of each round is passed on.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 9 — the paradox of thrift (moderate)">
  <p>
    An economy has MPC = 0.75 and is in equilibrium. Households collectively decide to save ₹ 100 crore
    more at every level of income (autonomous saving rises, autonomous consumption falls by ₹ 100
    crore). Explain, with the multiplier, what happens to equilibrium income, and comment on total
    saving.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — the fall in autonomous spending works through the multiplier</div>
    Multiplier k = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.75) = 4<br />
    A ₹ 100 crore fall in autonomous consumption is a fall in aggregate demand, so
    ΔY = k × (−100) = 4 × (−100) = <strong>−₹ 400 crore</strong> — equilibrium income falls by ₹ 400 crore.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — why total saving need not rise</div>
    In equilibrium saving equals investment (S = I). If investment is unchanged, equilibrium saving is
    <strong>unchanged</strong> despite everyone trying to save more — the attempt only lowers income.
    This is the <strong>paradox of thrift</strong>: a greater desire to save, acted on by all at once,
    reduces income rather than raising total saving.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes and examiner traps

- **Adding depreciation instead of subtracting it.** Gross to net always **subtracts** depreciation
  (NDP = GDP − depreciation). Adding it is the commonest sign error in the aggregate chain.
- **Subtracting NFIA instead of adding it.** Domestic to national **adds** net factor income from
  abroad (GNP = GDP + NFIA). NFIA can itself be negative, which is not the same as subtracting it.
- **Market price versus factor cost.** Market price = factor cost + **net** indirect taxes. Use net
  indirect taxes (indirect taxes − subsidies), not the indirect taxes alone, and remember market price
  is the larger figure.
- **Stopping one line early.** National Income is NNP at **factor cost**. Quoting NNP at market price,
  or NDP, as "national income" loses the mark; finish all three moves (add NFIA, subtract depreciation,
  subtract net indirect taxes).
- **Counting transfer payments as production.** Pensions, scholarships and gifts enter **none** of the
  three methods (no output was produced), but they **are** added when moving from national income to
  personal income.
- **The GDP deflator upside down.** Deflator = nominal ÷ real × 100. With inflation the deflator is
  above 100 and nominal GDP is the bigger number.
- **Treating share purchases as investment.** In national income, investment (I) is spending on **new
  capital goods**, not the buying of shares or bonds, which is a financial transaction and is excluded.
- **APC versus MPC (and APS versus MPS).** APC uses total income (C ÷ Y); MPC uses the change (ΔC ÷ ΔY).
  A single pair of figures gives APC; a change in each gives MPC.
- **The multiplier from the wrong propensity.** k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷ MPS, never 1 ÷ MPC. A high MPC
  gives a large multiplier.
- **The paradox of thrift misread.** It applies when **all** households save more **at once** and refers
  to **total** saving, which need not rise; it is not a claim about a single saver.

## One-page revision summary

**National income** = money value of all final goods and services produced by a country's normal
residents in a year — a flow, valued in money, counting final output only.

**The four aggregates** turn on two axes — domestic/national and gross/net:

<div class="formula">GNP = GDP + NFIA · NDP = GDP − depreciation · NNP = GNP − depreciation = GDP + NFIA − depreciation</div>

**Market price vs factor cost:** market price = factor cost + net indirect taxes, where **net indirect
taxes = indirect taxes − subsidies**. **National Income = NNP at factor cost = NNP at market price −
net indirect taxes**.

**Derived measures:** Personal Income = NI − undistributed profits − corporate taxes − social security
contributions + transfer payments. Disposable Personal Income = PI − direct taxes = Consumption +
Saving.

**Real vs nominal:** real GDP values output at base-year prices; **GDP deflator = (nominal ÷ real) ×
100** (100 in the base year, above 100 with inflation). **Per capita income = National Income ÷
Population** — an average that hides distribution.

**Circular flow:** value of output = income paid out = expenditure received. Leakages (saving, taxes,
imports) = injections (investment, government spending, exports) in equilibrium.

**The three methods (all give the same GDP):** value added (Σ value added, no double counting); income
(rent + wages + interest + profit + mixed income); expenditure (**C + I + G + (X − M)**). Common
precautions: no double counting, no transfer payments, no second-hand goods or financial transactions.

**Welfare limits:** national income ignores distribution, non-market production, the composition of
output, externalities and leisure — a necessary but not sufficient welfare signal.

**Keynesian determination:** income is fixed by aggregate demand (= C + I + …) with idle resources.
**Consumption function C = a + bY**; APC = C ÷ Y, MPC = ΔC ÷ ΔY = b. **Saving S = −a + (1 − b)Y**;
APS = S ÷ Y, MPS = ΔS ÷ ΔY. **MPC + MPS = 1** and **APC + APS = 1**.

**Equilibrium:** Y = C + I, equivalently **S = I**. **Investment multiplier k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 1 ÷
MPS**, and **ΔY = k × ΔI**; a larger MPC gives a larger multiplier.

**Paradox of thrift:** if all households try to save more at once, aggregate demand falls, income falls
through the multiplier, and total saving need not rise.

**Marking:** 1 mark correct, −0.25 wrong, 0 unattempted. Guessing among four is close to neutral;
eliminating even one option makes an attempt clearly worthwhile.
