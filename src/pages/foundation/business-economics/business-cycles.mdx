For three years a Tier-2 city on the edge of a metro cannot build fast enough. Cranes stand over every
plot, cement and steel are hard to get, masons name their own wages, and a flat booked on paper sells
for a profit before a single wall goes up. Then, almost without warning, the buyers thin out. Unsold
flats pile up, builders stop new projects, the sand lorries fall idle, and the masons who last year
turned down work now sit at the labour chowk with nothing to do. Nothing about the buildings changed.
The same economy that raced ahead is now stalled, and in a year or two, as prices settle and cheap
loans return, it will begin to climb again. That rise, that fall and that slow return are one turn of
a **business cycle** — the wave-like swing of a whole economy through good times and bad.

Paper 4 is an objective paper, and this chapter is almost entirely conceptual — there is nothing to
compute. The marks are won by holding four things apart and matching them cleanly: the **name of each
phase** against the state of the economy in it, a **feature** (rising prices, mass unemployment,
credit contraction) against the phase where it belongs, a **turning point** (peak or trough) against
its place on the wave, and — the examiner's favourite — a **theory** against the **economist** who
gave it. Learn the phase order, the six things that move in each phase, and the theory-to-name table,
and the chapter is yours.

## 1. Meaning and definition of a business cycle

A **business cycle**, also called a **trade cycle**, is the recurring **wave-like fluctuation in the
overall level of economic activity** — in aggregate output, income, employment and prices — that an
economy passes through over a period of years. Activity does not grow along a smooth straight line; it
rises for a stretch, reaches a peak, turns down, falls to a low, and then recovers, over and over.

Two features are built into the definition and are worth fixing at the start.

- It is a movement in **aggregate** activity — the economy as a whole, not one firm or one industry.
  A single shop closing is not a business cycle; a general fall in national output, employment and
  income is. This is why the business cycle belongs to **macroeconomics**.
- It is **cyclical and recurring** — the up-and-down pattern repeats. But, as Section 5 stresses, it
  repeats **irregularly**: the cycles are not of equal length or equal depth, so a business cycle is
  recurrent without being strictly periodic like the swing of a clock's pendulum.

A useful working definition, in the spirit of the standard texts, is this: a business cycle is a wave
of **alternating periods of prosperity and depression** in economic activity, spreading across the
whole economy and repeating over time, though not at fixed intervals.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The examiner's opening question usually tests one word: a business cycle is a fluctuation in
    <strong>aggregate</strong> (economy-wide) activity, not in a single firm or a single price. If an
    option describes "the seasonal rise in umbrella sales" or "one company's falling profit", it is
    the wrong scale. The right answer speaks of national output, income, employment and the general
    price level moving together.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. The four phases of a business cycle

A full business cycle passes through **four phases**, in a fixed order, and then begins again. Named
from the bottom of one cycle round to the bottom of the next, they are:

1. **Expansion** — also called **prosperity**, **boom** or **upswing**. Activity rises: output,
   employment, income, prices and profits all climb, investment grows and credit expands. The phase
   ends at its highest point, the **peak** (or **boom**).
2. **Recession** — also called **contraction** or the **downturn**. From the peak, activity turns
   down: orders fall, firms cut production and investment, employment and incomes begin to shrink, and
   optimism gives way to pessimism. A recession can begin suddenly and gather pace.
3. **Depression** — also called the **trough** or **slump**. Activity reaches its **lowest** level:
   output and employment are at their minimum, there is heavy unemployment and idle capacity, prices
   and profits are low, and investment is at its weakest. The lowest point is the **trough**.
4. **Recovery** — also called **revival** or the **upturn**. From the trough, activity turns up again:
   investment revives, employment and income begin to rise, prices firm up, credit expands and
   confidence returns — carrying the economy back toward expansion, where the cycle repeats.

The order is what matters. Read round the wave: **recovery → expansion (up to the peak) → recession →
depression (down to the trough) → recovery** again. The two **turning points** are the **peak** at the
top (where expansion turns into recession) and the **trough** at the bottom (where depression turns
into recovery); Section 4 treats them on their own.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Do not muddle <strong>recession</strong> with <strong>depression</strong>. A recession is the
    <em>downward phase</em> — the economy sliding <em>from</em> the peak, still above the bottom. A
    depression is the <em>bottom itself</em> — activity at its <strong>lowest</strong>, with mass
    unemployment and idle plant. Recession is the journey down; depression is the pit at the end of
    it. The same trap swaps <strong>recovery</strong> (rising from the trough) with expansion; recovery
    is the early climb, expansion the fuller boom that follows.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. Features of the four phases

Each phase has a recognisable signature in six variables the examiner loves to test — **output,
employment, prices, investment, profits and credit**. Learn the direction of each in each phase and
most feature-matching questions answer themselves.

| Variable | Expansion / boom | Recession | Depression / trough | Recovery / revival |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Output** | rising to a peak, near full capacity | falling | lowest, heavy idle capacity | rising from the low |
| **Employment** | rising toward full employment | falling | lowest, mass unemployment | rising |
| **Prices** | rising (inflationary) | falling | lowest / deflated | beginning to rise |
| **Investment** | high and rising | falling sharply | lowest | reviving |
| **Profits** | high | shrinking | lowest, often losses | recovering |
| **Credit / bank lending** | expanding freely (easy credit) | contracting | tight, cautious lending | expanding again |
| **Business mood** | optimism, confidence | growing pessimism | deep pessimism | returning confidence |

A few details behind the table are examined directly.

- **At the peak (boom),** the economy runs at or near full capacity. Bottlenecks appear — shortages of
  labour, raw materials and finance — costs and prices rise fast, and the very strains that mark the
  boom prepare its end. A boom cannot expand for ever, because resources run out.
- **In recession,** the turn can be **abrupt**: cancelled orders, falling asset prices and a loss of
  confidence feed on one another, banks grow cautious and call in loans, and the fall gathers speed.
- **In depression,** output and employment sit at their lowest; there is widespread unemployment, plant
  lies idle, prices and profits are depressed and many firms make losses. Interest rates and prices are
  low, but pessimism keeps investment down even so.
- **In recovery,** something lifts confidence — cheaper credit, replacement of worn-out equipment, a
  revival of demand — and investment starts again. Once it does, rising employment and income feed back
  into more spending, and the upturn builds toward the next boom.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Match the extreme to the phase. <strong>Highest</strong> output, employment, prices and profits sit
    at the <strong>peak (boom)</strong>; <strong>lowest</strong> of all four sit at the
    <strong>trough (depression)</strong>. The two <em>moving</em> phases are named for their direction:
    <strong>recession</strong> is the fall from the peak, <strong>recovery</strong> the rise from the
    trough. If a stem says "unemployment is at its maximum and firms are making losses", the phase is
    depression; if it says "the economy is running at full capacity with rising prices", it is the boom.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Turning points: the peak and the trough

Between the rising phases and the falling phases sit two **turning points**, where the direction of
the whole economy reverses.

- The **peak**, or **upper turning point**, is the **top** of the cycle — the highest level of activity,
  where **expansion ends and recession begins**. At the peak the economy is at or near full capacity;
  further growth is blocked by shortages of labour, materials and finance, so the boom stalls and turns
  down. The peak is also loosely called the **boom**.
- The **trough**, or **lower turning point**, is the **bottom** of the cycle — the lowest level of
  activity, where **depression ends and recovery begins**. At the trough output, employment, prices and
  investment are at their lowest, but the fall cannot go on for ever: worn-out machines must eventually
  be replaced, stocks run down, and cheap credit and low costs at last coax investment back, turning the
  economy up.

The two turning points frame the cycle. Everything between the trough and the next peak is the rising
half (recovery then expansion); everything between the peak and the next trough is the falling half
(recession then depression). Naming which turning point a described economy is at — "activity has
stopped rising and just begun to fall" is the **peak**; "activity has stopped falling and just begun to
rise" is the **trough** — is a standard one-line question.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The <strong>peak</strong> is not "the middle of the boom" and the <strong>trough</strong> is not
    "the middle of the depression" — each is a <em>turning point</em>, the exact place where the
    direction reverses. The peak is where the <strong>rise turns into a fall</strong> (expansion →
    recession); the trough is where the <strong>fall turns into a rise</strong> (depression →
    recovery). An option that calls the peak the start of expansion, or the trough the start of
    recession, has the turn backwards.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Characteristics of business cycles

Beyond the four phases, business cycles share a set of **characteristics** that the paper tests as a
group. Hold them as a short list.

- **Recurrent but not periodic.** Cycles repeat again and again, but **not at fixed intervals**. Their
  length, depth and force all vary — one cycle may run a few years, another much longer; one downturn is
  mild, the next severe. So a business cycle recurs without the clockwork regularity of, say, the
  seasons. This is the single most tested characteristic.
- **Synchronous and pervasive.** A cycle does **not** stay in one industry. Once it starts it spreads
  across the whole economy, and the different sectors move **together** — the synchronism the economist
  Wesley Mitchell stressed. Prosperity in one part of the economy pulls the rest up with it, and a
  downturn drags the rest down.
- **Wave-like and cumulative.** Activity moves in **waves** — up, over, down, under — not in jumps. And
  each movement is **self-reinforcing (cumulative)**: once an upswing starts, rising incomes create more
  demand, which raises output and incomes again, so the boom feeds on itself; the same reinforcing logic
  drives a downswing deeper. A cycle, once under way, tends to gather its own momentum.
- **International in scope.** Cycles are **transmitted from country to country** through trade and
  capital flows. A slump in a large economy cuts its imports, which hits the exports and incomes of its
  trading partners, spreading the downturn abroad. Modern cycles are rarely confined to one nation.
- **All sectors affected, but unequally.** A cycle touches every sector, yet not to the same degree.
  **Capital-goods (producer-goods) and durable-goods industries swing far more violently** than
  consumer-goods, especially non-durable, industries. Section 9 and Worked example 6 explain *why*; the
  fact to carry is that the makers of machines, steel, cement and consumer durables feel the boom and
  the slump most sharply, while the makers of food, soap and everyday necessities feel them least.
- **Complex and self-generating.** A cycle is not caused by one thing alone, and it does not need a
  fresh outside shock for every turn: the forces that build a boom also sow the recession, and the
  depression itself prepares the recovery, so the economy tends to move from one phase to the next of
  its own accord.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Two characteristics are asked most often. First, cycles are <strong>recurrent but not
    periodic</strong> — they repeat but not at equal intervals or with equal force; an option calling
    them "regular and of fixed duration" is wrong. Second, they are <strong>synchronous</strong> — they
    spread across and move all sectors together, so an option saying a cycle "stays within a single
    industry" is wrong. Pair these two with "capital-goods industries fluctuate more than consumer-goods
    industries" and you cover most characteristic questions.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. Causes: external (exogenous) factors

What sets a business cycle going? The explanations fall into two families. **External (exogenous)
factors** lie **outside the economic system** — shocks that hit the economy from the environment,
technology or politics and set the wave in motion.

- **Wars and political upheavals.** A war diverts resources to armaments, drives up spending and prices,
  and creates a wartime boom; its end brings a sharp fall in demand and a post-war slump. Political
  shocks and policy shifts disturb activity the same way.
- **Technological change and innovation.** A cluster of major inventions — the railway, electricity, the
  computer — sets off a wave of new investment and expansion; when the wave of investment is spent,
  activity falls back. This is the seed of **Schumpeter's innovations theory** (Section 8), which treats
  bursts of innovation as the mainspring of the cycle.
- **Population growth.** A faster-growing population raises demand, investment and activity; a slowing of
  population growth can weaken them. Long swings in population feed into long swings in the economy.
- **Weather, climate and sunspots.** Harvests depend on the weather, and in a farm-based economy a good
  or bad harvest ripples through incomes, demand and trade. The economist **W. S. Jevons** pushed this
  furthest with his **sunspot theory**, linking cycles in sunspot activity to cycles in weather, then in
  harvests, and so to cycles in trade. The mechanism is now treated as a historical curiosity, but the
  name-to-theory link — **Jevons = sunspots / weather** — is squarely examinable.
- **Gold discoveries and other shocks.** A new supply of gold (in a gold-based monetary system) or other
  outside disturbances can expand money and spending and start an upswing.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Keep the two families straight. <strong>External (exogenous)</strong> causes come from
    <em>outside</em> the economic machine — wars, weather and sunspots, population, new technology.
    <strong>Internal (endogenous)</strong> causes (Section 7) arise <em>within</em> it — swings in
    investment, credit and business expectations. Jevons's sunspots and Schumpeter's innovations are
    <strong>external</strong> shocks; Hawtrey's credit swings and Keynes's investment fluctuations are
    <strong>internal</strong>. Sorting a listed cause into the right family is a common one-line
    question.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. Causes: internal (endogenous) factors

**Internal (endogenous) factors** arise **within the economic system** itself. On this view the economy
carries the seeds of its own fluctuations: the very forces that build a boom eventually bring it to an
end, and the depression in turn sets up the recovery, with no outside shock needed for each turn.

- **Fluctuations in investment and effective demand.** The most important internal cause. Investment is
  volatile — it depends on businesses' expected profit (the marginal efficiency of capital) and on
  confidence, both of which swing widely. A rise in investment raises income and demand and lifts the
  economy; a fall in investment drags it down. This is the heart of the **Keynesian** explanation
  (Section 8): cycles are driven by changes in **aggregate (effective) demand**, led by investment.
- **Expansion and contraction of bank credit.** When banks lend freely, money and spending grow and the
  economy booms; when they grow cautious and call in loans, spending falls and the economy slumps. Swings
  in the supply of **money and credit** can drive the whole cycle — the core of **Hawtrey's monetary
  theory**.
- **Over-investment.** During an upswing, easy credit and optimism can push investment in capital goods
  **beyond** what consumer demand can sustain. The over-built capacity cannot be used profitably,
  investment collapses, and the boom breaks — the **over-investment theory** associated with **Hayek**.
- **Waves of optimism and pessimism.** Business decisions rest on **expectations**, and expectations
  swing between over-optimism in good times (which over-extends the boom) and over-pessimism in bad
  (which deepens the slump). This psychological element is stressed in **Pigou's** account.
- **The interaction of the multiplier and the accelerator.** A change in investment raises income by a
  multiple (the **multiplier**); rising income and demand in turn call forth new investment (the
  **accelerator**); and the two, feeding on each other, can generate a self-sustaining up-and-down cycle
  without any outside push — the **Samuelson–Hicks** model (Section 8).
- **Under-consumption / over-saving.** If households save too much and spend too little, demand falls
  short of the output the economy can produce, and the resulting glut brings on a downturn.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The exam does not ask you to weigh these theories — it asks you to <strong>sort a cause into
    internal or external</strong> and to <strong>attach a theory to its economist</strong> (Section 8).
    Fix the anchor idea for each internal cause: investment / effective demand → <strong>Keynes</strong>;
    money and credit → <strong>Hawtrey</strong>; over-investment in capital goods → <strong>Hayek</strong>;
    optimism and pessimism → <strong>Pigou</strong>; multiplier feeding the accelerator →
    <strong>Samuelson and Hicks</strong>.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. Theories of the business cycle and their economists

The single most reliable source of marks in this chapter is the **theory-to-economist** match. Each
theory picks out one force as the mainspring of the cycle. Learn the table, then the traps.

| Theory | Proponent(s) | Core idea — the mainspring of the cycle |
|---|---|---|
| **Monetary (over-expansion of credit) theory** | **R. G. Hawtrey** | The cycle is a purely **monetary** phenomenon: expansion of **bank credit and money** drives the boom, contraction of credit brings the slump. |
| **Innovations theory** | **J. A. Schumpeter** | Cycles are set off by **clusters of innovations** by entrepreneurs; a wave of new investment lifts the economy, and its exhaustion brings the downturn. |
| **Over-investment theory** | **F. A. Hayek** | Easy credit and optimism push **investment in capital goods beyond** what consumption can sustain; the over-built capacity collapses and ends the boom. |
| **Under-consumption theory** | **Hobson, Malthus** | Too much **saving and too little consumption** leave demand short of output; the resulting glut triggers the downswing. |
| **Psychological theory** | **A. C. Pigou** | Waves of business **optimism and pessimism** — swings in expectations — over-extend the boom and deepen the slump. |
| **Effective-demand / investment theory** | **J. M. Keynes** | Cycles are driven by fluctuations in **aggregate (effective) demand**, led by volatile **investment** (the marginal efficiency of capital and expectations). |
| **Sunspot (weather) theory** | **W. S. Jevons** | Cycles in **sunspots** cause cycles in weather and harvests, and so in agricultural incomes and trade — an **external** cause. |
| **Multiplier–accelerator interaction** | **P. A. Samuelson and J. R. Hicks** | The **multiplier** (investment raising income) and the **accelerator** (rising income inducing investment) feed on each other to generate a self-sustaining cycle. |

Three attributions are mixed up most often, so fix them hard: **money and credit is Hawtrey**;
**over-investment is Hayek**; **psychology and expectations is Pigou**. Add that **Keynes** puts
**effective demand and investment** at the centre, **Jevons** is the odd-one-out **external** sunspot
theory, **Schumpeter** is **innovations**, and the **multiplier-accelerator** pairing is
**Samuelson and Hicks**, and the table is complete.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The classic trap swaps <strong>Hawtrey</strong> and <strong>Hayek</strong> — the two names even look
    alike. <strong>Hawtrey = the monetary theory</strong> (bank credit and money); <strong>Hayek = the
    over-investment theory</strong> (too much investment in capital goods). A memory hook: Haw<strong>t</strong>rey
    for the <strong>t</strong>ill (money), Ha<strong>y</strong>ek for the over-buildin<strong>g</strong>
    of capital. And do not give the psychological theory to Keynes: expectations and the marginal
    efficiency of capital matter to Keynes, but the theory built squarely on <strong>optimism and
    pessimism</strong> is <strong>Pigou's</strong>.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Relevance of business cycles to business decision-making

Business cycles are not just a topic in theory; they shape almost every decision a firm makes, because
a firm that reads the cycle wrongly buys, builds and hires at the worst possible time. The relevance
runs through the main decision areas.

- **Demand forecasting and production planning.** Because demand rises in the boom and falls in the
  slump, a firm must forecast where the cycle is heading before it fixes output and stocks. Producing at
  full tilt just as a recession begins leaves it with unsold inventory; holding back in an early recovery
  loses sales to rivals.
- **Investment and capacity decisions.** Adding a plant is a long commitment made on a view of the
  future. The wise time to plan capacity is ahead of a recovery, not at the top of a boom when costs are
  highest and a downturn is near. Misreading the peak as permanent is how firms end up with idle capacity
  in the slump.
- **Inventory management.** Stocks built cheaply before an upswing pay off; stocks piled up at the peak
  become a burden when prices fall in the recession.
- **Pricing and credit policy.** Pricing power and easy credit in the boom give way to price-cutting and
  tight credit in the slump, so pricing, discounts and the terms a firm offers and accepts must move with
  the cycle.
- **Financing and cash management.** Borrowing is cheap and easy in the boom and dear and scarce in the
  slump. A firm that over-borrows at the peak can be caught when credit tightens, so prudent firms keep
  their finances strong enough to ride out a downturn.
- **Employment and cost control.** Hiring races ahead in the boom and wages rise; the slump forces
  retrenchment. Managing the workforce and fixed costs across the cycle protects the firm when demand
  falls.
- **Diversification and risk management.** Because capital-goods and durable industries swing hardest, a
  firm exposed to them may spread into steadier consumer-necessity lines to soften the cycle's blow.

The through-line is timing: business economics uses knowledge of the cycle to help managers **buy,
build, borrow and hire counter to the cycle rather than with it** — cautiously near the peak, boldly
near the trough — so that the firm is not carried up and then dashed down by a wave it did not see.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A relevance question usually describes a decision and asks what cycle-awareness tells the manager.
    The safe answer is nearly always about <strong>timing against the cycle</strong>: do not add
    capacity or pile up stock at the <strong>peak</strong>, and do prepare to invest and hire as the
    <strong>trough</strong> gives way to recovery. Firms in <strong>capital-goods and durable</strong>
    lines must plan most carefully, because their demand swings most.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. Negative marking and when to leave a question

Paper 4 is entirely objective and carries **negative marking of 0.25 mark for each wrong answer**. A
correct answer earns 1 mark, a wrong one costs 0.25, and an unattempted one scores 0.

The arithmetic is the same as for any objective paper. A blind guess among four options is right one
time in four: across four such guesses you expect 1 mark gained and 3 &times; 0.25 = 0.75 lost, a net
of 0.25 marks for four questions — very slightly positive, but with a wide spread. What changes the
picture is **elimination**. Rule out one option and a guess among the remaining three is worth about
0.5 marks per four attempts; rule out two and a guess between the last two is worth about 1.5 marks per
four.

This chapter is unusually kind to elimination, because most distractors are built from a **named
confusion** — recession swapped with depression, the peak read as the start of expansion, the monetary
theory pinned on Hayek instead of Hawtrey. A phase order you know, and a theory-to-name table you have
learned, let you strike two options on sight, and the guess that remains is clearly worth making.

A calm working rule: **answer whenever you can eliminate at least one option, and consider leaving a
question only when you cannot narrow the field at all.** The marks at stake either way are small, so
nothing here needs to be done anxiously.

## Fully worked examples

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — naming the phase from its description (easy)">
  <p>
    In an economy, factories run at full capacity, unemployment is very low, prices and profits are
    rising fast, banks are lending freely and businesspeople are highly optimistic — but shortages of
    labour and raw materials are starting to bite. Which phase is this, and what comes next?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — read the signature of the phase</div>
    Full capacity, low unemployment, rising prices and profits, easy credit and optimism are the marks
    of <strong>expansion at its peak — the boom</strong>.<br />
    The bottleneck detail is the clue to what follows: shortages of labour, materials and finance mean
    the economy cannot grow further, so the boom stalls at the <strong>peak (upper turning point)</strong>
    and the next phase is a <strong>recession</strong>. A boom cannot last for ever precisely because
    resources run out.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — recession versus depression (easy-moderate)">
  <p>
    A student writes: "When output first starts to fall from the top of the cycle, the economy is in a
    depression." Correct the statement and name the phase properly.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — the fall is not yet the bottom</div>
    Output first falling <em>from the peak</em> is a <strong>recession (contraction)</strong> — the
    <em>downward phase</em>, still above the bottom.<br />
    A <strong>depression</strong> is the <em>bottom itself</em>: activity at its <strong>lowest</strong>,
    with mass unemployment, idle plant, low prices and losses. Recession is the journey down; depression
    is the pit at the end of it. The correct sentence is: "When output first starts to fall from the
    peak, the economy is in a <strong>recession</strong>."
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — placing the turning points (moderate)">
  <p>
    On a business-cycle wave, activity has been falling for many months, unemployment is at its highest,
    and now the fall has just stopped and the first signs of a pick-up appear. Name the turning point the
    economy is at, and name the phase that follows it.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — a turning point is where direction reverses</div>
    The lowest level of activity, where a long fall has just ended and a rise is about to begin, is the
    <strong>trough (lower turning point)</strong> — the point where <strong>depression turns into
    recovery</strong>.<br />
    The phase that follows is <strong>recovery (revival)</strong>: investment starts again, employment
    and income rise, prices firm up and credit expands, carrying the economy back toward expansion.
    (The mirror-image turning point, where a rise ends and a fall begins, is the <strong>peak</strong>.)
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — sorting causes into external and internal (moderate)">
  <p>
    Classify each supposed cause of the business cycle as external (exogenous) or internal (endogenous):
    (a) a war; (b) a swing in bank credit; (c) a burst of new inventions; (d) waves of business optimism
    and pessimism; (e) sunspot-driven changes in harvests.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — inside or outside the economic machine?</div>
    (a) A war strikes the economy from <em>outside</em> → <strong>external</strong>.<br />
    (b) A swing in bank credit arises <em>within</em> the economic system → <strong>internal</strong>.<br />
    (c) New inventions are a shock from technology, outside the system → <strong>external</strong>
    (Schumpeter's innovations).<br />
    (d) Optimism and pessimism are generated <em>within</em> the economy → <strong>internal</strong>
    (Pigou's psychological theory).<br />
    (e) Sunspots and weather are outside the economy → <strong>external</strong> (Jevons's sunspot
    theory).<br />
    The test each time: does the cause come from <em>outside</em> the economic machine (war, weather,
    technology, population) or is it generated <em>within</em> it (investment, credit, expectations)?
  </div>
</Callout>

  <p>
    Match each theory of the business cycle to the economist most associated with it: (a) the cycle is a
    purely monetary matter of expanding and contracting bank credit; (b) the cycle comes from
    over-investment in capital goods beyond what consumption can support; (c) the cycle is driven by
    waves of optimism and pessimism; (d) the cycle arises from the interaction of the multiplier and the

  </p>

    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — anchor idea to name</div>
    (a) Money and credit → <strong>R. G. Hawtrey</strong> (monetary theory).<br />
    (b) Over-investment in capital goods → <strong>F. A. Hayek</strong> (over-investment theory).<br />
    (c) Optimism and pessimism → <strong>A. C. Pigou</strong> (psychological theory).<br />
    (d) Multiplier feeding the accelerator → <strong>Samuelson and Hicks</strong>.<br />
    The two names to keep apart are <strong>Hawtrey (money)</strong> and <strong>Hayek

    the external <em>sunspot</em> theory, and Schumpeter to <em>innovations</em>.
  </div>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 6 — why capital-goods industries swing more (hard)">
  <p>
    In a slump, an order-book for new machine tools may collapse by half while sales of soap and salt
    barely move. Explain why capital-goods and durable-goods industries fluctuate far more over the cycle
    than consumer-necessity industries.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — postponable demand and the accelerator</div>
    Consumer necessities — food, soap, salt — are bought in much the same quantity whatever the phase,
    because people must eat and wash even in a slump. Their demand is steady, so their output barely
    swings.<br />
    Capital goods and durables are different in two ways. First, their purchase is
    <strong>postponable</strong>: a firm can run an old machine a little longer, and a household can make
    last year's fridge do, so in a slump these purchases are simply put off — and rush back in the
    upswing. Second, the <strong>accelerator</strong> magnifies the swing: because investment responds to
    the <em>change</em> in consumer demand, even a small dip in consumption can turn the demand for new
    capital goods sharply negative.<br />
    Together, postponable demand and the accelerator make the makers of machines, steel, cement and
    consumer durables feel the boom and the slump most violently, while the makers of everyday
    necessities feel them least. This is exactly the "all sectors affected, but unequally" characteristic
    of Section 5.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 7 — using the cycle in a business decision (moderate)">
  <p>
    A cement company is deciding whether to commission a large new plant. Activity has been booming for
    three years: demand and prices are at record highs, credit is cheap, and shortages of labour and
    materials are appearing. What does business-cycle awareness tell the management, and why?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — read the phase, then time the decision</div>
    The signs — record demand and prices, easy credit, and emerging shortages of labour and materials —
    say the economy is at or near the <strong>peak (boom)</strong>, the phase that a
    <strong>recession</strong> follows.<br />
    Cement is a <strong>capital-goods-linked, cyclical</strong> product whose demand swings most sharply
    over the cycle. Commissioning a large plant at the top of the boom means building at the highest
    costs, just as demand is about to fall — the plant would come on stream into a recession and stand
    partly idle.<br />
    Cycle awareness therefore counsels <strong>caution near the peak</strong>: delay or scale back the
    expansion, keep finances strong for the coming downturn, and plan to invest instead as the
    <strong>trough</strong> gives way to recovery, when costs are low and demand is about to rise. The
    lesson is to act <em>counter</em> to the cycle, not with it.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes and examiner traps

- **Recession versus depression.** Recession is the *downward phase* (activity falling from the peak);
  depression is the *bottom* (activity at its lowest, mass unemployment, idle plant). Recession is the
  journey down; depression is the pit.
- **Recovery versus expansion.** Recovery is the *early climb* from the trough; expansion (boom) is the
  fuller rise that follows. Both are upward phases, but recovery comes first.
- **The turning points reversed.** The peak is where the *rise turns into a fall* (expansion →
  recession); the trough is where the *fall turns into a rise* (depression → recovery). Do not call the
  peak the start of expansion or the trough the start of recession.
- **"Periodic" instead of "recurrent".** Cycles are recurrent but *not* periodic — they repeat, but not
  at fixed intervals or with equal depth. "Regular and of fixed duration" is the wrong description.
- **Thinking a cycle stays in one industry.** Cycles are *synchronous and pervasive* — they spread
  across the whole economy and move all sectors together.
- **All sectors affected equally.** They are not: *capital-goods and durable-goods* industries swing far
  more than *consumer-necessity* industries, because their demand is postponable and the accelerator
  magnifies it.
- **External versus internal causes.** Wars, weather/sunspots, population and technology are *external
  (exogenous)*; investment, credit and expectations are *internal (endogenous)*. Jevons's sunspots and
  Schumpeter's innovations are external; Hawtrey's credit and Keynes's investment are internal.
- **Hawtrey versus Hayek.** Hawtrey = the *monetary* theory (bank credit and money); Hayek = the
  *over-investment* theory (too much investment in capital goods). The lookalike names are a deliberate
  trap.
- **The psychological theory given to Keynes.** Optimism and pessimism is *Pigou's* theory. Keynes puts
  *effective demand and investment* at the centre; Samuelson and Hicks give the
  *multiplier-accelerator* interaction.

## One-page revision summary

**Business (trade) cycle** = recurring, wave-like fluctuation in *aggregate* economic activity —
output, income, employment and prices — over a period of years. A macroeconomic, economy-wide
phenomenon, recurrent but not strictly periodic.

**The four phases (in order):** **recovery → expansion/boom (up to the peak) → recession → depression/
trough → recovery** again. Expansion = activity rising; **peak** = the top; recession = activity falling
from the peak; **trough** = the bottom (depression); recovery = activity rising from the trough.

**Features by phase** (output, employment, prices, investment, profits, credit): all *highest* at the
**peak/boom** (full capacity, rising prices, easy credit, optimism); all *lowest* at the
**trough/depression** (mass unemployment, idle plant, low prices, losses, tight credit, pessimism).
Recession = all falling; recovery = all rising.

**Turning points:** the **peak (upper turning point)** — expansion turns into recession; the **trough
(lower turning point)** — depression turns into recovery.

**Characteristics:** recurrent **but not periodic**; **synchronous and pervasive** (spread across and
move all sectors together); **wave-like and self-reinforcing (cumulative)**; **international in scope**
(transmitted through trade); **all sectors affected but unequally** — *capital-goods and durable*
industries swing more than *consumer-necessity* industries; complex and largely self-generating.

**Causes — external (exogenous):** wars, technological innovation, population growth, weather/sunspots
(**Jevons**), gold discoveries. **Internal (endogenous):** fluctuations in **investment / effective
demand** (**Keynes**), expansion and contraction of **bank credit** (**Hawtrey**), **over-investment**
in capital goods (**Hayek**), **optimism and pessimism** (**Pigou**), the **multiplier-accelerator**
interaction (**Samuelson and Hicks**), under-consumption / over-saving.

**Theory-to-economist:** monetary = **Hawtrey**; over-investment = **Hayek**; psychological = **Pigou**;
effective demand / investment = **Keynes**; sunspot/weather = **Jevons**; innovations = **Schumpeter**;
multiplier-accelerator = **Samuelson and Hicks**; under-consumption = **Hobson/Malthus**. Keep
**Hawtrey (money)** and **Hayek (over-investment)** apart.

**Relevance to business decisions:** cycle awareness guides *timing against the cycle* — demand
forecasting, investment and capacity, inventory, pricing and credit, financing, employment, and
diversification. Be cautious near the **peak**; prepare to invest and hire as the **trough** turns to
recovery. Capital-goods and durable firms must plan most carefully.

**Marking:** 1 mark correct, −0.25 wrong, 0 unattempted. Guessing among four is close to neutral;
eliminating even one option makes an attempt clearly worthwhile.
