Every time a tanker of crude oil docks at an Indian port, two prices decide what the fuel will cost at
the pump: the dollar price of the oil, and the number of rupees it takes to buy one dollar. India buys
most of its crude from abroad and pays for it in dollars, so when the rupee weakens from ₹ 80 to a
dollar to ₹ 85, the same barrel costs more rupees even if its dollar price has not moved. On the other
side of the same coin, an Indian software firm that bills a client in New York in dollars now earns
**more** rupees for each dollar it brings home. One exchange-rate movement helps the exporter and hurts
the importer at the same instant. This chapter is the careful account of why countries trade at all,
what governments do to help or hinder that trade, and how the exchange rate — the price that links two
economies — is set and what it does.

Paper 4 is an objective paper, and this chapter carries two kinds of question. Most test whether you
can state a theory cleanly, tell a tariff from a quota, name a principle of the WTO, or say which way a
depreciation pushes exports. A smaller but reliable set asks you to **compute** — an opportunity-cost
ratio to find who has the comparative advantage, a rupee amount converted at a given rate, a cross rate
from two quotes, the effect of a tariff on a price, or a balance-of-trade or current-account figure from
a small table. The computations are short. The marks are lost on confusing absolute with comparative
advantage, on mixing up depreciation with devaluation, on thinking appreciation helps exporters, and on
counting services inside the balance of **trade**, which holds only goods.

## 1. Why countries trade, and absolute advantage

No country makes everything it uses, and none would want to. Countries trade because they differ — in
climate, soil, minerals, skills, technology and the quantity of labour and capital they hold. Trade lets
each country concentrate on what it does relatively well, sell the surplus abroad, and buy from others
the things it makes poorly or cannot make at all. The result is that the world produces **more** from the
same resources, and every trading country can consume beyond what it could produce alone.

The first clear statement of this gain came from **Adam Smith** in 1776, in the **theory of absolute
advantage**. A country has an **absolute advantage** in a good when it can produce **more** of that good
than another country from the **same** resources — equivalently, when it can make one unit of the good
using **fewer** resources. Smith's argument was simple: if country A is the more efficient producer of
wheat and country B the more efficient producer of cloth, then A should make all the wheat, B should make
all the cloth, and the two should trade. Each specialises where it is the better producer, total output
rises, and both gain.

<div class="formula">A country has an absolute advantage in a good if it produces more of it from the same resources (uses fewer resources per unit)</div>

Consider two countries and two goods, with the output that one unit of resources can produce in each:

| Country | Shirts (per unit of resources) | Rice, kg (per unit of resources) |
|---|---|---|
| India | 80 | 40 |
| Vietnam | 60 | 60 |

India makes more shirts than Vietnam (80 against 60), so India has the **absolute advantage in shirts**.
Vietnam makes more rice (60 against 40), so Vietnam has the **absolute advantage in rice**. Smith's rule
tells India to specialise in shirts, Vietnam in rice, and the two to trade — and both end up with more of
both goods than if each had tried to make its own.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Absolute advantage is about producing <strong>more</strong> from the same resources (or using fewer
    resources per unit). Read the table the right way: if it gives <em>output per unit of resources</em>,
    the country with the <strong>bigger</strong> number has the absolute advantage; if it gives
    <em>resources needed per unit of output</em>, the country with the <strong>smaller</strong> number
    does. Examiners flip between the two presentations on purpose.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. Comparative advantage and the gains from trade

Smith's theory had a gap. What if one country is better at making **both** goods — has the absolute
advantage in everything? On Smith's rule the weaker country would have nothing to sell, and there would be
no trade. **David Ricardo** closed the gap in 1817 with the **theory of comparative advantage**, the most
important idea in the whole chapter. Ricardo showed that trade still benefits both countries even when one
is more efficient at everything, **provided the degree of its advantage differs between the two goods**.
What matters is not who is better in absolute terms, but who gives up **less** to make a good — the
**opportunity cost**.

The **opportunity cost** of a good is the quantity of the **other** good a country must give up to produce
one more unit of it. A country has a **comparative advantage** in the good it can produce at the **lower
opportunity cost**. Each country should specialise in the good in which its opportunity cost is lower, and
trade for the rest.

<div class="formula">Opportunity cost of 1 unit of good X (in terms of good Y) = units of Y given up ÷ units of X gained</div>

Take the same two countries. From one unit of resources India makes 80 shirts **or** 40 kg of rice, and
Vietnam makes 60 shirts **or** 60 kg of rice. Work out what each gives up.

| | India | Vietnam |
|---|---|---|
| Opportunity cost of **1 shirt** (kg of rice given up) | 40 ÷ 80 = **0.5 kg rice** | 60 ÷ 60 = **1 kg rice** |
| Opportunity cost of **1 kg rice** (shirts given up) | 80 ÷ 40 = **2 shirts** | 60 ÷ 60 = **1 shirt** |

India gives up only 0.5 kg of rice for a shirt, while Vietnam gives up a full kilogram — so **India has
the comparative advantage in shirts**. Vietnam gives up only 1 shirt for a kilogram of rice, while India
gives up 2 shirts — so **Vietnam has the comparative advantage in rice**. Notice how the two comparative
advantages must fall on **opposite** goods: if one country's opportunity cost is lower in shirts, the same
country's opportunity cost is necessarily higher in rice.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — the gain from specialising</div>
  Before trade, suppose each country splits its resources and India makes 40 shirts + 20 kg rice, Vietnam
  makes 30 shirts + 30 kg rice — a world total of 70 shirts and 50 kg rice.<br />
  Let each specialise: India puts everything into shirts (80 shirts, 0 rice); Vietnam puts everything into
  rice (0 shirts, 60 kg rice) — a world total of <strong>80 shirts and 60 kg rice</strong>.<br />
  Specialisation produced <strong>10 more shirts and 10 more kg of rice</strong> from the same resources.
  That extra output is the gain from trade, and the two countries share it through the price at which they
  exchange shirts for rice.
</div>

The **gains from trade** are shared only if the exchange ratio between the two goods settles **between**
the two countries' opportunity-cost ratios. India will not trade a shirt for less than 0.5 kg of rice
(that is what a shirt costs it at home); Vietnam will not give more than 1 kg of rice for a shirt (its own
cost). Any ratio between 0.5 and 1 kg of rice per shirt leaves **both** better off than producing alone.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Absolute advantage is not comparative advantage.</strong> A country can hold the absolute
    advantage in both goods (produce more of each) and still have a comparative advantage in only
    <em>one</em> of them — because comparative advantage is about the <strong>lower opportunity cost</strong>,
    not the larger output. Ricardo's whole point is that a country which is worse at everything in absolute
    terms still gains by exporting the good it is <em>least bad</em> at. Answer the comparative-advantage
    question by comparing opportunity costs, never by comparing raw outputs.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. The Heckscher-Ohlin theory and the terms of trade

Ricardo explained comparative advantage by differences in **labour productivity** but did not say where
those differences came from. The **Heckscher–Ohlin theory** (also called the **factor-endowment theory**),
developed by the Swedish economists Eli Heckscher and Bertil Ohlin, offers an answer: comparative advantage
grows out of differences in the **factor endowments** of countries — the relative amounts of land, labour
and capital they possess.

The core idea is that **a country will export the goods that use its abundant (and therefore cheap) factor
intensively, and import the goods that use its scarce (and therefore expensive) factor intensively.** A
country rich in labour and short of capital — many developing economies — has cheap labour, so it has a
comparative advantage in **labour-intensive** goods (garments, handicrafts) and tends to export them. A
country rich in capital exports **capital-intensive** goods (machinery, aircraft). Trade, in this view, is
an indirect way of exchanging a country's abundant factor for its scarce one.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The one-line summary examiners want: under Heckscher–Ohlin a country <strong>exports the good that
    uses its abundant factor intensively</strong>. Labour-abundant country → exports labour-intensive
    goods; capital-abundant country → exports capital-intensive goods. The theory locates the
    <em>source</em> of comparative advantage in factor endowments — it does not replace the comparative-cost
    principle, it explains it.
  </p>
</Callout>

**The terms of trade.** Once countries trade, the rate at which one country's exports exchange for its
imports is its **terms of trade**. In the commodity (or net barter) form it is measured as an index of
export prices against an index of import prices:

<div class="formula">Terms of trade = (index of export prices ÷ index of import prices) × 100</div>

A rise in the index means the terms of trade have moved in the country's **favour** — each unit of exports
now buys more imports, because export prices have risen relative to import prices. A fall means the terms of
trade have **worsened**: the country must sell more exports to buy the same imports. For a country whose
export prices rose 20% while import prices rose only a little, the terms of trade improve; for an oil
importer facing a crude-price spike, they usually worsen.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — export price index 120, import price index 96</div>
  Terms of trade = (120 ÷ 96) × 100 = 1.25 × 100 = <strong>125</strong><br />
  The index is above 100, so the terms of trade have moved in the country's <strong>favour</strong> — its
  export prices have risen faster than its import prices, and each unit of exports now buys more imports
  than in the base year.
</div>

## 4. Free trade versus protection, and tariffs

**Free trade** is trade left free of government restrictions — no tariffs, no quotas, no artificial
barriers — so that goods move between countries according to comparative advantage. The theories of Units 1
and 3 make the case that free trade raises world output and benefits consumers through lower prices and
wider choice. **Protection** is the opposite policy: the government shields domestic producers from foreign
competition using **instruments of trade policy**. The two chief instruments are the **tariff** and the
**quota**, and Unit 2 is about how they work.

A **tariff** is a **tax on an imported (or, less commonly, an exported) good**. An **import duty** raises the
price of the foreign good in the home market, making domestic producers more competitive and raising revenue
for the government. Tariffs come in two forms:

- A **specific tariff** is a fixed money amount **per unit** of the good — say ₹ 90 on each imported tyre,
  whatever its price.
- An **ad valorem tariff** is a **percentage of the value** of the good — say 15% of the price, so a dearer
  good pays a larger duty.

<div class="formula">Price after a specific tariff = world price + tariff per unit</div>
<div class="formula">Price after an ad valorem tariff = world price × (1 + tariff rate)</div>

The effect of an import tariff is to **raise the domestic price** of the good by the amount of the duty.
That helps domestic producers (they can sell at the higher price and expand output) and the government
(which collects the duty), but it **hurts domestic consumers**, who pay more and buy less. Because a tariff
lifts the price above the free-trade level, it moves the country away from the pattern of comparative
advantage — the gain to producers and the government is smaller than the loss to consumers, so a tariff
carries a net cost to the economy as a whole.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — a specific and an ad valorem tariff</div>
  A good has a world (landed) price of ₹ 450. A <strong>specific</strong> import tariff of ₹ 90 makes the
  domestic price 450 + 90 = <strong>₹ 540</strong>.<br />
  A different good is imported at ₹ 1,200 with a <strong>15% ad valorem</strong> tariff. The duty is
  1,200 × 15% = ₹ 180, so the price becomes 1,200 + 180 = <strong>₹ 1,380</strong> — the same as
  1,200 × 1.15.
</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Keep the two tariff forms straight. <strong>Specific</strong> = a fixed sum per unit (₹ so-many per
    piece, per tonne, per litre); <strong>ad valorem</strong> = a percentage of value (Latin
    <em>ad valorem</em>, "according to value"). A specific tariff is unchanged as the price rises; an ad
    valorem tariff grows with the price. An import duty <em>raises</em> the domestic price — that is the
    whole mechanism of protection.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Quotas and other non-tariff barriers

Where a tariff works through **price**, a **quota** works through **quantity**. A quota is a **direct limit
on the amount of a good that may be imported** in a period — for example, a ceiling of 50,000 tonnes of a
commodity a year. Once the quota is filled, no more may be imported whatever the price. By restricting
supply, a quota **raises the domestic price** just as a tariff does; the difference is that a tariff lets in
any quantity at the higher price and earns the government revenue, while a quota fixes the quantity and the
extra profit from the higher price usually goes to whoever holds the import licences, not the treasury.

Everything that restricts trade **without** being a tariff is a **non-tariff barrier (NTB)**. The main ones:

- **Quotas**, as above — the most important quantitative restriction.
- **Subsidies to domestic producers.** A subsidy lowers home producers' costs, letting them undercut
  imports. An **export subsidy** helps domestic firms sell abroad below cost. Subsidies protect indirectly,
  by making the domestic good artificially cheap rather than the foreign good artificially dear.
- **Voluntary export restraints (VERs).** The exporting country "voluntarily" agrees to limit its exports
  to another country — usually under the threat of harsher measures if it does not. A VER is a quota
  administered by the exporter rather than the importer.
- **Administrative and technical barriers** — complex customs procedures, licensing, and product, health or
  safety standards set so as to keep foreign goods out.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    A <strong>tariff is a tax</strong> (it works on price and raises revenue); a <strong>quota is a
    quantity limit</strong> (it works on volume and usually raises no revenue for the government). Both
    push the domestic price <em>up</em> and both protect home producers, so do not treat "raises the
    price" as the thing that separates them — the separation is <strong>price instrument versus quantity
    instrument</strong>, and whether the government collects revenue.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. The arguments for and against protection

Free trade is efficient in theory, yet every country protects some industries. The exam expects you to know
the standard arguments on both sides.

**Arguments *for* protection:**

- **The infant-industry argument.** A new domestic industry cannot yet compete with established foreign
  giants; a temporary tariff gives it time to grow, gain scale and become efficient, after which the
  protection is removed. This is the strongest theoretical case for protection.
- **Protecting employment.** Restricting imports is said to protect domestic jobs from cheaper foreign
  competition.
- **National security / strategic industries.** A country may protect industries (defence, food, energy) it
  does not wish to depend on foreigners for.
- **Anti-dumping.** If a foreign firm "dumps" goods below cost to destroy domestic rivals, a duty can offset
  the unfair advantage.
- **Revenue and the balance of payments.** Tariffs raise government revenue and can curb imports to ease a
  balance-of-payments deficit.

**Arguments *against* protection:**

- It **raises prices** and narrows choice for domestic consumers.
- It **shelters inefficiency**: protected firms have little pressure to cut costs or innovate.
- It provokes **retaliation** — other countries raise their own barriers, and everyone's exports suffer.
- It **misallocates resources** away from the pattern of comparative advantage, lowering world (and usually
  national) output.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The single most-tested item here is the <strong>infant-industry argument</strong>: a young industry is
    protected <em>temporarily</em> until it can stand on its own. Note the word "temporarily" — the case
    depends on the protection being removed once the industry matures. The chief argument <em>against</em>
    protection that examiners look for is the loss to <strong>consumers</strong> (higher prices) and the
    risk of <strong>retaliation</strong>.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. The WTO: from GATT to today

After the Second World War, countries wanted rules to keep trade open and to settle disputes without trade
wars. From 1948 the **General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT)** provided a framework of negotiated
tariff cuts through a series of "rounds" of talks. GATT was an agreement, not a permanent organisation. The
last and largest round, the **Uruguay Round**, created a proper institution: the **World Trade Organization
(WTO)**, which began on **1 January 1995** and absorbed and extended GATT. The WTO is the global body that
administers the rules of international trade, based in Geneva.

**Objectives and functions of the WTO.** Its broad objectives are to raise living standards and incomes
through expanding trade, to promote **freer trade** by lowering barriers, and to provide a stable and
predictable trading environment. Its main functions are:

- to **administer WTO trade agreements**;
- to serve as a **forum for trade negotiations** among members;
- to **settle trade disputes** through a binding dispute-settlement mechanism;
- to **review national trade policies**; and
- to assist developing countries with trade matters and cooperate with other international bodies.

**The principles of the WTO.** Two non-discrimination principles are the ones to remember:

- **Most-favoured-nation (MFN) treatment.** A country must **not discriminate between its trading
  partners**: a trade advantage (say a lower tariff) granted to one member must be granted to **all** other
  members equally. "Most favoured" is misleading — it actually means treating everyone as well as the most
  favoured.
- **National treatment.** Once a good has entered a country and cleared customs, it must be treated **no
  less favourably than a like domestic good** — foreign and domestic goods are treated equally inside the
  home market.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Do not swap the two principles. <strong>Most-favoured-nation</strong> is non-discrimination
    <em>between different foreign countries</em> (treat all your trading partners alike).
    <strong>National treatment</strong> is non-discrimination <em>between imported and domestic goods
    inside your market</em> (treat the foreign good like your own once it is in). MFN looks outward across
    partners; national treatment looks inward at home-versus-imported.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. Regional trade blocs, and India and the WTO

Alongside the global WTO system, groups of countries form **regional trade blocs** — agreements to trade
more freely among themselves. They come in rising degrees of integration: a **free-trade area** (members
remove tariffs on each other's goods but keep their own external tariffs), a **customs union** (a free-trade
area plus a common external tariff), a **common market** (a customs union that also allows free movement of
labour and capital), and an **economic union** (a common market with unified economic policies and often a
single currency). The European Union is the leading example of deep integration; regional groupings such as
ASEAN and NAFTA/USMCA are others. Blocs can boost trade **among members**, but by diverting trade away from
cheaper non-members they can pull against the WTO's global, non-discriminatory ideal.

**India and the WTO.** India is a founder member of the WTO (and was a party to GATT before it). It
participates in trade negotiations and the dispute-settlement system, and its stance has generally pressed
for the interests of developing countries — for example on protecting the livelihood concerns of poor
farmers (food security and agricultural subsidies), on flexibility for developing economies, and on issues
of market access for its services and manufactured exports. The examinable point is conceptual: India
engages with the WTO to gain from a rules-based trading order while seeking policy space suited to a
developing economy.

## 9. The foreign exchange rate

The **foreign-exchange rate** is the **price of one currency in terms of another** — how many rupees it takes
to buy one US dollar, for instance (₹ 83 per $ means one dollar costs ₹ 83). It is the single price that
links the Indian economy to the rest of the world, because every cross-border payment must convert one
currency into another. Three versions of the rate are worth naming:

- The **nominal exchange rate** is the plain market rate — the number of units of one currency per unit of
  another (₹ per $), unadjusted for prices.
- The **real exchange rate** adjusts the nominal rate for the **price levels** in the two countries. It
  measures the rate at which the goods of one country exchange for the goods of another, and so tells you
  about **competitiveness** rather than just the currency price.
- The **effective (or nominal effective) exchange rate** is a **weighted average** of a currency's exchange
  rates against a **basket** of other currencies, weighted by trade shares. It summarises the currency's
  overall strength against its trading partners in a single number, rather than against one currency alone.

To convert an amount from one currency to the other, multiply or divide by the rate. If ₹ 83 buys one
dollar, then a dollar amount is turned into rupees by **multiplying** by 83, and a rupee amount is turned
into dollars by **dividing** by 83.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — converting both ways at ₹ 83 per $</div>
  An importer must pay $ 600. In rupees: 600 × 83 = <strong>₹ 49,800</strong> (multiply the dollar amount
  by the rate).<br />
  An exporter receives ₹ 3,32,000. In dollars: 3,32,000 ÷ 83 = <strong>$ 4,000</strong> (divide the rupee
  amount by the rate).
</div>

A **cross rate** is the exchange rate between two currencies worked out **through a third**. If the rupee
and the pound are each quoted against the dollar, the rupee–pound rate follows by combining the two quotes.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — a cross rate through the dollar</div>
  Suppose $ 1 = ₹ 80 and £ 1 = $ 1.25.<br />
  Then £ 1 buys $ 1.25, and each of those dollars is worth ₹ 80, so £ 1 = 1.25 × 80 =
  <strong>₹ 100</strong>.
</div>

## 10. How a floating exchange rate is determined

Under a **flexible (floating) exchange-rate system** the rate is left to the market, set by the **demand for
and supply of foreign exchange** — exactly like any price. Take the market for US dollars in India, with the
rupee price of a dollar on the vertical axis and the quantity of dollars on the horizontal axis.

- The **demand for foreign exchange** (dollars) comes from Indians who need dollars — importers paying for
  foreign goods, students and tourists spending abroad, and investors buying foreign assets. The demand
  curve slopes **downward**: when the dollar is cheaper in rupees, imports and foreign spending become
  cheaper, so more dollars are demanded.
- The **supply of foreign exchange** (dollars) comes from foreigners who need rupees — foreign buyers of
  Indian exports, tourists visiting India, and foreign investors bringing money in. The supply curve slopes
  **upward**: when the dollar is dearer in rupees (the rupee cheap), Indian exports look cheap to
  foreigners, so more dollars flow in.

<div class="formula">Equilibrium exchange rate: quantity of foreign exchange demanded = quantity supplied</div>

The equilibrium exchange rate is where the two curves cross — the rupee price of a dollar at which the
dollars Indians want to buy exactly match the dollars foreigners want to supply. If Indians suddenly demand
more dollars (say the oil import bill jumps), the demand curve shifts right, and the rupee price of a dollar
**rises** — the rupee depreciates. If foreigners buy more Indian exports, the supply of dollars rises, and
the rupee price of a dollar **falls** — the rupee appreciates.

## 11. Appreciation, depreciation, and fixed versus floating systems

Under a floating system the rate moves continuously, and two words describe the moves.

- **Appreciation** of the rupee means the rupee becomes **stronger** — it takes **fewer** rupees to buy a
  dollar (say ₹ 83 → ₹ 80 per $). The rupee's value in dollars has risen.
- **Depreciation** of the rupee means the rupee becomes **weaker** — it takes **more** rupees to buy a
  dollar (₹ 83 → ₹ 85 per $). The rupee's value in dollars has fallen.

**Effect on exports and imports.** A **depreciation** makes the home currency cheaper, so it makes the
country's **exports cheaper** to foreigners (they buy more) and its **imports dearer** in home-currency terms
(residents buy fewer) — depreciation therefore tends to **boost exports and curb imports**. An
**appreciation** does the reverse: it makes **exports dearer** and **imports cheaper**, so it tends to help
importers and consumers of foreign goods and to hurt exporters.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — a rupee depreciation, exporter and importer</div>
  A software exporter invoices $ 50,000. Before, at ₹ 80 per $, that is 50,000 × 80 = ₹ 40,00,000. After
  the rupee depreciates to ₹ 85 per $, it is 50,000 × 85 = ₹ 42,50,000 — a gain of
  <strong>₹ 2,50,000</strong>. The exporter is <strong>better off</strong>.<br />
  An importer with a $ 1,00,000 bill pays 1,00,000 × 78 = ₹ 78,00,000 before, but 1,00,000 × 80 =
  ₹ 80,00,000 after a depreciation to ₹ 80 per $ — an extra <strong>₹ 2,00,000</strong>. The importer is
  <strong>worse off</strong>.
</div>

**Fixed versus floating systems.** Under a **fixed (pegged) exchange-rate system** the government or central
bank sets the rate and holds it there, buying and selling foreign exchange from its reserves to keep the rate
at the announced level. Under a **floating (flexible) system** the market sets the rate as above, and the
central bank does not commit to any level (a "managed float" is the common middle ground, where the central
bank intervenes to smooth movements but does not fix the rate). A fixed rate gives certainty for trade and
investment but demands large reserves and surrenders monetary independence; a floating rate adjusts
automatically to shocks but can be volatile.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Depreciation is not devaluation.</strong> A currency <strong>depreciates</strong> when it loses
    value under a <em>floating</em> system through market forces — no one decides it. A currency is
    <strong>devalued</strong> when the government <em>deliberately</em> lowers its official value under a
    <em>fixed</em> system (and <strong>revalues</strong> when it deliberately raises it). Same direction of
    change, opposite cause: depreciation is the market's doing, devaluation is a policy decision. Watch also
    that <strong>appreciation helps importers, not exporters</strong> — the exporter gains from a
    <em>depreciation</em>.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 12. Foreign capital: FDI versus FPI

Money crosses borders not only to pay for goods but to be **invested**. **Foreign capital** flowing into a
country takes two main forms, and the exam turns constantly on the difference between them.

- **Foreign direct investment (FDI)** is investment that gives the foreign investor a **lasting interest and
  a degree of control** in a business in the host country — building a factory, or buying a large enough
  stake to influence management. It is tied to real, productive assets, is relatively **long-term and
  stable**, and usually brings technology, management skill and jobs with it.
- **Foreign portfolio investment (FPI)** is investment in a country's **financial assets** — shares and
  bonds — **without control** of the business. The investor seeks a financial return, not management.
  Portfolio money is **liquid and short-term**: it can leave quickly if sentiment turns, which is why it is
  sometimes called "hot money".

| Feature | Foreign direct investment (FDI) | Foreign portfolio investment (FPI) |
|---|---|---|
| What is bought | real/productive assets (a plant, a controlling stake) | financial assets (shares, bonds) without control |
| Control of the business | yes — a lasting management interest | no — purely a financial stake |
| Time horizon | long-term, stable | short-term, easily reversed |
| Volatility | low — hard to pull out quickly | high — "hot money" that can flee fast |
| What it brings | capital plus technology, skills, jobs | capital (financial) only |

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The dividing line is <strong>control</strong>. FDI buys a lasting management interest in a real business
    (long-term, stable); FPI buys shares or bonds for a financial return with no control (short-term, liquid,
    "hot money"). If a question stresses "controlling interest", "sets up a plant" or "brings technology", it
    means <strong>FDI</strong>; if it stresses "shares and bonds", "no management role" or "can leave
    quickly", it means <strong>FPI</strong>.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 13. The balance of payments

The **balance of payments (BoP)** is a systematic record of **all economic transactions** between the
residents of a country and the rest of the world over a period. It is drawn up on double-entry principles, so
in the accounting sense it always **balances**; when people speak of a BoP "surplus" or "deficit" they mean an
imbalance in particular accounts, financed by a change in official reserves. The BoP has two main accounts:

- The **current account** records flows of **goods, services, income and current transfers**. It contains
  the **balance of trade** (exports minus imports of **goods**), **net services** (software, tourism, shipping
  — India's "invisibles"), **net income** (interest, dividends, wages across borders) and **net current
  transfers** (remittances, gifts, foreign aid).
- The **capital account** (with the financial account) records flows of **capital** — FDI and FPI, loans,
  banking capital and changes in **foreign-exchange reserves**. It records how a current-account gap is
  financed: a current-account deficit is matched by a net inflow on the capital account (borrowing or selling
  assets), and a surplus by a net outflow.

| | Current account records | Capital account records |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of flows | goods, services, income, current transfers | capital and financial flows |
| Examples | exports and imports of goods; software and tourism receipts; remittances; interest and dividends | FDI, FPI, external loans, banking capital, change in reserves |
| Question it answers | are we earning enough from trade and invisibles? | how is any current-account gap financed? |

The **balance of trade (BoT)** is the narrowest of these ideas: it is **only the goods** part of the current
account.

<div class="formula">Balance of trade = exports of goods − imports of goods</div>
<div class="formula">Current-account balance = balance of trade + net services + net income + net current transfers</div>
<div class="formula">Balance of payments identity: current account + capital account (incl. reserve changes) = 0</div>

A country can run a **deficit on the balance of trade** (importing more goods than it exports) yet a
**surplus on the current account**, if its earnings from services and remittances more than cover the goods
gap — which is close to India's own pattern, where large software and remittance earnings offset a big
merchandise-trade deficit.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — balance of trade and current-account balance</div>
  Merchandise exports = ₹ 500; merchandise imports = ₹ 700 → balance of trade = 500 − 700 =
  <strong>−₹ 200 (a trade deficit)</strong>.<br />
  Add net services = +250, net income = −40, net current transfers = +90:<br />
  Current-account balance = −200 + 250 − 40 + 90 = <strong>+₹ 100 (a current-account surplus)</strong>.<br />
  So a goods deficit sits alongside a current-account surplus, because invisibles more than covered the
  goods gap.
</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>The balance of trade is only goods.</strong> It does not include services, income or transfers —
    those belong to the wider current account. So the balance of trade can be in deficit while the current
    account is in surplus (or the reverse). And the <strong>balance of payments</strong> is wider still: it
    is the whole record (current account <em>plus</em> capital account). Do not use "balance of trade",
    "current account" and "balance of payments" as if they meant the same thing — trade ⊂ current account ⊂
    balance of payments.
  </p>
</Callout>

## Fully worked examples

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — absolute advantage from an output table (easy)">
  <p>
    From one unit of resources, India produces 80 shirts or 40 kg of rice, and Vietnam produces 60 shirts or
    60 kg of rice. (a) Which country has the absolute advantage in shirts? (b) Which has it in rice?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) compare shirt output</div>
    India makes 80 shirts, Vietnam 60, from the same resources. The larger output is India's, so
    <strong>India has the absolute advantage in shirts</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) compare rice output</div>
    India makes 40 kg of rice, Vietnam 60. Vietnam's output is larger, so <strong>Vietnam has the absolute
    advantage in rice</strong>. Each country is the more efficient producer of one good.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — comparative advantage from opportunity costs (moderate)">
  <p>
    Using the same figures (India: 80 shirts or 40 kg rice; Vietnam: 60 shirts or 60 kg rice), find each
    country's opportunity cost of a shirt and of a kilogram of rice, and say who has the comparative
    advantage in each good.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — opportunity cost of 1 shirt (rice given up)</div>
    India: 40 ÷ 80 = 0.5 kg of rice per shirt.<br />
    Vietnam: 60 ÷ 60 = 1 kg of rice per shirt.<br />
    India gives up less, so <strong>India has the comparative advantage in shirts</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — opportunity cost of 1 kg rice (shirts given up)</div>
    India: 80 ÷ 40 = 2 shirts per kg.<br />
    Vietnam: 60 ÷ 60 = 1 shirt per kg.<br />
    Vietnam gives up less, so <strong>Vietnam has the comparative advantage in rice</strong>. Each country
    should specialise where its opportunity cost is lower, and trade for the rest.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — the terms of trade (easy-moderate)">
  <p>
    A country's index of export prices is 120 and its index of import prices is 96 (base year = 100). Find
    its terms of trade and say whether they have improved or worsened.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — apply the formula</div>
    Terms of trade = (export price index ÷ import price index) × 100 = (120 ÷ 96) × 100 = 1.25 × 100 =
    <strong>125</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — read the result</div>
    The index is above 100, so the terms of trade have <strong>improved (moved in the country's
    favour)</strong>: export prices have risen faster than import prices, so each unit of exports now buys
    more imports than in the base year.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — tariff effect on price (easy-moderate)">
  <p>
    (a) A good has a world price of ₹ 450 and faces a specific import tariff of ₹ 90. Find the domestic
    price. (b) Another good is imported at ₹ 1,200 and faces a 15% ad valorem tariff. Find the domestic
    price.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) specific tariff adds a fixed sum</div>
    Domestic price = world price + tariff per unit = 450 + 90 = <strong>₹ 540</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) ad valorem tariff is a percentage of value</div>
    Duty = 1,200 × 15% = ₹ 180. Domestic price = 1,200 + 180 = <strong>₹ 1,380</strong> (= 1,200 × 1.15).<br />
    In both cases the tariff <strong>raises the domestic price</strong>, protecting home producers and
    burdening consumers.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — currency conversion at a given rate (easy)">
  <p>
    The exchange rate is ₹ 83 per US dollar. (a) An importer must pay $ 600 for a shipment — what is the cost
    in rupees? (b) An exporter receives ₹ 3,32,000 — how many dollars is that?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) dollars to rupees: multiply by the rate</div>
    Cost in rupees = 600 × 83 = <strong>₹ 49,800</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) rupees to dollars: divide by the rate</div>
    Amount in dollars = 3,32,000 ÷ 83 = <strong>$ 4,000</strong>. To go from dollars to rupees multiply; to
    go the other way divide.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 6 — a cross rate through the dollar (moderate)">
  <p>
    In the market, $ 1 = ₹ 80 and £ 1 = $ 1.25. Work out how many rupees one pound is worth.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — chain the two quotes</div>
    One pound buys $ 1.25. Each of those dollars is worth ₹ 80.<br />
    So £ 1 = 1.25 × 80 = <strong>₹ 100</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — the general rule</div>
    A cross rate is found by <strong>multiplying through the common currency</strong>: (₹ per $) × ($ per £)
    = ₹ per £. Always check the units cancel to leave the rate you want.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 7 — rupee depreciation: exporter and importer (moderate)">
  <p>
    (a) A software exporter invoices a US client $ 50,000. The rupee depreciates from ₹ 80 to ₹ 85 per
    dollar. How much more does the exporter earn in rupees? (b) An importer with a bill of $ 1,00,000 faces a
    depreciation from ₹ 78 to ₹ 80 per dollar. How much more must the importer pay?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) the exporter gains</div>
    Before: 50,000 × 80 = ₹ 40,00,000. After: 50,000 × 85 = ₹ 42,50,000.<br />
    Extra earnings = 42,50,000 − 40,00,000 = <strong>₹ 2,50,000</strong> — a depreciation helps the
    exporter.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) the importer loses</div>
    Before: 1,00,000 × 78 = ₹ 78,00,000. After: 1,00,000 × 80 = ₹ 80,00,000.<br />
    Extra cost = 80,00,000 − 78,00,000 = <strong>₹ 2,00,000</strong> — the same depreciation hurts the
    importer. One rate move, opposite effects on the two sides.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 8 — balance of trade and current-account balance (moderate)">
  <p>
    A country records (all in ₹ crore): merchandise exports 600, merchandise imports 800, net services +250,
    net income −40, net current transfers +90. Find (a) the balance of trade and (b) the current-account
    balance, and state the position of each.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) balance of trade is goods only</div>
    Balance of trade = exports of goods − imports of goods = 600 − 800 = <strong>−₹ 200 crore</strong> — a
    <strong>trade deficit</strong>. Services, income and transfers are excluded here.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) add the invisibles for the current account</div>
    Current-account balance = balance of trade + net services + net income + net current transfers<br />
    = −200 + 250 − 40 + 90 = <strong>+₹ 100 crore</strong> — a <strong>current-account surplus</strong>.<br />
    A goods deficit turned into a current-account surplus because services and transfers more than covered
    the goods gap.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes and examiner traps

- **Absolute versus comparative advantage.** Absolute advantage compares **outputs** (who makes more);
  comparative advantage compares **opportunity costs** (who gives up less). A country can hold the absolute
  advantage in both goods yet have a comparative advantage in only one. Answer comparative-advantage
  questions from opportunity costs, never from raw output.
- **Reading the trade table the wrong way.** If the table gives **output per unit of resources**, the bigger
  number is the more efficient producer; if it gives **resources needed per unit**, the smaller number is.
  Check which way the table runs before you compare.
- **Specific versus ad valorem tariff.** Specific = a fixed money amount per unit; ad valorem = a percentage
  of value. An import tariff, of either kind, **raises** the domestic price.
- **Tariff versus quota.** A tariff is a **tax** on imports (price instrument, raises revenue); a quota is a
  **quantity limit** on imports (usually raises no revenue). Both lift the domestic price.
- **MFN versus national treatment.** MFN = treat all your **trading partners** alike; national treatment =
  treat an **imported good like a domestic good** once it is inside your market.
- **Depreciation versus devaluation.** Depreciation is a market fall under a **floating** system;
  devaluation is a deliberate policy cut under a **fixed** system. Same direction, different cause.
- **Which way a depreciation pushes trade.** A depreciation makes **exports cheaper and imports dearer** — it
  helps exporters and curbs imports. Appreciation does the opposite and **helps importers, not exporters**.
- **FDI versus FPI.** The dividing line is **control**: FDI buys a lasting management stake (long-term,
  stable); FPI buys shares and bonds with no control (short-term, "hot money").
- **Balance of trade is only goods.** It excludes services, income and transfers. Trade ⊂ current account ⊂
  balance of payments — do not treat the three as the same.

## One-page revision summary

**Why trade / absolute advantage (Smith):** countries trade because they differ; a country has an
**absolute advantage** in a good if it produces more of it from the same resources. Specialise where you are
the more efficient producer.

**Comparative advantage (Ricardo):** a country has the comparative advantage in the good with the **lower
opportunity cost**; opportunity cost of X = units of the other good given up ÷ units of X gained. Trade
benefits both countries even if one is better at everything, provided the degrees of advantage differ. The
exchange ratio must lie **between** the two opportunity-cost ratios for both to gain.

**Heckscher–Ohlin (factor endowments):** a country **exports the good that uses its abundant (cheap) factor
intensively** — labour-abundant → labour-intensive exports; capital-abundant → capital-intensive exports.

<div class="formula">Terms of trade = (export price index ÷ import price index) × 100; above 100 = favourable</div>

**Trade policy:** free trade vs protection. **Tariff** = tax on imports (specific = per unit; ad valorem =
% of value); it **raises the domestic price** and earns revenue. **Quota** = quantity limit on imports;
raises price, usually no revenue. Other **non-tariff barriers**: subsidies, voluntary export restraints,
technical/administrative barriers. **For protection:** infant industry (temporary), jobs, national security,
anti-dumping. **Against:** higher prices, sheltered inefficiency, retaliation, misallocation.

**WTO:** grew from **GATT** (1948); the WTO began **1 Jan 1995**. Functions: administer agreements, host
negotiations, settle disputes, review trade policies. Principles: **MFN** (treat all partners alike) and
**national treatment** (treat imported and domestic goods alike inside the market). Regional blocs:
free-trade area → customs union → common market → economic union.

**Exchange rate** = price of one currency in another. **Nominal** (plain market rate), **real** (adjusted
for price levels — competitiveness), **effective** (trade-weighted average against a basket). Under a
**floating** system the rate is set by the **demand for and supply of foreign exchange**; a fixed system pegs
it with reserves.

**Appreciation** = fewer rupees per dollar (rupee stronger); **depreciation** = more rupees per dollar
(rupee weaker). A **depreciation** makes **exports cheaper, imports dearer** (helps exporters); appreciation
helps importers. **Devaluation** = a deliberate cut of a **fixed** rate (depreciation is the market's doing).

**Convert:** dollars → rupees, multiply by the rate; rupees → dollars, divide. **Cross rate:** multiply
through the common currency.

**Foreign capital:** **FDI** = lasting control of a real business (long-term, stable, brings technology);
**FPI** = shares/bonds without control (short-term, "hot money"). The dividing line is **control**.

**Balance of payments** = record of all transactions with the rest of the world. **Current account** (goods,
services, income, transfers) and **capital account** (FDI, FPI, loans, reserves).

<div class="formula">Balance of trade = exports of goods − imports of goods (goods only)</div>
<div class="formula">Current-account balance = balance of trade + net services + net income + net current transfers</div>

A country can have a **trade deficit but a current-account surplus** if invisibles cover the goods gap
(India's pattern). Trade ⊂ current account ⊂ balance of payments.
