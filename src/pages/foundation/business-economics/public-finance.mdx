On the first of February each year the Union Finance Minister stands in Parliament and reads the
Budget. The next morning the newspapers carry one number above all the others — the **fiscal
deficit**, so many lakh crore of rupees, so many per cent of national income. On the same page sits a
photograph of a new highway, a public hospital, a subsidised bag of grain. Every one of those things
is public finance at work: the government deciding what to provide, whom to tax, how much to spend,
and how to cover the gap when spending runs ahead of what it collects. A private shop would never
build a streetlight that everyone can use for free; the government does, and this chapter explains
why, and how it pays for it.

Paper 4 is an objective paper, and this chapter carries two kinds of question. Most test whether you
can name a fiscal function, tell a public good from a private good, spot the externality in a
scenario, or state a canon of taxation cleanly. A steady minority ask you to **compute** — a revenue,
fiscal or primary deficit from a small budget table, a tax liability from a slab, or an average tax
rate. The computations are short. The marks are lost on the exact definition of the fiscal deficit
(receipts **excluding** borrowings), on confusing the impact of a tax with its incidence, and on
reading a different deficit from the one asked.

## 1. The three fiscal functions (Musgrave)

Why does the government take part in the economy at all? The economist **Richard Musgrave** gave the
classic answer: the public household performs **three fiscal functions**, each aimed at a problem the
market alone cannot solve.

- **The allocation function.** The market under-provides some goods — a streetlight, national
  defence, a public park — and over-provides others that impose costs on outsiders, such as
  pollution. Through the budget the government **reallocates resources**: it provides public and
  merit goods and discourages harmful ones, correcting the market's allocation.
- **The distribution function.** The market rewards people according to what they own and can sell,
  which can leave income and wealth very unequally spread. The government **redistributes** through
  progressive taxes on the better-off and transfers, subsidies and welfare spending for the poor,
  aiming at a distribution society finds more acceptable.
- **The stabilization function.** A market economy swings between boom and slump, with inflation in
  the upswing and unemployment in the downswing. The government uses its budget to **stabilise** the
  economy — restraining demand when prices race ahead and boosting it when output and jobs fall
  short.

| Fiscal function | The problem it addresses | The main budget tool |
|---|---|---|
| **Allocation** | market under- or over-provides certain goods | provide public/merit goods; tax harmful ones |
| **Distribution** | income and wealth too unequally spread | progressive taxes; transfers and subsidies |
| **Stabilization** | booms and slumps, inflation and unemployment | vary taxes and spending over the cycle |

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The three functions — <strong>allocation, distribution, stabilization</strong> — are Musgrave's,
    and a whole family of questions gives you a scenario and asks which function it illustrates.
    Match the clue word: "provides a public good" or "corrects an externality" → <strong>allocation</strong>;
    "reduces inequality" or "helps the poor" → <strong>distribution</strong>; "controls inflation" or
    "fights a recession" → <strong>stabilization</strong>.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. Government in a mixed economy and Indian federal finance

India is a **mixed economy**: private firms and markets do most of the producing, but the government
plays a large part alongside them. The government's role is exactly the three functions above — it
provides what the market will not, redistributes income, and stabilises the economy — while leaving
ordinary production and exchange to private decision-makers guided by prices.

India is also a **federation**, so the taxing and spending powers are **divided between the Centre
(Union) and the States**. Broadly, the Union raises the larger, more mobile taxes and takes on
subjects of national reach (defence, currency, national highways); the States raise their own taxes
and run subjects closer to daily life (public order, agriculture, local health). Because the Centre's
revenue tends to outrun its needs while the States' needs outrun their revenue, some Central tax
money is **shared down** to the States.

The body that decides how the shareable central taxes are split is the **Finance Commission**,
appointed periodically. It recommends the **share of central taxes** that goes to the States as a
whole (**vertical devolution**) and how that pool is divided **among** the States (**horizontal
devolution**), and it suggests **grants-in-aid** to States that need them. For this paper the point
is conceptual: the Finance Commission is the constitutional mechanism for **tax devolution** between
the Centre and the States. You are not asked for percentages or Article numbers.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Two facts about Indian federal finance are examinable at Foundation level: taxing and spending
    powers are <strong>split between the Centre and the States</strong>, and the <strong>Finance
    Commission</strong> recommends how central taxes are shared with the States (tax devolution). If
    an option credits tax-sharing to the "Planning Commission", the "RBI" or the "GST Council", it is
    the distractor — the sharing of central taxes is the Finance Commission's job.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. Market failure: meaning

Left to itself a competitive market usually allocates resources efficiently. **Market failure** is
the situation where the market, on its own, **fails to allocate resources efficiently** — it produces
too much of some things and too little of others, so society's welfare is not maximised. Market
failure is the economic **case for government intervention**: where the market fails, there is a
reason (though not always a guarantee of improvement) for the government to step in.

The standard causes of market failure are four, and each has its own section below.

- **Public goods** — goods the market will not supply in the right amount because no one can be made
  to pay for them.
- **Externalities** — costs or benefits that fall on third parties, so private and social values
  diverge.
- **Imperfect competition (market power)** — a monopoly or near-monopoly restricts output and raises
  price above the competitive level.
- **Imperfect or asymmetric information** — buyers and sellers do not know enough, or one side knows
  more than the other, so the market misfires.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Market failure does <strong>not</strong> mean the market has "collapsed" or "stopped working". It
    means the market <em>works</em> but produces an <strong>inefficient</strong> outcome — the wrong
    quantities — which the price mechanism alone will not correct. Reading market failure as a market
    that has ceased to exist is the trap.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Public goods, private goods and merit goods

A **public good** has two properties that a private good lacks.

- **Non-rival in consumption.** One person's use does **not** reduce the amount left for others. When
  you enjoy a streetlight or national defence, there is no less of it for your neighbour.
- **Non-excludable.** Once the good is provided, you **cannot keep** non-payers from using it. The
  lighthouse shines for every passing ship, whether or not its owner paid.

Because a public good is non-excludable, each person hopes to enjoy it **without paying**, letting
others foot the bill. This is the **free-rider problem**: if everyone waits for someone else to pay,
the good is not provided at all, even though everyone wants it. That is why the market under-supplies
public goods and why the **government** provides them, paying out of taxes.

A **private good** is the opposite — **rival** (one person's use leaves less for others) and
**excludable** (non-payers can be shut out): a sandwich, a shirt, a seat on a train. A **merit good**
is different again: it is a good (like education or vaccination) that society judges people should
have **more of than they would choose to buy**, because it benefits them and others beyond what they
privately reckon. Merit goods can be excludable and rival — you can be charged for a school place —
but the government subsidises or provides them because private demand under-states their true worth.

| Feature | Private good | Public good | Merit good |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rival in use?** | yes | no | usually yes |
| **Excludable?** | yes | no | usually yes |
| **Why the market fails** | it does not — market supplies it well | free-rider problem: under-supplied | private demand under-states the social benefit |
| **Example** | a sandwich, a shirt | national defence, a streetlight | education, vaccination |

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    A merit good is <strong>not</strong> the same as a public good. A merit good (education,
    healthcare) is usually <strong>excludable and rival</strong> — you can charge for it and one
    person's place uses it up — yet the government encourages it because its benefit to society
    exceeds private demand. A public good (defence, a streetlight) is <strong>non-rival and
    non-excludable</strong>. Mixing the two is a favourite trap.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Externalities and the divergence of private and social cost

An **externality** is a cost or a benefit of an activity that falls on a **third party** — someone
who is neither the buyer nor the seller — and is **not** reflected in the market price. Because the
market price ignores it, the decision-maker weighs only the **private** cost or benefit, while
society bears the **social** cost or benefit. Where the two diverge, the market produces the wrong
quantity.

- **A negative externality** imposes a cost on third parties, so the **social cost exceeds the
  private cost**. A factory that pollutes a river passes a clean-up cost to villagers downstream. The
  firm, counting only its private cost, produces **too much**.
- **A positive externality** confers a benefit on third parties, so the **social benefit exceeds the
  private benefit**. A person who takes a vaccine protects others from catching the disease; a
  well-kept garden pleases the whole street. Counting only the private benefit, the market produces
  **too little**.

Externalities arise in **production** (a factory's smoke — negative; a beekeeper's hive pollinating a
neighbour's orchard — positive) and in **consumption** (a smoker's fumes — negative; a person's
education raising those around them — positive). The general rule is the same in every case:

<div class="formula">Negative externality: social cost &gt; private cost → the market over-produces</div>
<div class="formula">Positive externality: social benefit &gt; private benefit → the market under-produces</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The examiner's shortcut is the direction of the divergence. If the activity <strong>harms</strong>
    outsiders (pollution, noise, smoke), it is a <strong>negative</strong> externality, social cost
    exceeds private cost, and the market makes <strong>too much</strong>. If it <strong>benefits</strong>
    outsiders (vaccination, education), it is a <strong>positive</strong> externality, social benefit
    exceeds private benefit, and the market makes <strong>too little</strong>.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. Imperfect competition and asymmetric information

Two further causes of market failure round out the list.

**Imperfect competition (market power).** Perfect competition, with many small sellers, drives price
down to cost and output up to the efficient level. A **monopoly** or a firm with **market power** does
the opposite: it **restricts output** and charges a **price above marginal cost**, so some buyers who
value the good above what it costs to make go without. The result is an inefficient, under-produced
outcome — a market failure that justifies regulation of monopolies or anti-competition law.

**Imperfect or asymmetric information.** Markets work well only when buyers and sellers know what they
are trading. **Imperfect information** means neither side knows enough — buyers cannot judge quality,
so they will not pay for it and good products are driven out. **Asymmetric information** is sharper:
**one side knows more than the other**. The seller of a used car knows its faults; the buyer does
not. The buyer of insurance knows his own risks; the insurer does not. Such gaps lead to bad outcomes
— low-quality goods crowding out good ones, or risky customers crowding out safe ones — and are a
recognised source of market failure.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Imperfect</strong> information and <strong>asymmetric</strong> information are related but
    not identical. Imperfect information means <em>both</em> sides lack full knowledge; asymmetric
    information means <em>one side knows more than the other</em> (the used-car seller, the insured
    person). If the scenario stresses that one party has hidden knowledge the other lacks, the answer
    is <strong>asymmetric</strong> information.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. Correcting market failure: government intervention

For each cause of market failure the government has a matching tool. The exam likes to pair the
failure with the remedy, so hold the pairs together.

- **Public goods → direct provision.** Because no private firm can charge for a non-excludable good,
  the government **provides it directly** and pays from taxes — defence, streetlights, public roads.
- **Negative externalities → taxes and regulation.** A **tax** on the harmful activity (a pollution
  tax) makes the polluter bear the social cost, so it cuts output back toward the efficient level.
  **Regulation** — emission limits, an outright ban — does the same by rule.
- **Positive externalities → subsidies.** A **subsidy** on the beneficial activity (subsidised
  education or vaccination) lowers its private cost and encourages more of it, up toward the efficient
  level.
- **Imperfect competition → regulation and competition law.** The government **regulates** monopolies
  (price controls, service obligations) or uses **anti-competition law** to keep markets contestable.
- **Asymmetric information → regulation and disclosure rules.** Compulsory **disclosure** (labelling,
  quality standards, licensing) narrows the information gap.

<div class="formula">Harmful activity (negative externality) → tax it · Beneficial activity (positive externality) → subsidise it</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The single most tested pairing is the externality remedy: <strong>tax the negative
    externality, subsidise the positive one</strong>. A pollution tax makes the firm pay for the harm
    it does; an education subsidy rewards the wider benefit. For a pure public good the remedy is
    <strong>direct government provision</strong>, because a tax or subsidy cannot fix a good no one can
    be charged for.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. The government budget

The **government budget** is a statement of the government's **estimated receipts and expenditure**
for a financial year. It is the master instrument of public finance, and it has two broad sides —
what the government **receives** and what it **spends** — each split into a **revenue** part and a
**capital** part.

- **Revenue receipts** are receipts that neither create a liability nor reduce an asset — chiefly
  **taxes** and **non-tax revenue** such as interest, fees and dividends. **Capital receipts** either
  **create a liability** (borrowing) or **reduce an asset** (selling a public undertaking, recovering
  a loan).
- **Revenue expenditure** is spending for the normal running of government that neither creates an
  asset nor reduces a liability — salaries, interest payments, subsidies. **Capital expenditure**
  either **creates an asset** (a new road, a bridge) or **reduces a liability** (repaying the
  principal of a debt).

The gaps between these flows are the **budget deficits**, and they are the most examined idea in the
chapter (Section 13). First, though, the two sources of receipts and the nature of expenditure and
debt deserve a closer look.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Borrowing is a capital receipt, not revenue.</strong> When the government borrows, it
    takes on a liability, so the money is a capital receipt — never counted among revenue receipts.
    This matters enormously for the fiscal deficit, which is defined as total expenditure minus total
    receipts <em>excluding</em> borrowings. Slipping borrowing into revenue is the classic error.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Sources of revenue: tax and non-tax

Government revenue comes from two sources.

**Tax revenue** is the compulsory payment individuals and firms make to the government, for which no
direct service is promised in return. It is the largest source and comes in two families — **direct**
taxes (income tax, corporation tax) and **indirect** taxes (GST, customs duty), treated in Section
10.

**Non-tax revenue** is everything else the government earns without a tax, including:

- **Interest** on loans the government has made,
- **Dividends and profits** from public-sector undertakings and the central bank,
- **Fees, fines and penalties** — court fees, licence fees, traffic fines,
- **Charges for services** the government sells, and grants it receives.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    A <strong>fee</strong> is not a tax. A tax is a compulsory payment with <em>no direct
    service</em> in return; a fee is charged for a <em>specific service</em> the payer receives (a
    passport fee, a licence fee) and is <strong>non-tax revenue</strong>. Filing a passport fee under
    "tax revenue" is a common slip.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. Taxes: classification, canons, and impact versus incidence

**Direct versus indirect.** A **direct tax** is one whose **burden cannot be shifted** — the person
who pays it also bears it (income tax, corporation tax, wealth tax). An **indirect tax** is levied on
goods and services and its **burden can be shifted** from the person who first pays it (the seller) to
someone else (the buyer), through the price (GST, customs and excise duties).

| Feature | Direct tax | Indirect tax |
|---|---|---|
| **Levied on** | income and wealth | goods and services |
| **Can the burden be shifted?** | no — payer bears it | yes — passed on in the price |
| **Impact and incidence** | fall on the **same** person | fall on **different** persons |
| **Examples** | income tax, corporation tax | GST, customs duty, excise |

**Progressive, proportional and regressive.** This classifies a tax by how the **average rate**
changes as income rises.

- **Progressive** — the average rate **rises** with income (the rich pay a larger share). A
  slab-based income tax is progressive.
- **Proportional** — the average rate **stays constant** at every income (all pay the same
  percentage).
- **Regressive** — the average rate **falls** as income rises (the poor pay a larger share of their
  income). A flat charge that is the same for everyone bears more heavily on the poor.

**The canons of taxation (Adam Smith).** Adam Smith set out four canons of a good tax:

- **Equality (ability to pay)** — tax in proportion to the taxpayer's ability, so the burden is fair.
- **Certainty** — the amount, time and manner of payment should be **clear** to the taxpayer, not
  arbitrary.
- **Convenience** — the tax should be levied at the time and in the manner **most convenient** for
  the payer (a tax on income deducted when income is received).
- **Economy** — the **cost of collecting** the tax should be small relative to what it raises.

**Impact versus incidence.** These two words are constantly confused and constantly tested.

- The **impact** of a tax is on the person who **first pays** it to the government — the point of
  levy.
- The **incidence** of a tax is on the person who **finally bears** it — who cannot pass it on.

<div class="formula">Impact = who pays the tax first (point of levy) · Incidence = who finally bears it (cannot shift it)</div>

For a **direct** tax the impact and incidence are on the **same** person (you pay your income tax and
you bear it). For an **indirect** tax they are on **different** persons: the shopkeeper pays the GST
to the government (impact on the seller) but adds it to the price, so the buyer finally bears it
(incidence on the buyer).

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Impact and incidence are not the same.</strong> Impact is the <em>initial</em> burden on
    whoever hands the tax to the government; incidence is the <em>final</em> burden on whoever cannot
    shift it further. They coincide for a direct tax and differ for an indirect tax — that difference
    is the whole point of calling a tax "indirect".
  </p>
</Callout>

## 11. Public expenditure and its management

**Public expenditure** is government spending, and it is classified in a way that mirrors the
receipts side.

- **Revenue expenditure** keeps the government running and neither creates an asset nor reduces a
  liability — salaries, pensions, **interest payments**, subsidies, grants.
- **Capital expenditure** either **creates an asset** or **reduces a liability** — building roads,
  bridges, schools and dams, buying machinery, and repaying the principal of loans.

A second, older split is between **developmental** (or plan) expenditure — spending that directly
adds to economic and social development, such as on infrastructure, education and health — and
**non-developmental** expenditure — spending on the general running of the state, such as defence,
administration and interest payments. Good **management** of public expenditure means keeping it
productive, controlling waste, and steering it toward capital and developmental uses that raise the
economy's future capacity rather than toward runaway current spending.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>Interest payments are revenue expenditure</strong>, because they are a running cost that
    neither creates an asset nor reduces a liability — only the repayment of the <em>principal</em> is
    capital expenditure. This fact is load-bearing for the deficits: the primary deficit is the fiscal
    deficit with interest payments stripped out.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 12. Public debt

When a government spends more than it receives, it **borrows**, and the accumulated borrowing is
**public debt**. Governments borrow for good reasons: to finance **capital projects** whose benefits
run for decades, to **stabilise** the economy in a downturn, and to cover a temporary gap between
receipts and spending.

Public debt is classified by **who lends**.

- **Internal debt** is owed to lenders **within** the country — households, banks and firms that buy
  government bonds. It is repaid in the domestic currency and does not, by itself, draw resources out
  of the country.
- **External debt** is owed to lenders **outside** the country — foreign governments, foreign
  investors and international institutions. It brings in foreign resources but must be **serviced in
  foreign currency**, which can strain the balance of payments.

**Redemption** is the repayment of public debt — clearing the principal when it falls due, whether by
paying it off, refinancing it with fresh borrowing, or building a sinking fund to retire it
gradually. Debt is not costless: every rupee borrowed carries **interest**, and heavy interest
payments crowd out other spending — which is exactly why the **primary deficit** singles interest
out.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Internal debt is owed inside the country; external debt is owed abroad.</strong> The
    lender's location — not the currency alone — is the dividing line. A common trap swaps the two, or
    claims external debt is "less of a burden" because it brings in money; in fact external debt must
    be repaid in foreign currency and can weigh on the balance of payments.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 13. The budget deficits: revenue, fiscal and primary

A **deficit** arises when a flow of spending exceeds the matching flow of receipts. Three deficits
matter, and each measures a different thing. Learn the three formulas exactly, because most
computational questions in this chapter turn on them.

<div class="formula">Revenue deficit = revenue expenditure − revenue receipts</div>
<div class="formula">Fiscal deficit = total expenditure − total receipts excluding borrowings</div>
<div class="formula">Primary deficit = fiscal deficit − interest payments</div>

- The **revenue deficit** is the shortfall on the **revenue account** alone — revenue expenditure
  running ahead of revenue receipts. It signals that the government is borrowing to meet its ordinary
  running costs, which is a warning sign.
- The **fiscal deficit** is the **total** gap the government must **borrow** to fill — total spending
  minus every receipt **other than borrowing**. It equals the government's **total borrowing** for
  the year, and it is the headline number of the Budget.
- The **primary deficit** is the fiscal deficit **less interest payments**. Because interest is the
  cost of **past** borrowing, the primary deficit shows the **current** year's fresh gap, stripped of
  the burden inherited from earlier deficits. A zero primary deficit means the government is borrowing
  only to pay interest on old debt.

| Deficit | Formula | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| **Revenue deficit** | revenue expenditure − revenue receipts | borrowing to meet ordinary running costs |
| **Fiscal deficit** | total expenditure − total receipts excl. borrowings | total borrowing needed this year |
| **Primary deficit** | fiscal deficit − interest payments | this year's gap, excluding old-debt interest |

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — a small budget (₹ crore)</div>
  Revenue receipts = 1,600 · Revenue expenditure = 1,800 · Capital expenditure = 400<br />
  Non-debt capital receipts = 100 · Interest payments = 300<br />
  Revenue deficit = 1,800 − 1,600 = <strong>₹ 200 crore</strong><br />
  Total expenditure = 1,800 + 400 = 2,200; total receipts excl. borrowings = 1,600 + 100 = 1,700<br />
  Fiscal deficit = 2,200 − 1,700 = <strong>₹ 500 crore</strong> (this equals the year's borrowing)<br />
  Primary deficit = 500 − 300 = <strong>₹ 200 crore</strong>
</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The fiscal deficit is total expenditure minus total receipts <strong>excluding borrowings</strong>
    — never minus all receipts. If you subtract every receipt including borrowing, the budget always
    "balances" and the deficit vanishes, because borrowing is exactly what fills the gap. The fiscal
    deficit <em>is</em> the borrowing. Leaving borrowings in the receipts is the single most common
    numerical error in this chapter.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 14. Fiscal policy: meaning and objectives

**Fiscal policy** is the government's use of its **taxation and expenditure** (and its borrowing) to
influence the economy. It is the budget used deliberately as a lever, and it is the counterpart of
**monetary policy** (the central bank's use of interest rates and the money supply).

Its **objectives** are the broad goals of macroeconomic management:

- **Economic growth** — raising the economy's output and capacity over time.
- **Full employment** — keeping unemployment low.
- **Price stability** — restraining inflation.
- **Equitable distribution** — reducing inequality of income and wealth.
- **External balance and overall stabilisation** — smoothing the swings of the business cycle.

These objectives line up with Musgrave's three functions: distribution answers the second objective,
stabilisation the third and fifth, and the allocation function shapes what the growth is made of.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Keep the two policies apart. <strong>Fiscal policy</strong> is run by the <em>government</em>
    through <strong>taxes and public spending</strong>; <strong>monetary policy</strong> is run by the
    <em>central bank</em> through <strong>interest rates and the money supply</strong>. An option that
    lists "changing the repo rate" as a fiscal-policy tool is the distractor — that is monetary policy.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 15. The instruments of fiscal policy

Fiscal policy works through the parts of the budget. Its **instruments** are four:

- **Taxation** — raising or lowering taxes to take spending power out of, or leave it in, private
  hands.
- **Public expenditure** — raising or cutting government spending to add to or subtract from total
  demand.
- **Public debt (borrowing)** — how the government finances a deficit, which itself affects demand
  and interest rates.
- **The budget** as a whole — running a deficit, a surplus or a balanced budget as the situation
  requires.

The two active levers are **taxation** and **public expenditure**; borrowing and the budget balance
follow from how those two are set. A change in either lever moves **aggregate demand** — the total
spending in the economy — which is the channel through which fiscal policy reaches output, employment
and prices (Section 17).

## 16. Expansionary and contractionary fiscal policy; automatic stabilizers

Fiscal policy is used in two opposite directions depending on where the economy stands in the cycle.

- **Expansionary fiscal policy** is used in a **recession**, to **raise** aggregate demand. The
  government **cuts taxes**, **raises spending**, or both — which typically **widens the deficit**.
  More demand pulls up output and employment.
- **Contractionary fiscal policy** is used in a **boom with inflation**, to **reduce** aggregate
  demand. The government **raises taxes**, **cuts spending**, or both — which **narrows the deficit or
  builds a surplus**. Less demand cools rising prices.

The way policy is applied is also of two kinds.

- **Discretionary fiscal policy** is a **deliberate** change — a new tax rate, a fresh spending
  programme — decided and legislated in response to conditions.
- **Automatic stabilizers** work **without any new decision**. As the economy changes, some taxes and
  transfers move on their own to steady it. In a boom, rising incomes push people into higher tax
  brackets and cut unemployment payments, restraining demand automatically; in a slump, tax take
  falls and unemployment benefits rise, cushioning demand. A **progressive** income tax and
  **unemployment benefits** are the two classic automatic stabilizers.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    An <strong>automatic stabilizer</strong> needs <em>no new government decision</em> — it is built
    into the tax-and-transfer system and reacts on its own (a progressive tax, unemployment benefit).
    A one-off tax cut announced in the Budget is <strong>discretionary</strong>, not automatic.
    Labelling a fresh, announced measure an "automatic stabilizer" is the trap.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 17. Fiscal policy and aggregate demand

Fiscal policy reaches the economy by moving **aggregate demand** — the total planned spending on
goods and services. Government spending is itself a component of aggregate demand, and taxes work on
it indirectly by changing how much households and firms have left to spend.

- **A rise in government spending** adds **directly** to aggregate demand.
- **A cut in taxes** leaves households and firms with more **disposable income**, part of which they
  spend, so aggregate demand rises **indirectly**.
- **A rise in taxes** or a **cut in spending** works the other way, lowering aggregate demand.

So expansionary policy (lower taxes, higher spending) **raises** aggregate demand and is the remedy
for a recession; contractionary policy (higher taxes, lower spending) **lowers** aggregate demand and
is the remedy for demand-pull inflation. The direction of the deficit follows: expansion widens it,
contraction narrows it.

<div class="formula">Expansionary: cut taxes / raise spending → aggregate demand rises (fights recession)</div>
<div class="formula">Contractionary: raise taxes / cut spending → aggregate demand falls (fights inflation)</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Fix the direction once and the questions become easy. To fight a <strong>recession</strong> you
    want <em>more</em> demand → <strong>expansionary</strong> policy (cut taxes, raise spending,
    deficit widens). To fight <strong>inflation</strong> you want <em>less</em> demand →
    <strong>contractionary</strong> policy (raise taxes, cut spending, deficit narrows). A cut in
    government spending during a recession would deepen it — that is the classic wrong answer.
  </p>
</Callout>

## Fully worked examples

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — the three deficits from one budget (moderate)">
  <p>
    A government's budget for the year shows (₹ crore): revenue receipts 2,000; revenue expenditure
    2,300; capital expenditure 700; recovery of loans and other non-debt capital receipts 200;
    interest payments 400. Find the revenue deficit, the fiscal deficit and the primary deficit.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — revenue deficit</div>
    Revenue deficit = revenue expenditure − revenue receipts = 2,300 − 2,000 = <strong>₹ 300 crore</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — fiscal deficit (receipts exclude borrowings)</div>
    Total expenditure = 2,300 + 700 = 3,000<br />
    Total receipts excluding borrowings = revenue receipts 2,000 + non-debt capital receipts 200 = 2,200<br />
    Fiscal deficit = 3,000 − 2,200 = <strong>₹ 800 crore</strong> (the amount to be borrowed)
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — primary deficit</div>
    Primary deficit = fiscal deficit − interest payments = 800 − 400 = <strong>₹ 400 crore</strong>
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — match the scenario to the fiscal function (easy)">
  <p>
    Name the Musgrave fiscal function each measure illustrates: (a) the government builds a public
    lighthouse no private firm would provide; (b) it levies a higher tax rate on high incomes and
    funds a welfare scheme for the poor; (c) it cuts taxes and raises spending to pull the economy out
    of a recession.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) providing a public good</div>
    Providing a good the market under-supplies is the <strong>allocation function</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) taxing the rich to help the poor</div>
    Reducing inequality by progressive tax and transfers is the <strong>distribution function</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (c) fighting a recession</div>
    Smoothing the cycle — here lifting demand in a slump — is the <strong>stabilization function</strong>.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — classify the good (easy-moderate)">
  <p>
    Classify each as a private, public or merit good, and say why: (a) national defence; (b) a
    restaurant meal; (c) school education.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) national defence</div>
    Non-rival (your protection does not reduce mine) and non-excludable (no one can be shut out) →
    a <strong>public good</strong>; the free-rider problem means the government must provide it.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) a restaurant meal</div>
    Rival (one diner's meal is not another's) and excludable (no payment, no meal) → a
    <strong>private good</strong>, which the market supplies well.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (c) school education</div>
    Excludable and rival, yet its benefit to society exceeds private demand → a <strong>merit
    good</strong>, which the government subsidises so more is consumed.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — identify the externality (moderate)">
  <p>
    For each case, state whether the externality is positive or negative, whether it arises in
    production or consumption, and what the market does wrong: (a) a factory discharges smoke over a
    residential colony; (b) a household plants trees that cool and beautify the whole street.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) the factory's smoke</div>
    The smoke harms third parties, so the <strong>social cost exceeds the private cost</strong> — a
    <strong>negative externality in production</strong>. The firm counts only its private cost and
    <strong>over-produces</strong>; a pollution tax would correct it.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) the household's trees</div>
    The trees benefit others, so the <strong>social benefit exceeds the private benefit</strong> — a
    <strong>positive externality in consumption</strong>. The market <strong>under-produces</strong>;
    a subsidy would encourage more.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — impact versus incidence (moderate)">
  <p>
    The government levies GST on soap. The manufacturer pays the tax to the government and then raises
    the retail price so that consumers pay it. (a) On whom is the impact of the tax? (b) On whom is
    the incidence? (c) Is this a direct or an indirect tax, and why?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) impact = who pays first</div>
    The <strong>manufacturer</strong> hands the tax to the government, so the <strong>impact</strong>
    is on the manufacturer (the point of levy).
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) incidence = who finally bears it</div>
    The manufacturer shifts the tax into the price, so the <strong>consumer</strong> finally bears it
    — the <strong>incidence</strong> is on the consumer.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (c) direct or indirect</div>
    Because the burden is <strong>shifted</strong> from the payer to another person, it is an
    <strong>indirect tax</strong>; impact and incidence fall on different persons.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 6 — progressive tax liability from a slab (moderate)">
  <p>
    Under a slab system, income up to ₹ 2,50,000 is taxed at nil, income from ₹ 2,50,001 to ₹
    5,00,000 at 5%, and income from ₹ 5,00,001 to ₹ 10,00,000 at 20%. A person has a taxable income of
    ₹ 6,00,000. (a) Find the tax liability. (b) Find the average tax rate, and say whether the tax is
    progressive.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) tax slab by slab</div>
    First ₹ 2,50,000 → nil<br />
    Next ₹ 2,50,000 (from 2,50,001 to 5,00,000) at 5% = ₹ 12,500<br />
    Remaining ₹ 1,00,000 (from 5,00,001 to 6,00,000) at 20% = ₹ 20,000<br />
    Total tax = 12,500 + 20,000 = <strong>₹ 32,500</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) average rate and the classification</div>
    Average tax rate = 32,500 ÷ 6,00,000 = <strong>5.42%</strong>, well below the 20% top-slab rate.<br />
    Because the average rate <strong>rises</strong> as income moves into higher slabs, the tax is
    <strong>progressive</strong>.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 7 — fiscal deficit and its financing (moderate)">
  <p>
    A government plans total expenditure of ₹ 45,000 crore. Its revenue receipts are ₹ 32,000 crore
    and its non-debt capital receipts (loan recoveries and disinvestment) are ₹ 3,000 crore. (a) Find
    the fiscal deficit. (b) How much must the government borrow this year?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) fiscal deficit</div>
    Total receipts excluding borrowings = 32,000 + 3,000 = 35,000<br />
    Fiscal deficit = total expenditure − total receipts excl. borrowings = 45,000 − 35,000 =
    <strong>₹ 10,000 crore</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) the borrowing equals the fiscal deficit</div>
    The fiscal deficit <em>is</em> the gap the government must borrow to fill, so borrowing this year =
    <strong>₹ 10,000 crore</strong>.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 8 — choose the fiscal-policy stance (moderate-hard)">
  <p>
    The economy is in a recession, with rising unemployment and weak demand. (a) Should fiscal policy
    be expansionary or contractionary? (b) Name two measures that fit, and their effect on aggregate
    demand and the fiscal deficit. (c) Which of your measures is an automatic stabilizer rather than a
    discretionary one?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) the stance</div>
    A recession needs <strong>more</strong> aggregate demand, so the stance is
    <strong>expansionary</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) measures and effects</div>
    Cut taxes and raise public spending. Both <strong>raise aggregate demand</strong> (spending
    directly, tax cuts by lifting disposable income) and both <strong>widen the fiscal deficit</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (c) automatic versus discretionary</div>
    A deliberately announced tax cut or spending programme is <strong>discretionary</strong>. The
    <strong>automatic</strong> stabilizer is the built-in response — under a progressive tax the tax
    take falls on its own as incomes drop, and unemployment benefits rise automatically, cushioning
    demand with no new decision.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes and examiner traps

- **The fiscal-deficit definition.** Fiscal deficit = total expenditure − total receipts
  **excluding borrowings**. Including borrowings in the receipts makes the deficit vanish; the fiscal
  deficit is exactly equal to the borrowing.
- **Which deficit is asked.** Revenue deficit uses the **revenue account only**; fiscal deficit is
  the **whole** borrowing gap; primary deficit is the **fiscal deficit minus interest**. Compute the
  one the question names.
- **Interest payments.** Interest is **revenue expenditure** (a running cost), and it is what the
  **primary** deficit removes from the fiscal deficit. Only repayment of principal is capital
  expenditure.
- **Impact versus incidence.** Impact is on who **first pays** the tax; incidence is on who
  **finally bears** it. They coincide for a direct tax and differ for an indirect tax.
- **Public good versus merit good.** A public good is **non-rival and non-excludable** (defence, a
  streetlight); a merit good is usually **excludable and rival** but under-demanded (education,
  vaccination). Do not merge the two.
- **Positive versus negative externality.** A harmful spillover (pollution) is **negative** — social
  cost exceeds private cost, so the market over-produces; a beneficial spillover (vaccination) is
  **positive** — social benefit exceeds private benefit, so the market under-produces.
- **Externality remedy.** **Tax** the negative externality; **subsidise** the positive one; **provide**
  a pure public good directly. A tax cannot fix a good no one can be charged for.
- **Fiscal versus monetary policy.** Fiscal policy is the **government's** taxes and spending;
  monetary policy is the **central bank's** interest rates and money supply. The repo rate is not a
  fiscal instrument.
- **Automatic versus discretionary.** An automatic stabilizer works with **no new decision** (a
  progressive tax, unemployment benefit); an announced tax change is discretionary.
- **Fee versus tax.** A fee buys a specific service and is **non-tax revenue**; a tax is compulsory
  with no direct service in return.
- **Internal versus external debt.** Internal debt is owed **within** the country; external debt is
  owed **abroad** and must be serviced in foreign currency.

## One-page revision summary

**Fiscal functions (Musgrave):** **allocation** (provide public/merit goods, correct externalities),
**distribution** (reduce inequality by progressive tax and transfers), **stabilization** (smooth the
cycle, control inflation and unemployment).

**Mixed economy and federal finance:** markets do most production; the government performs the three
functions. Taxing and spending powers are **split between Centre and States**; the **Finance
Commission** recommends the sharing of central taxes with the States (tax devolution).

**Market failure** = the market fails to allocate resources efficiently. Four causes: **public
goods**, **externalities**, **imperfect competition**, **asymmetric/imperfect information**.

**Public good:** **non-rival** and **non-excludable** → **free-rider problem** → under-supplied →
government provides. **Private good:** rival and excludable. **Merit good:** usually excludable/rival
but under-demanded (education, health) → subsidised.

**Externalities:** negative → social cost &gt; private cost → over-production (tax it); positive →
social benefit &gt; private benefit → under-production (subsidise it). Arise in production and in
consumption.

**Remedies:** public good → direct provision; negative externality → tax/regulation; positive
externality → subsidy; monopoly → regulation/competition law; asymmetric information → disclosure
rules.

**Budget:** estimated receipts and expenditure. **Revenue receipts** (taxes, non-tax) create no
liability; **capital receipts** create a liability (borrowing) or reduce an asset. **Revenue
expenditure** (salaries, interest, subsidies); **capital expenditure** (assets, principal repayment).

**Taxes:** **direct** (burden not shifted — income, corporation tax) versus **indirect** (burden
shifted — GST, customs). **Progressive** (average rate rises), **proportional** (constant),
**regressive** (falls). **Canons (Adam Smith):** equality, certainty, convenience, economy. **Impact**
= who pays first; **incidence** = who finally bears it (same person for a direct tax, different for an
indirect tax).

**Public debt:** **internal** (owed within) versus **external** (owed abroad, serviced in foreign
currency); governments borrow for capital projects, stabilisation and temporary gaps; **redemption** =
repayment.

<div class="formula">Revenue deficit = revenue expenditure − revenue receipts</div>
<div class="formula">Fiscal deficit = total expenditure − total receipts excluding borrowings (= borrowing)</div>
<div class="formula">Primary deficit = fiscal deficit − interest payments</div>

**Fiscal policy:** government use of taxes, spending, debt and the budget to pursue growth,
employment, price stability and equity. **Instruments:** taxation, public expenditure, public debt,
the budget. **Expansionary** (cut taxes, raise spending → aggregate demand rises → fights recession,
widens deficit); **contractionary** (raise taxes, cut spending → aggregate demand falls → fights
inflation, narrows deficit). **Automatic stabilizers** (progressive tax, unemployment benefit) act
with no new decision; **discretionary** policy is a deliberate, announced change.
