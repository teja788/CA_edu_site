A small workshop in Ludhiana owns one machine and eight hours of a working day. In those hours it
can turn out bicycle frames, or it can turn out steel gates, but it cannot turn out a full run of
both. Every hour the owner books for frames is an hour that will never make a gate. Nothing about
the machine is broken, no order has been lost — and yet the owner is forced to give something up the
moment she decides what to make. That giving-up is not a mistake in the workshop. It is the single
fact from which the whole of economics grows: wants outrun the means of meeting them, so every choice
is paid for by the choice not taken.

This first chapter is the vocabulary of that idea. It asks what economics is, how four famous
economists each defined it, how the small-scale study of a firm differs from the large-scale study of
a whole nation, what basic problems every society must answer, and how a simple curve — the
Production Possibility Curve — draws scarcity and choice on a single diagram. Paper 4 is entirely
objective, so the marks here are won by holding definitions apart from one another and by reading a
statement precisely: which economist said this, is this a fact or an opinion, is this point on the
curve or beyond it. Almost nothing in the chapter is arithmetic; almost everything is careful
reading.

## 1. What economics means: four definitions and their shift

Economics has been defined four times over by four economists, and the definitions are not rivals so
much as a slow change of focus over a century and a half. Naming the author of each is one of the
most reliably examined points in the chapter.

- **Adam Smith — the wealth definition (1776).** In *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the
  Wealth of Nations*, Smith treated economics as the **science of wealth** — how a nation produces
  and accumulates riches, and how it grows prosperous. Because he placed wealth at the centre and
  said little about the human being who earns it, later writers called this the classical or wealth
  view. Smith is remembered as the **father of economics**.
- **Alfred Marshall — the welfare definition (1890).** In *Principles of Economics*, Marshall moved
  the focus from wealth to the person. Economics, he wrote, studies mankind in the **ordinary
  business of life**; it examines that part of individual and social action most closely connected
  with the attainment and use of the **material requisites of well-being**. Wealth became a means and
  human **welfare** the end.
- **Lionel Robbins — the scarcity definition (1932).** Robbins objected that Marshall's line between
  "material" and "non-material" welfare could not be drawn cleanly. His own definition is the one the
  paper quotes most often: economics is **the science which studies human behaviour as a relationship
  between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses**. Four ideas are packed into that one
  sentence — ends (wants) are unlimited, means (resources) are scarce, the means have alternative
  uses, and so the human problem is one of **choice**.
- **Paul Samuelson — the growth definition.** Samuelson widened Robbins to bring in **time and
  growth**: economics is the study of how people and society choose, with or without money, to employ
  **scarce productive resources** that could have alternative uses, to produce various commodities
  **over time** and distribute them for consumption among various persons and groups. The dynamic,
  growth-oriented flavour — resources, production and distribution seen across time — is what marks
  this as Samuelson's.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The examiner rarely asks "what is economics?"; it asks "whose definition is this?" Fix one keyword
    to each name and the question answers itself: <strong>wealth</strong> is Adam Smith,
    <strong>welfare</strong> is Marshall, <strong>scarce means with alternative uses</strong> is
    Robbins, and <strong>over time / growth</strong> is Samuelson. The phrase "ends and scarce means
    which have alternative uses" is Robbins word for word and is the single most quoted line in the
    chapter.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. Business economics and managerial economics

**Business economics**, also called **managerial economics**, is the branch that applies economic
theory and method to the practical problems of a business. A widely quoted description, due to
Spencer and Siegelman, calls it the **integration of economic theory with business practice for the
purpose of facilitating decision-making and forward planning by management**. Three words in that
line carry its whole character — *integration* (theory joined to practice), *decision-making* (a
choice among alternatives) and *forward planning* (choices made with the future in view).

Its nature is worth stating plainly, because the paper tests each feature:

- It is **microeconomic** in its main emphasis: it studies the individual firm, its costs, its
  demand and its pricing rather than the economy as a whole — though it draws on macroeconomics for
  the business environment.
- It is **pragmatic**. Pure economic theory can afford to simplify away awkward facts; business
  economics keeps the awkward facts because a manager must act in the real world.
- It uses the **theory of the firm** and the tools of decision science, and it is **normative** in
  emphasis in the sense that it prescribes what a firm *should* do to reach its goal, most often the
  goal of maximising profit or value.
- It is **goal-oriented and forward-looking**: every tool is bent towards a decision that has yet to
  be taken.

The relationship to pure economics is one of application. Economic theory supplies the concepts —
demand, cost, market structure, the margin — and business economics puts them to work on a specific
firm's specific problem.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    "Business economics is the same as economics" is the trap. Economics is the wider science of how
    society uses scarce resources; business economics is the <strong>application</strong> of that
    science to the decisions of a single firm. The relationship is application, not identity — an
    option that calls them identical, or that calls business economics purely macroeconomic, is
    wrong.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. Microeconomics and macroeconomics

Economics is studied at two levels, and the terms for them were coined by the Norwegian economist
**Ragnar Frisch**. The prefixes carry the meaning: *micro* is from the Greek for small, *macro* from
the Greek for large.

**Microeconomics** studies the **individual** units of the economy — a single consumer, a single
firm, the price of one product, one market. Because its central concern is how the prices of goods
and factors are determined, it is also called **price theory**. Its questions are small in scale and
sharp in focus: what quantity will this consumer buy, what price will this firm set, how is the wage
in this industry fixed.

**Macroeconomics** studies the economy in the **aggregate** — national income, total output, the
general price level, total employment, the overall level of saving and investment. Because its
central concern is the level of income and employment for the economy as a whole, it is also called
the **theory of income and employment**. Its questions are large in scale: what determines the
nation's output, why do prices in general rise, what causes unemployment across the economy.

| Feature | Microeconomics | Macroeconomics |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of study | an individual consumer, firm, product or market | the economy as a whole — aggregates |
| Central concern | how prices are determined | how income and employment are determined |
| Also called | price theory | theory of income and employment |
| Typical variables | price of a good, output of a firm, a single wage | national income, general price level, total employment |
| Method | studies the parts | studies the whole |

The two are complements, not rivals. A statement about "the price of onions in a mandi" is
microeconomic; a statement about "the rate of inflation in the country" is macroeconomic. The same
event can be looked at both ways: one firm cutting its output is microeconomics, every firm cutting
output together — a fall in national output — is macroeconomics.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Sort by scale. If the subject is <strong>one</strong> of something — one buyer, one seller, one
    good, one market, one price — it is microeconomics. If it is a <strong>total</strong> or a
    <strong>general</strong> — national income, the general price level, aggregate demand, total
    employment — it is macroeconomics. The words "individual" and "aggregate" are the giveaways the
    examiner plants in the stem.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. The central problems of an economy

Every economy, however rich or poor and whatever its politics, faces the same basic problems,
because in every economy resources are scarce relative to the wants they must satisfy. These are
usually stated as three questions of allocation, with two further problems of efficiency and growth
often added.

1. **What to produce, and in what quantities?** Resources used to make one thing cannot make
   another, so a society must choose the goods it will produce and how much of each — more consumer
   goods or more capital goods, more food or more cloth. This is the problem of the **allocation of
   resources**.
2. **How to produce?** The same good can usually be made by different techniques — by more labour and
   less machinery, or by more machinery and less labour. A society must choose the method, and it
   will choose the one that uses its scarce resources most economically.
3. **For whom to produce?** The goods produced must be shared among the people. The rule that decides
   who gets how much is the problem of the **distribution** of the national product.

Two further problems complete the picture:

4. **Are the resources fully and efficiently used?** An economy that leaves workers unemployed or
   machines idle is producing less than it could. This is the problem of the **efficient use** of
   resources.
5. **Is the economy's capacity growing?** A society that only maintains its resources stands still; a
   society that adds to them grows richer over time. This is the problem of **growth**.

The first three are the ones the paper asks about most, usually as a single line — "the problem of
choosing the technique of production is the problem of ___" — where the answer is *how to produce*.
The root cause behind all of them is the same, and the examiner likes to test that too: the central
problems arise because of **scarcity**, the gap between unlimited wants and limited means.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Do not attach the problems to a particular system. The three central problems face
    <strong>every</strong> economy — capitalist, socialist and mixed alike. What differs between
    systems is only the <em>mechanism</em> used to answer them: the price mechanism in a capitalist
    economy, the planning authority in a socialist one. An option that says a planned economy escapes
    the central problems is wrong.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Scarcity, choice and opportunity cost

The chain that ties the whole subject together is short. Human wants are **unlimited**; the resources
to satisfy them are **limited** and have **alternative uses**. Because a limited resource put to one
use cannot be put to another at the same time, using it forces a **choice**. And every choice has a
cost — not the money paid, but the best thing given up.

That cost has a name. The **opportunity cost** of any decision is the value of the **next best
alternative forgone** — the most valuable thing you could have done with the same resources but did
not. The workshop that spends its machine-hours on frames pays, as the opportunity cost, the gates it
could have made instead.

Three features of opportunity cost are worth holding on to:

- It is the **next best** alternative, not the sum of all the alternatives given up. If a resource
  could have gone to three other uses, its opportunity cost is the single most valuable of the three,
  not the three added together.
- It need not be a money cost. The opportunity cost of an hour spent studying is the leisure or the
  earnings that hour could have brought — a real cost even though no money changed hands.
- It applies to **every** agent — a person, a firm, a government. A government that spends a fixed
  budget on a bridge gives up the school the same money would have built.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — the opportunity cost of a decision</div>
  A graduate turns down a job paying ₹ 4,80,000 a year to run her own shop, and puts ₹ 2,00,000 of
  savings into it that had been earning 8% a year in a deposit.<br />
  Salary given up = ₹ 4,80,000 · Interest given up = 8% of 2,00,000 = ₹ 16,000<br />
  Opportunity cost of a year in the shop = 4,80,000 + 16,000 = <strong>₹ 4,96,000</strong><br />
  The shop is worth running only if it is expected to yield more than ₹ 4,96,000 of benefit in the
  year — that figure, not zero, is what the choice must beat.
</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    When a stem lists several things given up, the opportunity cost is the <strong>single best</strong>
    of them, never their total. "A student can spend an evening studying, working a paid shift, or
    meeting friends; the opportunity cost of studying is..." — the answer is the one alternative he
    values most highly, not both of the others combined.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. The Production Possibility Curve

The **Production Possibility Curve** (PPC), also called the production possibility frontier or the
**transformation curve**, draws scarcity and choice on one diagram. It shows the **maximum
combinations of two goods** an economy can produce when its resources and technology are given and
every resource is **fully and efficiently employed**.

It rests on four assumptions:

- The economy produces only **two goods** (so the choice can be drawn in two dimensions).
- The quantity of resources is **fixed** for the period.
- The state of **technology** is fixed.
- Resources are **fully and efficiently** used, and they are **not equally efficient** in every use.

Three properties follow, and each is examined.

**It slopes downward, from left to right.** With resources fully used, the only way to make more of
one good is to move resources away from the other and so make less of it. More of one always means
less of the other, which is a downward slope.

**It is concave to the origin.** As the economy makes more and more of one good, it must give up
ever-larger amounts of the other for each additional unit — the **marginal opportunity cost rises**.
This happens because resources are not equally suited to both goods: the resources best suited to the
second good are drawn away last and reluctantly, and each transfer buys less than the one before.
Rising marginal opportunity cost bows the curve outward, away from the origin.

**Points on it, inside it and outside it each mean something different.**

- A point **on** the curve is **efficient** — resources are fully and efficiently employed, and the
  economy is producing as much as it can.
- A point **inside** the curve is **inefficient** — some resources are idle or misused; the economy
  could produce more of both goods. Unemployment shows up here.
- A point **outside** the curve is **unattainable** with the present resources and technology. It can
  be reached only if the economy **grows** — more resources or better technology — which shifts the
  whole curve outward.

The slope of the curve at any point measures the **marginal rate of transformation** (MRT) — the
amount of one good that must be given up to produce one more unit of the other. The MRT is simply the
marginal opportunity cost read off the curve, and its rising size is what makes the curve concave.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — reading opportunity cost from a schedule</div>
  An economy's production possibilities for wheat (tonnes) and cloth (units) are:<br />
  A (0, 30) · B (1, 27) · C (2, 21) · D (3, 12) · E (4, 0), each written as (wheat, cloth).<br />
  Opportunity cost of the 1st tonne (A→B): cloth falls 30 → 27, cost = <strong>3 units of cloth</strong><br />
  2nd tonne (B→C): 27 → 21, cost = 6 · 3rd tonne (C→D): 21 → 12, cost = 9 · 4th tonne (D→E): 12 → 0, cost = 12<br />
  The cost per tonne rises 3, 6, 9, 12 — <strong>increasing marginal opportunity cost</strong>, which
  is exactly why the curve is concave to the origin.
</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    A point inside the PPC does not mean the economy is producing "the wrong goods" — it means it is
    producing <strong>too little</strong>, because some resources sit idle. And a point outside the
    PPC is not merely expensive or difficult; with present resources and technology it is
    <strong>impossible</strong>, reachable only by shifting the whole curve out through growth. Mixing
    up "inefficient" (inside) with "unattainable" (outside) is the commonest PPC error.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. Positive and normative economics

Economic statements come in two kinds, and telling them apart is a favourite one-line question.

**Positive economics** deals with **what is, what was, or what will be** — statements of fact that can
in principle be **tested** against evidence. They are objective and carry no opinion. "A rise in the
price of a good reduces the quantity demanded of it" is positive: it describes a relationship that
data can confirm or refute.

**Normative economics** deals with **what ought to be** — statements of **value judgement**, of
opinion about what is desirable. They cannot be tested by facts, because they rest on someone's view
of what is good or fair. "The government should provide free healthcare to all" is normative: whether
it *should* is a matter of values, not of measurement.

The reliable signal is the vocabulary. Words such as **ought, should, must, good, bad, fair, unfair,
desirable** mark a normative statement; a positive statement simply describes or predicts without
judging. Positive economics tells you what *will* happen if a tax is raised; normative economics tells
you whether the tax *should* be raised.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Scan the statement for a value word. "Should", "ought to", "fair" and "too high" turn a sentence
    normative on their own. A number does not make a statement positive, and the absence of a number
    does not make it normative — "inflation is 6%" and "inflation is far too high" use the same fact,
    but the second passes judgement and is normative.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. The basic economic systems

An economic system is the arrangement a society uses to answer the three central problems of §4.
Three broad types are distinguished by **who owns the resources** and **who decides**.

**The capitalist (market) economy.** The means of production are **privately owned**, and the central
problems are settled by the **price mechanism** — the free interplay of demand and supply in markets
— with little direct government interference. Its features are private property, freedom of
enterprise and choice, the **profit motive** as the driving force, competition, and **consumer
sovereignty**, since goods are made because consumers are willing to pay for them. Its strength is
efficiency and the incentive to work and invest; its weakness is that it tends to produce **inequality
of income**, neglects goods that carry no profit, and can be unstable.

**The socialist (centrally planned or command) economy.** The means of production are **owned by the
state** on behalf of society, and a **central planning authority** — not the market — decides what,
how and for whom to produce, guided by **social welfare** rather than private profit. Its strength is
a more **equal distribution** of income and the absence of wasteful competition; its weakness is the
lack of the profit incentive, the heavy weight of bureaucracy, and little freedom of choice for the
consumer.

**The mixed economy.** Private and public sectors **coexist**: the market operates over much of the
economy for private profit, while the government owns key industries, regulates the private sector,
and steps in to correct the market's failures and to reduce inequality. It aims to keep the
efficiency of the market and the fairness of planning together. **India** is the standard example of
a mixed economy.

| System | Ownership of resources | Who answers the central problems | Driving motive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capitalist / market | private | the price mechanism (market forces) | private profit |
| Socialist / command | state / collective | a central planning authority | social welfare |
| Mixed | private and public together | market and government together | profit within a public framework |

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Consumer sovereignty belongs to the <strong>capitalist</strong> economy, where consumers' spending
    guides what is produced — not to the socialist economy, where the planning authority decides.
    And the mixed economy is defined by the <strong>coexistence</strong> of private and public sectors,
    not by the government owning everything; an option that describes the mixed economy as fully
    state-owned is describing socialism instead.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. The scope and role of business economics in decision-making

Business economics earns its place by turning economic theory into managerial decisions. Its scope is
usually mapped to a handful of decision areas inside a firm:

- **Demand analysis and forecasting** — estimating how much of the product buyers will want, and
  predicting future demand, so that output and capacity can be planned.
- **Production and cost analysis** — finding the combination of inputs that produces a given output
  at least cost, and understanding how costs behave as output changes.
- **Pricing decisions and policies** — setting the price and the pricing method that best serve the
  firm's goal in its market.
- **Profit management** — planning and measuring profit, the central objective of most firms, under
  conditions of uncertainty.
- **Capital management** — deciding how to invest the firm's funds among competing projects, a
  problem of capital budgeting.

Underneath these areas sit a few **fundamental concepts** that a manager uses again and again, and
which the paper names directly:

- The **opportunity cost principle** — every decision is judged against the best alternative given up.
- The **incremental (or marginal) principle** — a decision is worthwhile if the extra revenue it
  brings exceeds the extra cost it adds; compare the change, not the total.
- The **time perspective** — weigh both the short-run and the long-run effects of a decision, not one
  alone.
- The **discounting principle** — a rupee in the future is worth less than a rupee today, so future
  sums must be discounted to their present value before they are compared.
- The **equi-marginal principle** — a scarce resource is best spread across its uses so that the last
  rupee spent on each use brings the same marginal return.

The **role** these tools play is to help a manager make the **optimal decision under scarcity**: to
choose, plan ahead, and use limited resources where they yield most. Business economics is, in the
end, the discipline of applying the logic of scarcity and choice to the running of a firm.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Match the tool to the task. "A firm decides whether to accept a special order by comparing the
    <strong>extra</strong> revenue with the <strong>extra</strong> cost" is the incremental principle.
    "A firm compares a project's future returns brought back to today's value" is the discounting
    principle. These pairings — task to named concept — are exactly what the scope questions test.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. Basic terms: utility, price and the market

Three terms recur throughout the paper and are defined here once.

- **Utility** is the **want-satisfying power** of a good or service — the capacity of a commodity to
  satisfy a human want. It is **subjective**: the same good can carry different utility for different
  people, and even for the same person at different times. Utility is not the same as usefulness in
  the everyday sense, and it carries no moral judgement.
- **Price** is the **exchange value of a good expressed in money** — the amount of money that must be
  paid to obtain one unit of it. Price is what the buyer gives up and the seller receives per unit.
- A **market**, in economics, is **not a place** but an **arrangement** through which buyers and
  sellers come into contact to exchange a good or service at a price. The contact may be face to face
  or, just as validly, by telephone or over the internet, and the market may be **local, national or
  global** depending on how far buyers and sellers are spread.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    "A market is a place where goods are sold" is the everyday meaning, not the economic one. To an
    economist a market is any <strong>arrangement</strong> that brings buyers and sellers together for
    an exchange, with no need for a physical location — a share traded online is bought in a market
    that has no building at all.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 11. Negative marking and when to leave a question

Paper 4 is entirely objective and carries **negative marking of 0.25 mark for each wrong answer**. A
correct answer earns 1 mark, a wrong one costs 0.25, and an unattempted one scores 0.

The arithmetic is the same as for any objective paper. A blind guess among four options is right one
time in four: across four such guesses you expect 1 mark gained and 3 × 0.25 = 0.75 lost, a net of
0.25 marks for four questions — very slightly positive, but with a wide spread. What changes the
picture is **elimination**. Rule out one option and a guess among the remaining three is worth about
0.5 marks per four attempts; rule out two and a guess between the last two is worth about 1.5 marks
per four.

This chapter is unusually kind to elimination, because most distractors are built from a **named
confusion** — the wrong economist against a definition, "unattainable" where "inefficient" belongs,
a normative statement dressed as positive. A definition you know lets you strike two options on
sight, and the guess that remains is clearly worth making.

A calm working rule: **answer whenever you can eliminate at least one option, and consider leaving a
question only when you cannot narrow the field at all**. The marks at stake in either direction are
small, so nothing here needs to be done anxiously.

    "Economics is the science which studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce

  </div>

  <p>
    Classify each as positive or normative: (a) "A cut in the repo rate tends to raise borrowing."
    (b) "The rich ought to be taxed more heavily." (c) "Unemployment rose to 8% last year." (d) "The

  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — hunt for the value word</div>
    (a) Describes a relationship that data can test → <strong>positive</strong>.<br />
    (b) "ought to" is a value judgement → <strong>normative</strong>.<br />
    (c) A statement of measured fact → <strong>positive</strong>.<br />
    (d) "should" passes judgement on what is desirable → <strong>normative</strong>.<br />
    The pattern to carry away: (a) and (c) say what <em>is</em> or <em>will be</em>; (b) and (d) say
    what <em>ought</em> to be. The presence of "ought" and "should" settles it.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — opportunity cost from a production schedule (moderate)">
  <p>
    An economy can produce guns (units) and butter (kg) in these full-employment combinations:
    P (0, 40), Q (1, 36), R (2, 30), S (3, 22), T (4, 12). Find (a) the opportunity cost of the 3rd

    curve?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — opportunity cost is the butter sacrificed</div>
    (a) The 3rd gun is the move R → S: butter falls 30 → 22, so the opportunity cost of the 3rd gun is
    <strong>8 kg of butter</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — total sacrifice over several steps</div>
    (b) Q → T: butter falls 36 → 12, so the total given up is 36 − 12 = <strong>24 kg of butter</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — read the pattern</div>
    Gun by gun the butter cost is 4, 6, 8, 10 — rising each time. This <strong>increasing marginal
    opportunity cost</strong> is precisely the reason the Production Possibility Curve is
    <strong>concave to the origin</strong>.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — why the PPC bends the way it does (moderate)">
  <p>
    A student claims a Production Possibility Curve should be a straight line, "because giving up one
    good to get another is a fixed trade". Where is the reasoning wrong, and what shape is right?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — the hidden assumption</div>
    A straight-line PPC would mean a <strong>constant</strong> opportunity cost — every extra unit of
    one good costs the same amount of the other. That holds only if resources are equally efficient in
    both uses.<br />
    In fact resources are <strong>not</strong> equally suited to both goods, so as more of one good is
    made, less and less suitable resources are pushed into it and each extra unit costs more of the
    other. Opportunity cost <strong>rises</strong>, and a rising opportunity cost bends the curve
    <strong>concave to the origin</strong>. The straight-line answer is the special case of constant
    opportunity cost, not the general one.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — points on, inside and outside the curve (moderate)">
  <p>
    On a country's PPC for food and machines, point M lies on the curve, point N lies inside it and
    point W lies outside it. Describe the state of the economy at each, and say how W could ever be
    reached.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — three positions, three meanings</div>
    <strong>M (on the curve):</strong> resources are fully and efficiently employed — the economy is
    producing as much as it can and is <strong>efficient</strong>.<br />
    <strong>N (inside the curve):</strong> some resources are idle or misused — the economy is
    <strong>inefficient</strong> and could make more of both food and machines. Unemployment sits
    here.<br />
    <strong>W (outside the curve):</strong> <strong>unattainable</strong> with present resources and
    technology.<br />
    W becomes reachable only through <strong>economic growth</strong> — more resources or better
    technology — which shifts the entire curve outward until W lies on or inside the new frontier.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 6 — naming the economic system (easy-moderate)">
  <p>
    Identify the system: (a) all major industries are owned by the state and a planning commission
    fixes output targets; (b) firms are privately owned and prices are set by demand and supply with
    little government interference; (c) a large private sector operates for profit while the government
    runs key industries and regulates the rest.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — decide by ownership and who chooses</div>
    (a) State ownership plus a central planning authority → <strong>socialist / centrally planned
    economy</strong>.<br />
    (b) Private ownership plus the price mechanism → <strong>capitalist / market economy</strong>.<br />
    (c) Private and public sectors coexisting → <strong>mixed economy</strong>, of which India is the
    standard example.<br />
    The two questions that settle every such item are always the same: <em>who owns the resources</em>
    and <em>who answers the central problems</em>.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 7 — the opportunity cost of a business decision (moderate)">
  <p>
    Ravi closes a job that paid ₹ 6,00,000 a year to run a workshop full time. He also uses a shop he
    owns that could have been let for ₹ 90,000 a year, and puts in ₹ 3,00,000 of savings that had
    earned 7% a year. What is the opportunity cost of running the workshop for one year?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — add up the best alternatives given up</div>
    Salary forgone = ₹ 6,00,000<br />
    Rent forgone on the shop = ₹ 90,000<br />
    Interest forgone on the savings = 7% of 3,00,000 = ₹ 21,000<br />
    Opportunity cost for the year = 6,00,000 + 90,000 + 21,000 = <strong>₹ 7,11,000</strong><br />
    Each figure is a resource with a next-best use that Ravi has given up. The workshop is worth
    running only if it is expected to return more than ₹ 7,11,000 in the year.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 8 — microeconomics or macroeconomics? (easy)">
  <p>
    Classify each: (a) the wage paid to weavers in a Surat mill; (b) the country's rate of inflation;
    (c) the price of a cup of tea at a stall; (d) the level of total employment in the economy.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — one thing, or a total?</div>
    (a) One industry's wage → <strong>microeconomics</strong>.<br />
    (b) The general price level's rate of change → <strong>macroeconomics</strong>.<br />
    (c) The price of a single good → <strong>microeconomics</strong>.<br />
    (d) An economy-wide aggregate → <strong>macroeconomics</strong>.<br />
    "One of something" is micro; "a total or a general level" is macro. Micro is price theory, macro
    is the theory of income and employment.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 9 — the central problems in a real economy (moderate)">
  <p>
    A newly independent country must decide (i) whether to build more tractors or more textiles, (ii)
    whether to make cloth by hand looms or by power looms, and (iii) how the goods produced will be
    shared among its citizens. Name each central problem, and state the one cause behind all three.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — label each decision</div>
    (i) Choosing the goods and their quantities → <strong>what to produce</strong> (allocation of
    resources).<br />
    (ii) Choosing hand looms or power looms → <strong>how to produce</strong> (the technique).<br />
    (iii) Deciding how output is shared → <strong>for whom to produce</strong> (distribution).<br />
    The single cause behind all three is <strong>scarcity</strong> — wants exceed the resources
    available — and every economy, whatever its system, must answer the same three questions.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 10 — matching a task to a concept of business economics (moderate-hard)">
  <p>
    For each managerial task, name the fundamental concept of business economics being used: (a)
    accepting a bulk order only if the extra revenue beats the extra cost; (b) valuing a project's
    future cash returns in today's rupees before deciding; (c) judging a new product line against the
    best alternative use of the same funds; (d) spreading an advertising budget across media so the
    last rupee on each brings the same return.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — task to named principle</div>
    (a) Comparing the change in revenue with the change in cost → the <strong>incremental (marginal)
    principle</strong>.<br />
    (b) Bringing future sums back to present value → the <strong>discounting principle</strong>.<br />
    (c) Judging against the best forgone alternative → the <strong>opportunity cost principle</strong>.<br />
    (d) Equal marginal return from the last rupee on each use → the <strong>equi-marginal
    principle</strong>.<br />
    Each principle is scarcity and choice seen from a slightly different angle, which is why they are
    the working tools of a manager's decision.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes and examiner traps

- **Swapping the four definitions.** Wealth is Adam Smith, welfare is Marshall, "scarce means with
  alternative uses" is Robbins, growth over time is Samuelson. The examiner offers the right sentence
  against the wrong name.
- **Calling business economics the same as economics.** Business economics is the *application* of
  economic theory to a firm's decisions, not the whole science.
- **Mixing up micro and macro.** One consumer, one firm, one price is micro; national income, the
  general price level, total employment is macro. Micro is price theory; macro is income-and-
  employment theory.
- **Attaching the central problems to one system.** What, how and for whom face *every* economy; only
  the mechanism of answering them differs between capitalism, socialism and a mixed economy.
- **Adding up alternatives for opportunity cost.** Opportunity cost is the *single next best*
  alternative forgone, never the total of all the alternatives.
- **Confusing "inside" with "outside" the PPC.** Inside means inefficient — idle resources, could
  produce more. Outside means unattainable now — reachable only by growth. On the curve means
  efficient.
- **Thinking the PPC is a straight line.** A straight PPC would need constant opportunity cost.
  Because resources are not equally efficient in both uses, opportunity cost rises and the curve is
  concave to the origin.
- **Reading a normative statement as positive.** Words like *should, ought, fair, too high* mark a
  value judgement. A statement with a number can still be normative if it passes judgement.
- **Giving consumer sovereignty to the wrong system.** Consumer sovereignty belongs to the capitalist
  economy; in a socialist economy the planning authority decides.
- **Defining a market as a place.** In economics a market is any *arrangement* linking buyers and
  sellers for an exchange — no building required.
- **Forgetting who coined "micro" and "macro".** The terms are due to Ragnar Frisch, and Adam Smith
  is the father of economics — small attribution facts the paper likes.

## One-page revision summary

**Four definitions of economics.** Adam Smith → **wealth** (science of wealth; father of economics).
Marshall → **welfare** (the material requisites of well-being). Robbins → **scarcity** ("ends and
scarce means which have alternative uses"). Samuelson → **growth** (scarce resources used to produce
and distribute goods **over time**).

**Business (managerial) economics.** The application of economic theory and method to a firm's
decisions — integration of theory and practice for decision-making and forward planning. Mainly
microeconomic, pragmatic, normative in emphasis, goal-oriented.

**Micro and macro** (terms coined by Ragnar Frisch). Micro studies the **individual** unit — consumer,
firm, one price, one market — and is **price theory**. Macro studies the **aggregate** — national
income, general price level, total employment — and is the **theory of income and employment**.

**Central problems of every economy.** (1) *What* to produce and how much — allocation; (2) *How* to
produce — technique; (3) *For whom* to produce — distribution; plus efficient use of resources and
growth. All arise from **scarcity**, and every system must answer them.

**Scarcity, choice, opportunity cost.** Unlimited wants, limited resources with alternative uses →
choice. **Opportunity cost = the value of the next best alternative forgone** — the single best, not
the total; not always a money cost; faced by every agent.

**Production Possibility Curve.** Maximum combinations of two goods with given, fully and efficiently
employed resources and fixed technology. **Slopes downward** (more of one means less of the other);
**concave to the origin** (rising marginal opportunity cost, because resources are not equally
efficient). **On** the curve = efficient; **inside** = inefficient (idle resources); **outside** =
unattainable, reachable only by growth. Its slope is the **marginal rate of transformation**.

**Positive vs normative.** Positive = what **is / will be**, testable, objective. Normative = what
**ought to be**, a value judgement, untestable. Signal words: *should, ought, fair, desirable* →
normative.

**Economic systems.** Capitalist/market → private ownership, price mechanism, profit motive, consumer
sovereignty. Socialist/command → state ownership, central planning, social welfare. Mixed → private
and public sectors **coexist** (India). Every system answers the same central problems by a different
mechanism.

**Scope and role of business economics.** Decision areas: demand analysis and forecasting, production
and cost analysis, pricing, profit management, capital management. Fundamental concepts: opportunity
cost, incremental/marginal, time perspective, discounting, equi-marginal. Role: the optimal decision
under scarcity.

**Basic terms.** *Utility* = want-satisfying power (subjective). *Price* = exchange value in money per
unit. *Market* = an arrangement, not a place, linking buyers and sellers to exchange at a price.

**Marking.** 1 mark correct, −0.25 wrong, 0 unattempted. Guessing among four is close to neutral;
eliminating even one option makes an attempt clearly worthwhile.
