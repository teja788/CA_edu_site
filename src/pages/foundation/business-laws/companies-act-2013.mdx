Two brothers run a printing business in Coimbatore as a partnership. A machine they bought on credit
falls out of use, the supplier sues, and the decree runs against the brothers' house, their bank
balances and their gold. The following year the same two brothers register a private limited company,
subscribe ₹ 5,00,000 of share capital between them, and buy the same kind of machine on the same kind
of credit. This time the supplier sues, wins, and recovers only what the company has. The house is
untouched.

Nothing about the business changed. What changed is that a **new person** came into existence on the
day the Registrar issued the certificate of incorporation — a person who signs its own contracts,
owns its own machines, owes its own debts and can be sued in its own name. Every rule in this chapter
is a consequence of that one idea, or an exception carved out of it when someone abuses it.

This chapter is an **introduction** to the Companies Act 2013. It asks four questions and answers
them: *what is a company*, *what follows from being one*, *what kinds of company does the Act
recognise*, and *how is one brought into existence*. The Act itself has 470 sections and seven
schedules; the CA Intermediate paper on Corporate & Other Laws works through the incorporation
machinery, the alteration provisions and the doctrines in far more depth. At Foundation level the
examiner wants the **definitions applied to facts** — is this company private or public, small or
not, a subsidiary or an associate — and the arithmetic that goes with them. Learn the definitions as
**tests you run**, not paragraphs you recite.

The law here is stated **as it stands on 28 February 2026**, the cut-off for the **Sept 2026** and
**Jan 2027** attempts. One set of figures in this chapter — the small-company thresholds — lives in
the Rules and not in the Act, so it moves without any amendment to the Act. It is flagged where it
appears.

## 1. What a company is — sections 2(20) and 2(11)

The word "company" in ordinary speech means any largish business. The Act is much narrower.

**Company — section 2(20).** A company means a company **incorporated under this Act or under any
previous company law**. That is the whole definition, and it is deliberately circular: the only way
in is registration under the companies legislation. So a partnership firm, a limited liability
partnership, a co-operative society, a Hindu Undivided Family business, a trust or a sole proprietor
is **never a "company"** for this Act, however large the turnover and however corporate the letterhead
looks. A company registered under the Companies Act 1956, on the other hand, **is** a company today
and is governed by the 2013 Act, because 1956 is a "previous company law".

**Body corporate — section 2(11).** A wider bucket. "Body corporate" or "corporation" **includes a
company incorporated outside India**, but does **not** include:

- a **co-operative society** registered under any law relating to co-operative societies; and
- any **other body corporate**, not being a company as defined in this Act, which the **Central
  Government may notify** in the Official Gazette.

Put the two side by side and the picture is concentric:

| | **Body corporate — s. 2(11)** | **Company — s. 2(20)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Covers | Everything with corporate personality: Indian companies, foreign companies, LLPs, corporations created by a special Act | Only entities registered under the Companies Act 2013 or a previous company law |
| Foreign entities | **Included** | Excluded |
| Co-operative societies | **Excluded by name** | Excluded |
| Relationship | Every company is a body corporate | Most bodies corporate are not companies |

Where the Act wants to reach foreign parents and LLPs it says "body corporate". That is exactly why
the definitions of holding company and subsidiary company carry an Explanation saying that, for those
two clauses, "company" **includes any body corporate** — otherwise a foreign parent would escape.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A one-mark favourite: <strong>is a statutory corporation a company?</strong> The Reserve Bank of
    India, the Life Insurance Corporation and similar bodies are created by their own Acts of
    Parliament. They are <strong>bodies corporate</strong>, they have perpetual succession and a
    common seal, and they can sue and be sued — but they are <strong>not companies</strong> under
    s. 2(20), because they were never registered under any company law. Watch the stem for the words
    "registered under" versus "created by".
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. The characteristics of a company

Incorporation is not a formality; it produces a list of legal consequences. These eight are the
Foundation list, and a 4- to 6-mark question asks for them straight.

1. **Corporate personality — a separate legal entity.** On registration the company becomes a person
   in law, distinct from every member who owns it and every director who runs it. It holds property,
   makes contracts, and commits and suffers wrongs in its own name.
2. **Artificial legal person.** The company is a person created by law, not by nature. It has legal
   personality but no physical existence — no body to be imprisoned, no mind of its own, no capacity
   to take an oath. Everything it does, it does through **natural persons**: the Board, the officers,
   the agents. It is therefore often described as "an artificial person, invisible, intangible and
   existing only in contemplation of law".
3. **Perpetual succession.** Members die, become insolvent, retire or sell out; the company continues
   until it is wound up or struck off under the Act. Death or insolvency of a member changes only the
   register of members. The classroom line — "members may come and members may go, but the company
   goes on for ever" — is worth writing.
4. **Limited liability.** In a company limited by shares a member's liability is capped at the
   **amount unpaid on the shares he holds**. Once the shares are fully paid, he owes nothing further,
   whatever the company's debts. In a company limited by guarantee the cap is the **amount he
   undertook to contribute** in the memorandum. In an **unlimited company** there is no cap at all.
5. **Separate property.** The company's assets are the company's, not the members' proportionately.
   A member holding 99 per cent of the shares has **no insurable interest** in any particular company
   asset and cannot sell, mortgage or take it.
6. **Capacity to sue and be sued** in its own name. The corollary matters more: where a wrong is done
   to the company, the **company is the proper plaintiff**, and an individual member cannot ordinarily
   sue for it.
7. **Transferability of shares.** Shares are movable property, transferable in the manner provided by
   the articles. A **public** company's shares are **freely transferable**; a **private** company's
   articles must **restrict** transfer — and "restrict" means regulate, by pre-emption rights or a
   power in the Board to refuse, not prohibit altogether.
8. **Separation of ownership from management.** Members own the company; the **Board of Directors**
   manages it. A member, merely as a member, has no authority to bind the company or to act for it.

**And one former characteristic that is no longer one — the common seal.** Before 2015 the common
seal was the company's signature, and every deed had to carry it. The **Companies (Amendment) Act
2015 made the common seal optional**. A company may still have one, but where it has **not** got a
seal, a document that would have been executed under seal is signed by **two directors, or by one
director and the company secretary** where a company secretary has been appointed.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Do <strong>not</strong> list the common seal as an essential characteristic of a company. That is
    pre-2015 law and it is the single most common stale answer in this chapter. Say instead: the
    common seal is <strong>optional since the Companies (Amendment) Act 2015</strong>, and give the
    two-signature alternative. The same amendment removed the minimum paid-up capital requirement for
    private and public companies, so "a private company must have ₹ 1,00,000 of paid-up capital" is
    also stale — nothing is prescribed today.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Where the advantages end.** An answer that lists only advantages is half an answer. Incorporation
costs money and time, brings continuing compliance (statutory registers, filings, audit, meetings),
puts the company's affairs on a **public** file open to anyone, and — through the veil-lifting rules
in §4 — does not protect a member who uses the company for fraud or evasion. A company is also taxed
as a separate assessee, and its members are taxed again when profits are distributed.

## 3. Separate legal entity in action — sections 9 and the leading cases

**Section 9** is where the personality comes from. From the **date of incorporation mentioned in the
certificate**, the subscribers to the memorandum and all persons who from time to time become members
are a **body corporate** by the name contained in the memorandum, **capable of exercising all the
functions of an incorporated company** under the Act, having **perpetual succession**, with power to
**acquire, hold and dispose of property** (movable and immovable, tangible and intangible), to
**contract**, and to **sue and be sued**.

Three cases carry the principle, and each is examined by its proposition, not its facts.

- ***Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd***. A leather merchant sold his business to a company he formed,
  taking shares and **secured debentures** for the price; his wife and children held one share each.
  When the company failed, the unsecured trade creditors argued that the company was really Salomon
  himself and that he should not rank ahead of them. The House of Lords held the company was a
  **distinct person** properly formed under the Act, so Salomon was a **secured creditor** of it and
  was paid first. This is the foundation stone of company law: **the company is not the agent or
  trustee of its members**, however few they are.
- ***Lee v Lee's Air Farming Ltd***. Lee formed a crop-spraying company, held all but one of its
  shares, was its **governing director**, and was also employed by it as its **chief pilot**. He was
  killed while flying. His widow claimed compensation as the widow of a "worker". Held: the company
  and Lee were **two distinct persons**, so there was a valid contract of employment between them and
  the widow recovered. A person can be the controlling shareholder, the sole director and an employee
  of the same company at the same time.
- ***Sir Dinshaw Maneckjee Petit***, on the other side of the line. A wealthy individual formed four
  companies, transferred his investments to them and took the income back as "loans" that were never
  repaid, purely to reduce his tax. The court **disregarded the companies** as a device for evading
  tax. Separate personality is a shield; it is not a licence.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Every scenario in this chapter answers in the same two moves. <strong>Move 1 — state the
    rule:</strong> the company is a separate legal person distinct from its members
    (<em>Salomon</em>, s. 9), so prima facie the members are not liable for its debts and the company
    is not liable for theirs. <strong>Move 2 — ask whether any recognised ground for lifting the
    veil applies</strong>, and name it. An answer that jumps straight to "the veil will be lifted
    because it is a one-man company" loses most of the marks: a one-man company is exactly what
    <em>Salomon</em> upheld.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Lifting the corporate veil

The "veil" is the wall between the company and the people behind it. **Lifting** (or piercing) the
veil means looking through it — fixing the members or directors with liability, or attributing their
personal characteristics to the company. It is an **exception**, applied where the corporate form is
used as an instrument of fraud, evasion or improper conduct, and the courts have never reduced it to
a single formula. Group the grounds into two families and you can answer any question on it.

**A. Statutory grounds — where the Act itself pierces.** Here you do not argue equity; you cite the
section.

| Ground | What happens | Provision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Members below the statutory minimum** | If members fall below 7 (public) or 2 (private) and business is carried on for more than **6 months** while so reduced, every member who knows of it is **severally liable** for the whole of the debts contracted after those six months | s. 3A |
| **Incorporation by false information** | Where a company was got registered by furnishing false or incorrect information or by suppressing material facts, the promoters, the first directors and the persons making the declaration are liable for **fraud** | s. 7(7) read with s. 447 |
| **Misstatement in a prospectus** | Persons who authorised the issue of a prospectus containing an untrue statement are personally liable, criminally and civilly, to a subscriber who suffers loss | ss. 34 and 35 |
| **Fraudulent conduct of business** | Where business has been carried on with intent to defraud creditors, the Tribunal may declare the persons knowingly party to it **personally liable without any limitation of liability** | s. 339 |
| **Investigation into ownership** | The Central Government may appoint inspectors to determine the persons **really interested** in a company's shares and really able to control it | ss. 216 and 219 |
| **Ultra vires and unauthorised acts** | Directors who apply company funds outside the objects, or beyond their authority, are personally liable to restore them | ss. 4 and 166 read with the case law in §17 |

**B. Judicial grounds — where the courts pierce.** Named by case and proposition; the facts are
summarised in a line so that you can deploy them.

| Ground | Proposition | Anchor case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Protection of revenue** | A company formed only to reduce its controller's tax, with the income coming back to him as loans, is disregarded for that purpose | *Sir Dinshaw Maneckjee Petit* |
| **Fraud or improper conduct** | A company set up to evade an obligation the controller was already under — here a covenant not to solicit customers — is a mere cloak for him, and an injunction runs against both | *Gilford Motor Co v Horne* |
| **Evading a contract; sham or façade** | A seller who transferred land to a company he controlled to defeat a decree of specific performance failed; the court ordered performance against him **and** the company | *Jones v Lipman* |
| **Determination of enemy character** | In wartime a company registered in one country but controlled by residents of a hostile country may be treated as an **enemy**; the court looks at the nationality of those in control | *Daimler Co Ltd v Continental Tyre & Rubber Co* |
| **Avoidance of welfare or statutory liability** | A subsidiary formed only to divert turnover and depress workers' bonus entitlement was ignored, the two companies being treated as one | *Workmen of Associated Rubber Industry Ltd* |
| **Company acting as agent or trustee** | Where on the facts the company is genuinely carrying on the business of its members as their agent, the members are liable on the contracts it makes | decided case by case |

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Three wrong triggers that students write out of habit. <strong>(i) "It is a one-man
    company."</strong> That is <em>Salomon</em> itself, and it is not a ground. <strong>(ii) "It is a
    wholly-owned subsidiary, so parent and subsidiary are one."</strong> They are two persons; a
    holding company is not liable for its subsidiary's debts merely because it owns all the shares.
    <strong>(iii) "The company has become insolvent and cannot pay."</strong> Commercial failure is
    the risk creditors take; it is not fraud. The veil is lifted for a <strong>reason</strong> —
    fraud, evasion of an existing obligation, revenue evasion, enemy character, or an express
    statutory provision — and the answer must name it.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Vignette 1 — three claims against the same shareholder (moderate)">
  <p>
    Girish holds 96 per cent of the shares of <strong>Sthir Logistics Private Limited</strong> and is
    its managing director; his sister holds the balance. Three disputes arise in 2026.
  </p>
  <p>
    (a) The company defaults on an unsecured loan of <strong>₹ 42,00,000</strong> from a supplier. The
    supplier sues Girish personally, saying the company is "really him". (b) In 2024 Girish had sold
    his own warehouse to a buyer under an agreement for sale; in 2026, to avoid completing it, he
    transferred the warehouse to a second company, <strong>Sthir Estates Private Limited</strong>,
    which he alone owns and which was registered a fortnight earlier. The buyer sues for specific
    performance. (c) The company insures a fleet of trailers standing in Girish's own name and, when
    they burn, claims on the policy. Advise on each.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(a) — the rule, not the exception</div>
    <em>Salomon</em> and s. 9 answer this. Sthir Logistics is a person distinct from Girish; the debt
    is the company's, not his. That he owns 96 per cent is legally irrelevant — <em>Salomon</em> was
    decided on a company in which the founder held all but six shares. There is no fraud, no evasion
    of an existing personal obligation and no statutory ground pleaded, so the supplier recovers only
    from the company. <strong>Girish is not personally liable.</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(b) — a company formed to defeat an existing obligation</div>
    This is the <em>Jones v Lipman</em> pattern, and it is the opposite answer. Girish was already
    bound by the agreement for sale when he created and used Sthir Estates. The second company is a
    <strong>device or façade</strong> to escape that obligation, so the court will look through it and
    <strong>decree specific performance against Girish and against Sthir Estates</strong>. The
    <em>Gilford Motor v Horne</em> line is the same idea applied to a restrictive covenant.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(c) — separate property, and it cuts against the shareholder</div>
    The trailers stand in Girish's own name, so they are <strong>his</strong> property, not the
    company's. A company has <strong>no insurable interest</strong> in property belonging to a member,
    just as a member has none in the company's property. The insurer may repudiate for want of
    insurable interest. Separate personality is not a rule that always favours the company — here it
    defeats the claim.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">The pattern to carry into the exam</div>
    Same man, same two companies, three different answers. State <em>Salomon</em> first every time,
    then ask whether the facts show the company being used to <strong>escape an obligation that
    already existed</strong>. In (a) there was none; in (b) there was.
  </div>
</Callout>

## 5. Classification on the basis of incorporation

The Act recognises companies formed by registration, but the traditional classification names three
routes into corporate existence, and a one-mark question asks you to place an entity in the right box.

| Class | How it comes into existence | Examples and notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Chartered company** | By a **Royal Charter** granted by the Crown | Historical only. The East India Company and the Bank of England are the standard illustrations. **No chartered companies exist in India today**, the Crown having no such power here. |
| **Statutory company** | By a **special Act** of Parliament or a State legislature | The Reserve Bank of India, the Life Insurance Corporation of India, the Food Corporation of India. Governed primarily by their own Act; the Companies Act applies only so far as it is **not inconsistent** with that Act, and they are **bodies corporate but not "companies"** under s. 2(20). |
| **Registered company** | By **registration under the Companies Act 2013** (or a previous company law), on issue of the certificate of incorporation | Everything else — the overwhelming majority. This is the only class the rest of this chapter is about. |

## 6. Classification on the basis of liability

The second classification asks how much a member can be made to pay. Section 3(2) says a company may
be formed as any of three kinds, and the choice is recorded in the **liability clause** of the
memorandum.

- **Company limited by shares — section 2(22).** A company having the liability of its members
  limited by the memorandum **to the amount, if any, unpaid on the shares** respectively held by
  them. This is the ordinary commercial form.
- **Company limited by guarantee — section 2(21).** A company having the liability of its members
  limited by the memorandum to **such amount as the members may respectively undertake to contribute
  to the assets of the company in the event of its being wound up**. The guarantee is payable **only
  on winding up**, not while the company is a going concern. Clubs, trade associations, research
  bodies and most s. 8 companies take this form. A guarantee company **may or may not have share
  capital**; where it has, a member's liability is the unpaid amount on his shares **plus** the
  guaranteed amount.
- **Unlimited company — section 2(92).** A company **not having any limit on the liability of its
  members**. The members are liable to contribute, in a winding up, until the debts are paid in full
  — but note the two protections that survive: the company is still a **separate legal person**, so a
  creditor sues the **company**, not the members directly, and the members' liability is enforced
  through the **liquidator** in the winding up.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Two facts about a guarantee company that stems are built on. <strong>The guaranteed amount
    cannot be called up while the company is trading</strong> — a guarantee company that runs out of
    money mid-year cannot demand the guarantee from its members; only a liquidator can, and only in a
    winding up. And a <strong>guarantee company with share capital has two separate exposures</strong>:
    the unpaid amount on the shares (callable at any time) and the guarantee (callable only in a
    winding up). Do not add them into one figure.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. Private company — section 2(68)

A **private company** means a company which, **by its articles**:

1. **restricts the right to transfer its shares**;
2. **limits the number of its members to two hundred**, not counting persons in the employment of
   the company — and this limb **does not apply to a One Person Company**; and
3. **prohibits any invitation to the public** to subscribe for any securities of the company.

All three restrictions must be in the **articles**. A company whose articles are silent is not
private, whatever its size or its name, and a company whose name ends in "Private Limited" but whose
articles contain none of the three has a problem with its constitution, not a private-company status.

The clause opens with the words "having a **minimum paid-up share capital as may be prescribed**".
The fixed figure of ₹ 1,00,000 was **omitted by the Companies (Amendment) Act 2015** and nothing has
been prescribed since, so **there is no minimum paid-up share capital for a private company today**.

**Two counting rules sit inside limb 2, and between them they carry most of the questions.**

- **Joint holders count as ONE member.** Where two or more persons hold **one or more shares jointly**,
  they are treated as a **single member** for this clause.
- **Employee-members are EXCLUDED from the count.** Two categories are left out: (A) persons who are
  **in the employment of the company** and hold shares; and (B) persons who, **having been employees**
  of the company, **were members while in that employment** and have **continued to be members after
  the employment ceased**.

Three more points decide MCQs. The cap counts **members, not shares** — one person holding 40,000
shares is one member. **Debenture-holders are not members at all** and never enter the count.
And it is the restriction on transfer and the ban on public invitation, **not size**, that earn a
private company its statutory relaxations.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The ex-employee exclusion has <strong>three conditions</strong> and stems break one of them
    quietly. The person must (a) have been in the employment of the company, (b) have become a member
    <strong>while in that employment</strong>, and (c) have continued as a member after leaving. A
    retired employee who buys shares <em>after</em> resigning fails condition (b) and
    <strong>counts</strong> towards the 200. Read the stem for the word "while".
  </p>
</Callout>

  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 4 — the variation that flips it</div>
    Suppose the 17 joint holdings had instead been 17 <em>separate</em> holdings by 51 different
    individuals. The count becomes 118 + 51 + 4 = <strong>173</strong> — still inside the cap, but the
    headroom collapses from 61 to 27. Joint holding is a way of bringing people in without spending
    the cap.
  </div>
</Callout>

## 8. Public company — section 2(71)

A **public company** is defined negatively and then extended:

- it is a company **which is not a private company**; and
- it has such **minimum paid-up share capital as may be prescribed** — and here too the old figure of
  ₹ 5,00,000 was omitted in 2015 and nothing is currently prescribed.

Then comes the **proviso**, which produces more exam questions than the definition:

> A company which is a **subsidiary of a company, not being a private company**, shall be **deemed to
> be a public company** for the purposes of this Act **even where such subsidiary company continues
> to be a private company in its articles**.

Unpack it slowly. The parent must be a company that is **not private** — that is, a public company. If
it is, then its subsidiary is **public for the purposes of the Act** whatever the subsidiary's own
articles say and whatever its name says. The subsidiary keeps its restrictive articles and keeps
"Private Limited" at the end of its name; what it loses is the package of relaxations the Act gives
private companies. Had the parent itself been a **private** company, the proviso would not bite and
the subsidiary would stay private.

| | **Private company — s. 2(68)** | **Public company — s. 2(71)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Definition | Positive: three restrictions in the articles | Negative: a company that is not a private company |
| Minimum members to form | **2** (s. 3(1)(b)); 1 for an OPC | **7** (s. 3(1)(a)) |
| Maximum members | **200**, with the joint-holder and employee rules | **No limit** |
| Transfer of shares | **Restricted** by the articles | **Freely transferable** |
| Invitation to the public | **Prohibited** | Permitted, subject to the prospectus provisions |
| Name must end with | "Private Limited" | "Limited" |
| Minimum directors | **2** | **3** |
| Deeming | — | A subsidiary of a public company is **deemed public** whatever its articles say |

## 9. One Person Company — section 2(62)

**One Person Company** means a company which has **only one person as a member**. Three things follow,
and they are all that Foundation needs.

- **An OPC is a species of private company, not a fourth class.** It is formed as a private company
  under s. 3(1)(c) by **one person**, and every private-company provision applies to it unless the
  Act relaxes it. Its name carries **"(OPC) Private Limited"**.
- **The nominee.** The memorandum of an OPC must name **another person** who, with his **prior
  written consent** filed with the Registrar, becomes the member on the subscriber's **death or
  incapacity to contract**. The nominee is **not a member** while the subscriber lives; the member may
  change the nominee, and the nominee may withdraw, on intimation to the Registrar.
- **The 200-member limb of s. 2(68) is expressly disapplied** to an OPC, for the obvious reason that
  an OPC has exactly one member and could never engage a cap of 200.

Relaxations worth naming in an answer: an OPC need **not hold an annual general meeting**; its
financial statements need **not include a cash flow statement**; **one director** suffices; and where
there is only one director, a resolution entered in the minutes book and signed by him is enough.

## 10. Small company — section 2(85)

A **small company** means a company, **other than a public company**, which satisfies **both** of
these tests:

1. **paid-up share capital** does not exceed the prescribed amount; **and**
2. **turnover as per the profit and loss account for the immediately preceding financial year** does
   not exceed the prescribed amount.

On the position as on **28 February 2026** the prescribed figures are **₹ 4,00,00,000 of paid-up
share capital and ₹ 40,00,00,000 of turnover**. The Act itself only sets the ceilings up to which the
prescription may go (₹ 10,00,00,000 and ₹ 100,00,00,000); the operative figures are in the Rules.

And then the exclusion list. **Nothing in this clause applies to**:

- **(A)** a **holding company** or a **subsidiary company**;
- **(B)** a company registered under **section 8**; or
- **(C)** a company or **body corporate governed by any special Act**.

The exclusions are **absolute and are checked before the numbers**. A subsidiary with ₹ 1,00,000 of
capital and no turnover at all is not a small company, because it is a subsidiary.

**Why anyone cares.** Small-company status buys real relaxations: an **abridged annual return**, only
**two board meetings** a year, **no mandatory rotation of auditors**, **no cash flow statement** in
the financial statements, and **lower monetary penalties** under s. 446B. That is why examiners like
to give you a company that looks small and is not.

**The order of checking.** Run the test in this sequence and it becomes mechanical:

<div class="formula">Small company test: (1) Is it a PUBLIC company? If yes, stop — not small. (2) Is it a holding or subsidiary company, a s. 8 company, or governed by a special Act? If yes, stop — not small. (3) Is paid-up capital ≤ the prescribed limit? (4) Is turnover for the IMMEDIATELY PRECEDING financial year ≤ the prescribed limit? ## 11. Holding, subsidiary and associate — sections 2(46), 2(87) and 2(6)

Classification **on the basis of control** is where the arithmetic lives.

**Holding company — section 2(46).** In relation to one or more other companies, a **holding company**
means a company **of which such companies are subsidiary companies**. The definition is entirely
derivative: work out the subsidiary first and the holding company falls out of it. The **Explanation**
adds that, for this clause and for s. 2(87), the expression "company" **includes any body corporate**
— so a foreign parent counts.

**Subsidiary company — section 2(87).** In relation to another company (that is, the holding company),
a **subsidiary** means a company in which the holding company:

- **(i) controls the composition of the Board of Directors**; **or**
- **(ii) exercises or controls more than one-half of the total voting power**, either **on its own**
  or **together with one or more of its subsidiary companies**.

The two limbs are **alternatives**. Either one alone makes the company a subsidiary; you never need
both.

Three further pieces of the clause:

- **Control of Board composition.** A company controls the composition of another company's Board if
  it can, **at its discretion, appoint or remove all or a majority of the directors**. The right to
  appoint 4 of 9 directors is not control; the right to appoint 5 of 9 is. Note that this limb has
  nothing to do with shareholding at all — a company holding **no shares** can be a holding company
  through a Board-appointment right in the articles or a shareholders' agreement.
- **Control through another subsidiary — the Explanation.** A company is deemed to be a subsidiary of
  the holding company **even if the control is exercised by another subsidiary** of that holding
  company. So if A controls B and B controls C, then **C is a subsidiary of A**, without A holding a
  single share in C.
- **Layers.** Prescribed classes of holding companies may not have layers of subsidiaries beyond the
  prescribed number. The detail is Rules-level and belongs to the Intermediate paper; at Foundation,
  know that a restriction exists.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Two errors, and both are engineered into the options.</strong> First, the test in
    s. 2(87)(ii) is <strong>total VOTING POWER, not total share capital</strong> — the words were
    changed by the Companies (Amendment) Act 2017. Preference shares that carry no vote except on
    resolutions directly affecting them sit in the <em>capital</em> base but not in the
    <em>voting</em> base, so a stem that hands you equity capital, preference capital and a
    percentage is asking you to strip the preference capital out before you divide. Second,
    <strong>percentages are never multiplied down a chain</strong>: if A controls 60 per cent of B and
    B controls 55 per cent of C, do not compute 33 per cent and answer "associate". The Explanation
    makes C a subsidiary of A outright. Control is tested step by step down the chain, never diluted
    through it.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Associate company — section 2(6).** In relation to another company, an **associate company** means a
company in which that other company has **significant influence**, but which is **not a subsidiary**
of the company having such influence — **and includes a joint venture company**. The Explanation
defines the two limbs:

- **"Significant influence"** means **control of at least twenty per cent of total voting power**, or
  **control of or participation in business decisions under an agreement**;
- **"joint venture"** means a joint arrangement whereby the parties having joint control of the
  arrangement have rights to the **net assets** of the arrangement.

Read the three moving parts. **"At least twenty per cent"** makes exactly 20.00 per cent an associate
relationship — the boundary is **inclusive**, and it is deliberately different from the "**more than**
one-half" in s. 2(87)(ii). The words **"but which is not a subsidiary"** mean the tests run **in
order**: subsidiary first, and associate only if the subsidiary test has failed. And the
**agreement limb** means a small shareholding with a contractual say in business decisions can still
create an associate relationship.

<div class="formula">Run the control tests in this order: (1) Does it control the composition of the Board, or more than one-half of TOTAL VOTING POWER, on its own or with its subsidiaries? → SUBSIDIARY (s. 2(87)); the other company is the holding company (s. 2(46)). (2) If not — does it control at least 20% of total voting power, or business decisions under an agreement? → ASSOCIATE (s. 2(6)). (3) Neither → merely an investment.</div>

## 12. Government company and foreign company — sections 2(45) and 2(42)

**Government company — section 2(45).** A Government company means any company in which **not less
than fifty-one per cent of the paid-up share capital** is held by:

- the **Central Government**; or
- any **State Government or Governments**; or
- **partly by the Central Government and partly by one or more State Governments**;

**and includes a company which is a subsidiary company of such a Government company.**

Three details, and each of them is a whole question:

1. The test is **paid-up share capital** — this is the one control-style definition in the chapter
   that is *not* run on voting power. Do not import the s. 2(87) base into it.
2. Central and State holdings are **aggregated**. A company in which the Centre holds 22 per cent and
   two States hold 17 and 14 per cent is a Government company at 53 per cent, though no single
   Government holds a majority.
3. **Not less than 51 per cent** means exactly 51 per cent qualifies. And a **subsidiary of a
   Government company is itself a Government company** by the closing words — by inclusion, not by
   any deeming fiction.

A Government company is an ordinary company under the Act: it is registered under the Act, it is not
a statutory corporation, and the Companies Act applies to it in full except where the Central
Government has exempted it by notification.

**Foreign company — section 2(42).** A foreign company means any **company or body corporate
incorporated outside India** which:

- **(a)** has a **place of business in India**, whether by itself or through an agent, physically or
  through **electronic mode**; **and**
- **(b)** **conducts any business activity in India** in any other manner.

Both limbs are needed, joined by **"and"**. So a foreign body corporate with a representative office
in India that does no business here falls outside; and one that sells into India from abroad with no
place of business here also falls outside. Note that the clause says "company **or body corporate**",
so a foreign LLP-type entity can be a foreign company for this purpose.

**Listed company — section 2(52)**, for completeness, because it comes up in the same list: a company
which has **any of its securities listed on any recognised stock exchange**. "Any securities" — a
company whose **debentures alone** are listed is prima facie a listed company; an equity listing is
not required. A proviso lets the Central Government prescribe classes of companies which, though
listed, are not to be considered listed companies; that carve-out list is Rules-level and belongs to
the Intermediate paper.

## 13. Section 8 company — charitable objects and no dividend

**Section 8(1)** lets the Central Government, by licence, allow an association to be **registered as a
limited company without the words "Limited" or "Private Limited"** in its name, where the Central
Government is satisfied that the proposed company:

- **(a)** has in its objects the promotion of **commerce, art, science, sports, education, research,
  social welfare, religion, charity, protection of the environment or any such other object**;
- **(b)** intends to **apply its profits, if any, or other income in promoting only those objects**;
  and
- **(c)** intends to **prohibit the payment of any dividend to its members**.

The three conditions are cumulative. Limb (c) is the one that decides most questions: a s. 8 company
**may earn profits**, and often does — what it may not do is **distribute** them. A body that intends
to pay a dividend, however charitable its objects, cannot be a s. 8 company.

What follows from the licence:

- The company enjoys **all the privileges and is subject to all the obligations of limited
  companies** (s. 8(2)).
- A **firm may be a member** of a s. 8 company (s. 8(3)) — an exception worth remembering, because a
  firm is not a person capable of holding shares in an ordinary company in its own name.
- The **memorandum and articles may be altered only with the previous approval of the Central
  Government** (s. 8(4)(i)), and conversion into any other kind of company is possible only on the
  prescribed conditions (s. 8(4)(ii)).
- The licence may be **revoked** where the company contravenes s. 8 or the conditions of the licence,
  or where its affairs are conducted **fraudulently**, **contrary to its objects** or
  **prejudicially to the public interest**, after giving the company notice and an opportunity of
  being heard; the Registrar then enters "Limited" or "Private Limited" against its name (s. 8(6)).
- A s. 8 company may **amalgamate only with another s. 8 company having similar objects** (s. 8(10)).
- A s. 8 company can **never be a small company** — exclusion (B) in s. 2(85).

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The three quick facts about a s. 8 company that stems test. It is registered <strong>as a limited
    company</strong> (usually limited by guarantee) but drops the word "Limited" from its name — so a
    body whose name gives no clue may still be a company. It <strong>may make a profit</strong>; the
    prohibition is on <strong>distribution</strong>. And because the licence is what makes it a s. 8
    company, its <strong>revocation</strong> is what unmakes it — not any resolution of the members.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 14. The other special classes — dormant, Nidhi and producer companies

Three more classes complete the Foundation list.

**Dormant company — section 455.** Where a company is formed and registered under this Act **for a
future project or to hold an asset or intellectual property** and has **no significant accounting
transaction**, or is an **inactive company**, it may make an application to the Registrar in the
prescribed manner for the status of a **dormant company**. Two definitions inside the Explanation:

- An **inactive company** is one which has **not been carrying on any business or operation**, or has
  **not made any significant accounting transaction during the last two financial years**, or has
  **not filed financial statements and annual returns during the last two financial years**.
- A **significant accounting transaction** means any transaction **other than** (i) payment of fees to
  the Registrar, (ii) payments made to fulfil the requirements of the Act or any other law, (iii)
  allotment of shares to fulfil the requirements of the Act, and (iv) payments for the maintenance of
  its office and records.

The Registrar keeps a **register of dormant companies**, and a dormant company files a return and
holds the reduced number of board meetings the Act prescribes. Dormancy is a **status applied for**,
not a state a company drifts into.

**Nidhi company — section 406.** A **Nidhi** is a company which the Central Government has declared to
be a Nidhi or Mutual Benefit Society by notification, incorporated **with the object of cultivating
the habit of thrift and savings amongst its members**, receiving deposits from and lending to **its
members only**, for their mutual benefit. The defining feature at Foundation level is the phrase
**"members only"**: a Nidhi deals with nobody else. Nidhis are public companies limited by shares and
must carry **"Nidhi Limited"** in their name.

**Producer company — Chapter XXIA, sections 378A to 378ZU.** A producer company is a body corporate
having objects or activities specified in the Act and registered as a producer company, formed by
**primary producers** — farmers, growers, fishermen, artisans, and their associations. The essentials
for a one-mark answer:

- It may be formed by **ten or more individuals**, each of them a producer, or by **two or more
  producer institutions**, or by a combination of the two.
- Its objects run to **production, harvesting, procurement, grading, pooling, handling, marketing,
  selling and export** of the primary produce of its members, and the import of goods or services for
  their benefit.
- **Only producers or producer institutions may be members**, and its shares are **not freely
  transferable** — they may be transferred only with the approval of the Board and only to another
  active member.
- It is treated as a **private company** for the purposes of the Act, but the **200-member cap does
  not apply** to it, and it can never become a public company by any deeming.

## 15. Formation of a company — sections 3 and 3A

**Section 3(1)** — a company may be formed **for any lawful purpose** by persons **subscribing their
names to a memorandum** and complying with the registration requirements of the Act:

| Company to be formed | Minimum number of subscribers | Provision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Public** company | **Seven or more** persons | s. 3(1)(a) |
| **Private** company | **Two or more** persons | s. 3(1)(b) |
| **One Person Company** (which is a private company) | **One** person | s. 3(1)(c) |

"Persons" is not confined to natural persons: a **body corporate may subscribe** to the memorandum of
an ordinary company. An OPC is the exception — it needs a natural person.

**Section 3(2)** asks a different question. Not *how many subscribe*, but *how much they risk*: a
company may be formed as a company **limited by shares**, **limited by guarantee** (with or without
share capital), or **unlimited**, as set out in §6. The two questions are independent, so a private
company limited by guarantee and an OPC limited by shares are both perfectly ordinary combinations.

**Section 3A — members falling below the statutory minimum.** If at any time the number of members
falls **below seven** in the case of a public company or **below two** in the case of a private
company, and the company **carries on business for more than six months** while the number is so
reduced, then every person who is a member during that time **after those six months** and who is
**cognisant of the fact** is **severally liable for the whole of the debts** contracted during that
time, and may be **severally sued** for them.

Four traps live in that sentence, and they are all examined:

1. The company **does not dissolve** and does not stop being a company. Only the member's shield goes.
2. Liability attaches **only after the six months expire**, and only to **debts contracted while the
   shortfall continues** after that point.
3. Only members who are **cognisant of the fact** are caught. A member who genuinely does not know is
   not liable.
4. Liability is **several**, not joint — a creditor may sue any one such member for the whole debt.

## 16. Registration and incorporation in outline — section 7

Section 7 is the machinery. At Foundation level you need the sequence and the effect, not the forms;
the CA Intermediate paper works through every document, every declaration and the consequences of a
false one.

**Step 1 — the promoters.** A **promoter** is the person who conceives the idea of the company, takes
the steps to bring it into existence, arranges the capital and the first directors, and prepares the
documents. Section 2(69) defines a promoter as a person **named as such in a prospectus or identified
by the company in its annual return**, or who has **control over the affairs of the company**,
directly or indirectly, or **in accordance with whose advice, directions or instructions the Board is
accustomed to act** — with a proviso excluding a person acting **merely in a professional capacity**.
So the solicitor who drafts the memorandum and the chartered accountant who certifies a valuation are
not promoters; the same solicitor who also selects the first directors and negotiates the purchase of
the business is.

A promoter stands in a **fiduciary position** towards the company he is bringing into existence. He
is neither its agent (there is no principal yet) nor its trustee (there is no trust), but he must
make **no secret profit**, must **disclose** any interest in a transaction with the company, and must
**account** for what he receives. A promoter has **no legal right to preliminary expenses or
remuneration**; an article authorising payment creates no contract in his favour.

**Contracts made before incorporation** are the classic Foundation point. Because the company does
not exist, such a contract is **not binding on the company**, and — the part students miss — the
company **cannot ratify it after incorporation**, because ratification requires a principal in
existence at the time of the act. The **promoters remain personally liable**. The company becomes
bound only by entering into a **fresh contract** after incorporation.

**Step 2 — the filings under s. 7(1).** An application is filed with the **Registrar within whose
jurisdiction the registered office of the company is proposed to be situated**, with:

1. the **memorandum and articles**, signed by all the subscribers in the prescribed manner;
2. a **declaration by a professional** engaged in the formation — an advocate, chartered accountant,
   cost accountant or company secretary **in practice** — and by a person named in the articles as a
   **director, manager or secretary**, that all the requirements of the Act as to registration have
   been complied with;
3. a **declaration by each subscriber and each first director** that he has not been convicted of any
   offence in connection with the promotion, formation or management of any company, and has not been
   found guilty of fraud or breach of duty to any company in the preceding **five years**, and that
   the documents filed contain **correct and complete information**;
4. the **address for correspondence** until the registered office is established;
5. **particulars and proof of identity** of every subscriber;
6. **particulars of the first directors**, with their **consent to act**.

**Step 3 — registration and its effect.** The Registrar registers the documents, issues the
**certificate of incorporation** (s. 7(2)) and allots a **Corporate Identity Number** as the
company's distinct identity (s. 7(3)). From the **date of incorporation mentioned in the
certificate**, s. 9 gives the company its corporate personality, perpetual succession and power to
hold property, contract, and sue and be sued.

**Section 7(7) — incorporation by false information.** Where a company has been got incorporated by
furnishing **false or incorrect information**, or by **suppressing any material fact**, the Tribunal
may, on an application, pass orders that include **regulating the management** of the company,
**making the members' liability unlimited**, **removing the name** of the company from the register,
**winding it up**, or any other order it thinks fit. This is a statutory lifting of the veil, and it
is the reason the declarations in step 2 matter.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A registered office is <strong>not</strong> needed on the day of incorporation. Under
    <strong>s. 12</strong> the company must have a registered office capable of receiving
    communications <strong>within thirty days of its incorporation</strong>, and must notify the
    Registrar of its situation within the same period. That is why s. 7(1) asks only for an
    <em>address for correspondence</em> at the filing stage. The detail of s. 12 — painting the name,
    the shift within and beyond the same city, the State-to-State change — belongs to the
    Intermediate paper.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 17. The memorandum and the articles in outline — sections 4, 5 and 6

**The memorandum of association** is the company's **charter**. It fixes the company's identity, its
home State, what it may do, and how far its members are exposed. Its most important function is
**external**: it tells the outside world the **limits of the company's capacity**. Section 4(1)
requires it to state:

| Clause | What it states | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Name** | The name of the company, ending with **"Limited"** for a public company or **"Private Limited"** for a private one | A s. 8 company is exempt from the suffix |
| **Registered office** | The **State** in which the registered office is to be situated | The State, not the street address |
| **Objects** | The objects for which the company is proposed to be incorporated, and any matter considered necessary in furtherance of them | A single clause; the old main / ancillary / other objects split is gone |
| **Liability** | Whether the liability of members is limited or unlimited, and if limited, whether by shares or by guarantee, with the amount | See §6 |
| **Capital** | For a company having share capital: the **authorised capital**, divided into shares of a fixed amount, and the number of shares each subscriber takes — **not less than one share each** | No minimum paid-up capital exists any more |
| **Nominee** | For an OPC only: the person who becomes the member on the subscriber's death | See §9 |

The memorandum is signed by each subscriber, who states the number of shares he takes, and it must be
in the respective form in **Tables A to E of Schedule I** according to the kind of company.

**The articles of association — section 5.** If the memorandum says what the company **is**, the
articles say how it is to be **run**: issue and transfer of shares, calls, general meetings and
voting, appointment and powers of directors, borrowing, dividends and accounts. The articles are
**subordinate to the memorandum** and to the Act. Model articles are set out in **Tables F to J of
Schedule I**, and a company may adopt all or any of them; for a company registered after the
commencement of the Act, the model articles apply automatically **to the extent the registered
articles do not exclude or modify them**.

**Section 6 fixes the hierarchy.** The Act **overrides** anything to the contrary contained in the
memorandum, the articles, any agreement executed by the company or any resolution of the members or
the Board, whenever made, and any such provision is **void to the extent of the repugnancy**.

<div class="formula">Order of authority: THE ACT beats the MEMORANDUM, which beats the ARTICLES, which beat any agreement or resolution. An act outside the memorandum is ultra vires the COMPANY and void; an act within the memorandum but outside the articles is merely an internal irregularity, which the members can ratify.</div>

| | **Memorandum of association** | **Articles of association** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it does | Defines the company's **identity and capacity** — its constitution towards the outside world | Contains the **internal regulations** for management |
| Relationship | **Supreme**, subject only to the Act | **Subordinate** to the memorandum and the Act |
| Consequence of breach | An act beyond the memorandum is **ultra vires the company and void**; it **cannot be ratified**, even unanimously | An act beyond the articles but inside the memorandum is an **irregularity** and **can be ratified** by the members |
| Alteration | Only in the manner and to the extent the Act allows, and some alterations need approval | Alterable by **special resolution**, subject to the Act and the memorandum |
| Compulsory? | Every company must have one | Every company must have one; model articles apply so far as not excluded |

## 18. The three doctrines

**Doctrine of ultra vires.** *Ultra vires* means "beyond the powers". The **objects clause** of the
memorandum fixes the company's **capacity**, and an act outside it is **ultra vires the company** and
**void**. The leading case is ***Ashbury Railway Carriage & Iron Co Ltd v Riche***, where a company
whose objects were to make and sell railway carriages contracted to **finance the construction of a
railway line**; the contract was held ultra vires and void, and the later assent of every shareholder
could not save it. That is the sting of the doctrine: an ultra vires act is **incapable of
ratification even by the unanimous vote of all the members**, because members cannot confer on the
company a capacity its constitution never gave it.

The doctrine is applied **reasonably**, not literally: whatever is **fairly incidental to or
consequential upon** the stated objects is *intra vires*. A manufacturing company may open a bank
account and insure its plant without saying so in its objects.

Consequences worth stating in an answer:

- **Injunction** — any member may sue to restrain the company from a threatened ultra vires act.
- **Personal liability of the directors** — they must **restore** the funds applied ultra vires and
  compensate the company for the loss, since it is their duty to keep within the objects.
- **Ultra vires borrowing** — the lender **cannot sue the company** on a void loan, but equity allows
  him to **trace** his money, or the property bought with it, while it remains identifiable, and to
  be **subrogated** to the position of a lawful creditor whose valid debt was paid off with it.
- **Ultra vires acquired property** remains the **company's property** and is protected as such. The
  invalidity attaches to the transaction, not to the asset.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Keep three "beyond the powers" situations apart, because only the first is ultra vires the
    company. <strong>(i) Beyond the MEMORANDUM</strong> — ultra vires the company, void, and
    <strong>cannot be ratified by anybody</strong>. <strong>(ii) Beyond the ARTICLES but within the
    memorandum</strong> — an irregularity, and the members <strong>can ratify</strong> it, usually by
    altering the articles. <strong>(iii) Beyond the DIRECTORS' authority but within both</strong> —
    the company itself may ratify it in general meeting. Students collapse all three into "ultra
    vires" and lose the ratification mark every time.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Doctrine of constructive notice.** The memorandum and the articles are **public documents**, filed
with the Registrar and open to inspection by anyone on payment of the prescribed fee. Every person
dealing with the company is therefore **deemed to have notice of their contents** — and, harsher
still, deemed to have **read and understood them properly** — whether or not he ever looked at them. A
lender to a company whose articles cap borrowing at ₹ 50,00,000 cannot say he did not know of the cap.
The doctrine **protects the company** against outsiders.

**Doctrine of indoor management — the rule in *Royal British Bank v Turquand*.** Constructive notice
would make dealing with companies impossibly risky, so the courts supply a counterweight. In
*Turquand's* case the company's constitution allowed the directors to borrow on bonds **such sums as
were authorised by a resolution of the general meeting**; the directors gave a bond without any such
resolution having been passed. The lender still recovered: it knew of the requirement (constructive
notice), but it was entitled to **presume that the resolution had duly been passed**, that being an
**indoor** matter it had no means of checking.

So an outsider is bound by what the company's constitution **says**, but may assume that the
**internal procedure** the constitution requires has been complied with. The doctrine **protects the
outsider**, and it is the mirror image of constructive notice.

**The exceptions**, which are examined as a list:

1. **Knowledge of the irregularity.** A person who actually knows that the internal procedure was not
   followed cannot rely on the rule. This typically catches **insiders** — a director or officer
   dealing with his own company.
2. **Suspicion of irregularity, or negligence.** Where the circumstances are so unusual that a
   reasonable person would have made enquiries — a sole director paying company cheques into his own
   personal account, or a junior officer purporting to do something such an officer never does — the
   outsider who makes no enquiry gets no protection.
3. **Forgery.** A forged document is a **nullity**. There is no company act at all for the rule to
   regularise, so a share certificate issued under a forged signature confers nothing, however
   innocent the holder.
4. **No knowledge of the articles.** A person who never read the articles and did not know of them
   cannot say that they induced him to assume that some officer had been delegated authority.
5. **Acts void in themselves**, including acts **ultra vires the company**. The rule cures a defect
   of **procedure**, never a want of **capacity**.

<Callout kind="example" label="Vignette 5 — the three doctrines on one set of facts (hard)">
  <p>
    The memorandum of <strong>Trilok Papers Private Limited</strong> confines its objects to
    manufacturing and trading in paper products. Its articles allow the Board to borrow beyond
    <strong>₹ 25,00,000</strong> only after an ordinary resolution of the members. Four things happen
    in 2026.
  </p>
  <p>
    (a) The Board borrows <strong>₹ 40,00,000</strong> from <strong>Ashwath Traders</strong> on a bond
    signed by two directors; no members' resolution was ever passed, and Ashwath did not know that. (b)
    The company, with the recorded unanimous assent of all its members at a general meeting, spends
    <strong>₹ 90,00,000</strong> acquiring a chain of restaurants. (c) A clerk forges the company
    secretary's signature on a share certificate for 5,000 shares, which is handed to Ashwath. (d)
    <strong>Bhavesh</strong>, one of the company's own directors, lends it
    <strong>₹ 30,00,000</strong> on identical terms to Ashwath's bond, again with no members'
    resolution. Advise on each.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(a) — constructive notice, then indoor management</div>
    The articles are a public document, so Ashwath is <strong>deemed to know</strong> of the
    ₹ 25,00,000 threshold and cannot plead ignorance of it. But whether the members actually passed
    the resolution is an <strong>indoor</strong> matter he had no means of verifying, so
    <em>Turquand's</em> rule lets him presume it was passed. <strong>The company is bound and
    Ashwath may enforce the bond for ₹ 40,00,000.</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(b) — ultra vires, and the assent is worthless</div>
    Restaurants are neither within the objects nor fairly incidental to paper manufacture, so the
    acquisition is <strong>ultra vires the company and void</strong>. The unanimous shareholder assent
    changes nothing — <em>Ashbury v Riche</em>. The <strong>directors are personally liable</strong>
    to restore the ₹ 90,00,000 and make good the loss, any member may seek an
    <strong>injunction</strong> against further steps, and whatever was in fact acquired remains the
    company's property.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(c) — forgery cuts off Turquand entirely</div>
    A forged signature makes the certificate a <strong>nullity</strong>. There is no act of the
    company for the indoor-management rule to regularise, so exception 3 applies and
    <strong>Ashwath takes no title to the 5,000 shares</strong>, however innocent he was. Note the
    contrast with (a): the same person wins on the bond and loses on the certificate, because a
    procedural gap and a forgery are different things.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(d) — the insider exception</div>
    Bhavesh is a <strong>director</strong>. He is taken to know whether the members resolved, so he
    falls inside exception 1 and <strong>cannot rely on <em>Turquand's</em> rule</strong>. His loan is
    not enforceable against the company on the bond, though he may have a claim in restitution for
    money the company actually received and used. Identical facts, opposite answers, and the only
    difference is who the lender was.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

- **Listing the common seal as an essential characteristic.** It has been **optional since the
  Companies (Amendment) Act 2015**. Where there is no seal, two directors, or one director and the
  company secretary, sign instead.
- **"A private company must have ₹ 1,00,000 of paid-up capital."** The minimum paid-up capital
  requirements for both private and public companies were **omitted in 2015** and nothing has been
  prescribed since.
- **Calling a statutory corporation a company.** The RBI and the LIC are **bodies corporate created
  by a special Act**, not companies under s. 2(20). Read the stem for "registered under" versus
  "created by".
- **Treating a co-operative society or an LLP as a body corporate under s. 2(11).** A **co-operative
  society is expressly excluded** from the definition, though an LLP is within it.
- **Lifting the veil because the company is small, one-man or a wholly-owned subsidiary.**
  *Salomon* upheld exactly that. The veil is lifted for a **named ground** — fraud, evasion of an
  existing obligation, revenue evasion, enemy character, or an express statutory provision.
- **Counting joint holders separately for the 200-member cap.** Two or more persons holding shares
  jointly are **one member** for s. 2(68)(ii).
- **Counting employee-members.** Current employees who hold shares, and ex-employees who **became
  members while employed** and continued after leaving, are **excluded**. An ex-employee who buys in
  **after** leaving is counted.
- **Counting debenture-holders as members.** They are creditors, not members, and never enter the
  count.
- **Forgetting the deemed-public proviso to s. 2(71).** A "Private Limited" **subsidiary of a public
  company** is a public company for the Act. Its name and its articles do not save it.
- **Using paid-up share capital for the subsidiary test.** Section 2(87)(ii) has run on **total
  voting power** since the 2017 amendment. Strip non-voting preference capital out before dividing.
  But note that **s. 2(45) does use paid-up capital** — the bases are deliberately different.
- **Multiplying percentages down a chain.** 60 per cent of B and B's 55 per cent of C does not make
  33 per cent of C. The Explanation to s. 2(87) makes C a subsidiary of A outright.
- **Calling a chain company an "associate".** Section 2(6) excludes subsidiaries expressly. Test
  subsidiary first; only if that fails do you ask about significant influence.
- **Reading "at least twenty per cent" as "more than twenty per cent".** Exactly 20.00 per cent of
  total voting power creates an **associate** relationship. Compare s. 2(87), which needs **more
  than** one-half.
- **Treating small-company status as purely numerical.** A public company, a holding or subsidiary
  company, a s. 8 company and a special-Act company are out **regardless of size**, and the
  exclusions are checked first.
- **Applying the small-company turnover test to the current year.** The clause says the
  **immediately preceding** financial year, per the profit and loss account.
- **Quoting the small-company figures as if they were in the Act.** They are prescribed by
  **Rules** and have already moved twice.
- **"A section 8 company cannot make a profit."** It can. What it cannot do is **distribute** profit
  as dividend. And it can never be a small company.
- **Reading s. 2(42) with "or".** A foreign company needs a **place of business in India** **and**
  **business activity in India**. Either limb alone is not enough.
- **Thinking a dormant company becomes dormant automatically.** Dormancy under s. 455 must be
  **applied for** and the company entered in the register of dormant companies.
- **Saying a pre-incorporation contract can be ratified after incorporation.** It cannot — there was
  no principal in existence. The promoters stay personally liable, and the company must enter into a
  **fresh contract**.
- **Saying an ultra vires act can be ratified if all the members agree.** It cannot —
  *Ashbury v Riche*. Only an act beyond the **articles** or beyond the **directors' authority** is
  ratifiable.
- **Applying *Turquand's* rule to a forgery, or to an insider.** A forged document is a nullity, and
  a director dealing with his own company is taken to know the internal position.
- **Confusing the two doctrines' beneficiaries.** **Constructive notice protects the company**;
  **indoor management protects the outsider**.
- **Saying the company dissolves when members fall below the minimum.** Under s. 3A it does not; the
  cognisant members simply become **severally liable** for debts contracted after six months of
  trading while so reduced.

procedure. The two figures to re-check before the attempt are **₹ 4,00,00,000 and ₹ 40,00,00,000**,
because they are the only numbers here that live in the Rules.
