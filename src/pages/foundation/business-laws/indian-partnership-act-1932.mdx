Three friends open a coaching centre in Indore. One puts in the money, one teaches, one runs
the office. Nobody writes anything down. Two years later the teacher has ordered ₹4 lakh of
furniture on credit without asking anyone, the office manager has quietly been taking a
commission from the printer, and the one who put in the money wants out — and wants his money
back with interest.

Who pays the furniture dealer? Can the firm claw back the commission? Is there even a
partnership, when there is not a scrap of paper? Every one of those questions is answered by a
statute passed in 1932 that runs to only 74 sections, most of them short.

This chapter is the **law** of partnership: who is a partner, what a partner may bind the firm
to, what partners owe each other, what registration does and does not do, and how a firm ends.
The arithmetic of partners' capital accounts belongs to a different paper; here, the numbers you
will meet are the ones the Act itself fixes — six per cent, six months, and the order in which
money is paid out on dissolution.

## 1. What a partnership is — section 4

Section 4 does the whole of the definitional work in three sentences:

> "'Partnership' is the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profits of a
> business carried on by all or any of them acting for all. Persons who have entered into
> partnership with one another are called individually 'partners' and collectively 'a firm', and
> the name under which their business is carried on is called the 'firm name'."

Read the three labels carefully, because examiners test them directly:

- **Partnership** is a *relation* — an abstract legal relationship between people.
- **Firm** is the *collective name* for those people. A firm is **not** a person in law. It has
  no separate legal personality: it cannot own property in its own right, cannot sue in its own
  name except as a convenient label for all the partners, and its debts are the partners' debts.
- **Firm name** is merely the trading style — "Kohli &amp; Sons", "Trimurti Traders".

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    "A partnership firm is a legal person distinct from its partners" is <strong>false</strong>,
    and it is the single commonest one-mark loss in this chapter. A company is a legal person; a
    firm is not. For accounting and tax the firm is treated as a separate <em>entity</em>, but
    an entity for book-keeping is not a person in law.
  </p>
</Callout>

The Act applies to India as a whole, came into force on 1 October 1932 (except s. 69, which
came into force a year later), and does not disturb the Indian Contract Act 1872: by s. 3, the
unrepealed provisions of the Contract Act continue to apply to firms so far as they are
consistent with this Act. So offer, acceptance, consideration, free consent, capacity and lawful
object all still govern the partnership agreement, because a partnership *is* a contract.

## 2. The five essentials, and mutual agency as the true test

Unpack s. 4 and you get five elements. All five must be present.

**(a) Two or more persons.** The Act fixes no maximum; s. 464 of the Companies Act 2013 and the
rules made under it cap an unincorporated association at **50 persons**. Only a person competent
to contract can *become* a partner, so a minor cannot be a partner (though he can be admitted to
the benefits — §8). A firm is not a person, so **a firm cannot be a partner in another firm**,
although the partners of one firm may all join another firm in their individual names. A company,
being a legal person, *can* be a partner if its objects allow it.

**(b) An agreement.** Section 5 puts this beyond argument: "The relation of partnership arises
from contract and not from status." The classic illustration in the section itself is that the
members of a Hindu undivided family carrying on a family business are **not** partners in that
business, and neither is a Burmese Buddhist husband and wife carrying on business as such. The
agreement may be oral, written or inferred from conduct — a deed is prudent, never compulsory.

**(c) Business.** "Business" is defined in s. 2(b) as including every trade, occupation and
profession. There must be a business: a group of friends who buy a lottery ticket together, or
neighbours who jointly own and let out a shop, are not partners because there is no business
being carried on. A **single venture** is enough — the business need not be continuous (§5).

**(d) Sharing of profits.** Every partner must have a right to share the profits. The ratio is
whatever the partners agree; equal if they agree nothing (§11). Sharing of *losses* is not part
of the definition — a partner may validly be indemnified against losses — but sharing of profits
is essential. And, critically, sharing profits is **not conclusive proof** that a partnership
exists (§3).

**(e) Mutual agency — the business is carried on "by all or any of them acting for all".**
This is the clause that decides real cases, and s. 18 states its consequence: "Subject to the
provisions of this Act, a partner is the agent of the firm for the purposes of the business of
the firm." Each partner is at once a **principal** (bound by what the others do) and an **agent**
(able to bind the others). That double capacity is what the courts call the *true test* of
partnership.

<Callout kind="example">
  <p>
    Farida finances a bakery run by Gopal. She takes 30% of the profits, inspects the accounts
    monthly, and may veto any purchase over ₹50,000. Gopal alone deals with suppliers and
    customers; Farida has no power to buy flour or hire staff, and no supplier can hold her
    liable on a contract Gopal signs. Profit-sharing plus control of a sort — but
    <strong>no mutual agency</strong>. She is a financier, not a partner.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. Deciding whether a partnership exists — section 6

Section 6 tells a court how to answer the question in a disputed case:

> "In determining whether a group of persons is or is not a firm, or whether a person is or is
> not a partner in a firm, regard shall be had to the real relation between the parties, as shown
> by all relevant facts taken together."

Two working rules follow.

**Rule 1 — substance over label.** The parties' own description does not settle it. Calling the
document a "joint venture agreement" does not stop it being a partnership; calling someone a
"partner" on the letterhead does not make him one as between the parties. The court reads the
whole arrangement: who bears losses, who contributes capital, who deals with outsiders, whose
name is on the licences, who can bind whom.

**Rule 2 — profit-sharing is evidence, not proof.** The Explanation to s. 6 says so expressly,
and then lists the standard cases where a person takes a share of profits *without* becoming a
partner. Learn this list; it is examined almost every attempt.

| Who receives a share of profits | Why he is not a partner |
| --- | --- |
| A **lender** who has lent money to the business and is paid interest varying with profits | He is a creditor. He cannot bind the firm and has no interest in the assets as such |
| An **employee or agent** paid by way of a share of profits (a commission scheme) | He works under the firm's direction; the profit share is his remuneration |
| The **widow or child of a deceased partner** receiving an annuity out of profits | The payment is a continuing obligation to the family, not a share in the venture |
| A **seller of the goodwill** of the business who takes a share of profits as part of the price | The profit share is the price of an asset already sold |
| A **part-owner sharing gross returns** (s. 5 read with the Explanation) | Sharing *gross returns* from joint property is not even sharing profits |

The reason all five escape is the same one: **none of them is an agent of the others.**
Profit-sharing is present in each, mutual agency in none.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Do not write that "the person does not share losses, so he is not a partner". Loss-sharing is
    not an essential of partnership and a partner may be expressly indemnified against losses.
    The reason these five are outside is the absence of the agency relation, not the absence of
    loss-sharing.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A useful two-step for any "is X a partner?" question. <strong>Step 1:</strong> is there an
    agreement to share the profits of a business? If no, stop — not a partnership.
    <strong>Step 2:</strong> can each of them bind the others in the ordinary business? If no, it
    is one of the s. 6 Explanation situations. Only when both answers are yes is there a
    partnership.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Partnership distinguished from four look-alikes

### 4.1 Partnership and co-ownership

| Point | Partnership | Co-ownership |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How it arises | Only from agreement (s. 5) | May arise by agreement, inheritance, or operation of law |
| Business | Must exist | Need not; co-owners may simply hold an asset |
| Profit-sharing | Essential | Not essential; co-owners share the property, not necessarily profits |
| Agency | Each partner is agent of the others | No co-owner can bind another |
| Transfer of share | Not without the consent of all (s. 29 gives a transferee very limited rights) | A co-owner may transfer his share freely |
| Lien on assets | A partner has a lien for expenses and liabilities | No such lien |
| Division | Assets are sold and money divided | A co-owner may demand division in specie |

Two brothers who inherit a godown and let it out are co-owners. If they agree to run a
warehousing *business* in it and share the profits, they become partners.

### 4.2 Partnership and a Hindu undivided family business

| Point | Partnership | HUF business |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source | Contract (s. 5) | Status — birth into the family |
| Minors | Cannot be partners; only admitted to benefits (s. 30) | A minor becomes a coparcener by birth |
| Management | Every partner may take part (s. 12(a)) | Only the **karta** manages |
| Liability | Every partner is liable jointly and severally, personally and unlimitedly (s. 25) | The karta is personally liable; other coparceners are liable only to the extent of their share in the family property |
| Accounts | Every partner may inspect and copy the books (s. 12(d)) | A coparcener cannot ordinarily demand accounts of past dealings |
| Death | May dissolve the firm (s. 42(c), subject to contract) | The business continues; no dissolution |

### 4.3 Partnership and a company

| Point | Partnership firm | Company |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Legal personality | None; the firm is the partners | A separate legal person from incorporation |
| Governing law | Indian Partnership Act 1932 | Companies Act 2013 |
| Registration | Optional (but see §18) | Compulsory; the company exists only on registration |
| Liability | Unlimited, joint and several | Limited to the unpaid amount on shares, or the guarantee |
| Transfer of interest | Only with the consent of all partners | Shares are freely transferable in a public company |
| Perpetual succession | None; death, retirement or insolvency affects the firm | Perpetual succession; members change, the company survives |
| Agency | Each partner is an agent of the firm | A member is not an agent of the company |
| Property | Belongs to the partners jointly | Belongs to the company itself |
| Audit | Not compulsory under this Act | Compulsory |

### 4.4 Partnership and a club

A club is an **association of persons for a social, recreational or charitable purpose**. It
carries on no business and distributes no profits, so it fails the third and fourth essentials
of s. 4. A member of a club is not an agent of the other members, cannot bind them, and has no
interest in the club's property that he can call his own. The subscriptions belong to the club's
general fund, not to the members individually.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    A frequent slip in the HUF comparison: writing that coparceners have "no liability". They do
    — to the extent of their share in the joint family property. It is the <em>personal,
    unlimited</em> liability that is confined to the karta.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Kinds of partnership — sections 7 and 8

**Partnership at will (s. 7).** Where no provision is made by contract between the partners for
the **duration** of their partnership, or for the **determination** of their partnership, the
partnership is "partnership at will". Both conditions must fail before s. 7 applies: if the deed
fixes a term, or fixes a mode of ending the firm, it is not a partnership at will. Its practical
consequence is s. 43 — any partner may dissolve it by giving **notice in writing** to all the
other partners of his intention to dissolve, and the firm is dissolved from the date mentioned
in the notice, or (if no date is mentioned) from the date of communication of the notice.

**Particular partnership (s. 8).** A person may become a partner with another person in
**particular adventures or undertakings** — one shipment, one construction contract, one film.
Such a firm dissolves on the completion of the venture (s. 42(b), subject to contract), and it
is not a partnership at will while the venture is running.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Where a firm is constituted for a fixed term and the partners simply carry on after the term
    expires, the rights and duties remain as they were so far as they are consistent with a
    partnership at will (s. 17(b)) — and the firm now <em>is</em> at will, so any partner can end
    it by notice under s. 43.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. Kinds of partners

The Act names some of these and the study material names the rest. Learn what each may do, what
each is liable for, and whether public notice is needed on retirement.

- **Active (working, ostensible) partner** — contributes capital, takes part in management, is
  known to outsiders as a partner. He is an agent of the firm for all ordinary business. On
  retiring he **must give public notice**, failing which he remains liable to third parties for
  acts done after retirement (s. 32(3)).
- **Sleeping (dormant) partner** — contributes capital and shares profits but takes no part in
  management and is unknown to outsiders. He is fully liable to third parties for the firm's
  debts while he is a partner. On retiring he need **not** give public notice, because outsiders
  never gave him credit as a partner (s. 32(3) proviso, read with s. 45).
- **Nominal partner** — lends his name to the firm, contributes no capital and shares no
  profits, yet is **liable to third parties** as a partner because he holds himself out as one.
- **Partner in profits only** — shares profits but is not liable to contribute to losses as
  between the partners; his liability **to third parties** remains unlimited, because s. 25
  cannot be contracted out of as against outsiders.
- **Sub-partner** — a partner agrees to share his own share of profits with a stranger. The
  sub-partner has **no relationship with the firm at all**: he cannot bind it, cannot demand
  accounts from it, and is not liable for its debts. His only claim is against his own partner.
- **Incoming partner (s. 31)** — introduced with the consent of all existing partners (subject
  to contract and to s. 30). He is **not liable for any act of the firm done before he became a
  partner**, unless he agrees to assume it in a tripartite arrangement with the creditor.
- **Outgoing partner (ss. 32 to 38)** — retires, is expelled, becomes insolvent, or dies. See §17.
- **Partner by holding out (s. 28)** — see §7.

## 7. Partner by holding out — section 28

Section 28(1) makes a person liable as a partner even though he never was one:

> "Anyone who by words spoken or written or by conduct represents himself, or knowingly permits
> himself to be represented, to be a partner in a firm, is liable as a partner in that firm to
> anyone who has on the faith of any such representation given credit to the firm, whether the
> person representing himself or represented to be a partner does or does not know that the
> representation has reached the person so giving credit."

Four conditions, each of which must be proved by the person suing:

1. **A representation** that the defendant is a partner — by words, in writing, or by conduct.
2. Made by the defendant himself, **or knowingly permitted** by him. Mere passive knowledge that
   your name is on a signboard, once you know it and let it stay, is "knowingly permitting".
3. The claimant **knew of the representation** and **gave credit to the firm on the faith of it**.
4. The credit was given to the firm.

The liability is one-way. The holding-out partner is liable to the outsider **as if** he were a
partner, but he acquires **no rights** against the firm: no share in profits, no right to
accounts, no interest in the property. He is not a partner; he is only estopped from denying it.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Two traps. First, a claimant who never heard of the representation, or who dealt with the
    firm for reasons of his own, cannot use s. 28 — the credit must have been given
    <em>on the faith of</em> the representation. Second, s. 28 makes the person liable
    <strong>to the third party</strong>, not a partner <em>inter se</em>; he cannot turn round
    and claim a share of profits.
  </p>
</Callout>

**The deceased partner's name (s. 28(2)).** Where after a partner's death the business is
continued in the old firm name, the continued use of that name, or of the deceased partner's
name as part of it, does **not** of itself make his legal representative or his estate liable
for any act of the firm done after his death. The name may stay on the board; the estate is
still out. This is a favourite one-mark question and the answer is: **no liability**.

## 8. A minor admitted to the benefits of partnership — section 30

A minor cannot be a partner, because a minor's agreement is void under s. 11 of the Contract Act.
But s. 30(1) allows that "with the consent of all the partners for the time being, a minor may be
admitted to the benefits of partnership". Note the two limits built into that line: an existing
firm is needed (a partnership cannot be *formed* between a minor and a major alone), and the
consent must be of **all** the partners.

### 8.1 While he is a minor

**Rights.** He may share in the property and profits of the firm as agreed (s. 30(2)). He has
access to, and may inspect and copy, **any of the accounts of the firm** — note the narrowing:
not the books generally, only the accounts. He may sue the partners for an account or for his
share of the property or profits, but **only when severing his connection with the firm**, and
then the value of his share is determined as on the date of severance (s. 30(4)).

**Liability.** His share is liable for the acts of the firm, but he is **not personally liable**
(s. 30(3)). No creditor can touch his private property.

**Disabilities.** He cannot take part in management, cannot bind the firm, and cannot file a suit
against the firm for accounts or payment of his share while he remains in the firm.

### 8.2 On attaining majority — the six-month election

Section 30(5) gives him a window:

> "At any time within six months of his attaining majority, or of his obtaining knowledge that he
> had been admitted to the benefits of partnership, whichever date is later, such person may give
> public notice that he has elected to become or that he has elected not to become a partner in
> the firm, and such notice shall determine his position as regards the firm."

Three things to fix in your memory:

- The period is **six months**.
- It runs from the **later** of (i) attaining majority and (ii) obtaining knowledge that he had
  been admitted to the benefits. The second limb exists because a minor is often admitted by
  his guardian without his knowing.
- The election is made by **public notice** (defined in s. 72), not by a letter to the partners.

**If he fails to give any notice**, the proviso to s. 30(5) settles it: he **becomes a partner**
in the firm on the expiry of the six months. Silence is election *in*.

**Where the election is disputed** (s. 30(6)), the burden of proving that the person had no
knowledge of his admission until a particular date lies on **the person asserting that fact** —
that is, on the former minor.

### 8.3 The two possible outcomes

| | He elects (or is deemed) to **become** a partner | He elects **not** to become a partner |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Share in property and profits | Continues to be the share to which he was entitled as a minor (s. 30(7)(a)) | The same share, but his rights and liabilities continue only up to the date of the notice (s. 30(8)(a)) |
| Personal liability | Becomes **personally liable to third parties for all acts of the firm done since he was admitted to the benefits** — retrospective to the date of admission, not the date of majority (s. 30(7)(b)) | Not personally liable after the date of the public notice; his share remains liable for acts up to that date |
| Right to sue | Full partner's rights | May sue the partners for his share under s. 30(4) |

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The retrospective bite of s. 30(7)(b) is the trap. Students write that the new partner is
    liable "from the date he attains majority". He is liable
    <strong>from the date he was first admitted to the benefits</strong> — which may be years
    earlier. Compare s. 31: an ordinary incoming partner is <em>not</em> liable for anything
    before he joins. The former minor is the exception.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Date arithmetic appears in the bank. If he turns 18 on 12 March 2026 but learns of his
    admission only on 20 July 2026, the six months run from 20 July 2026 and expire on
    <strong>20 January 2027</strong>. Take the later trigger first, then add six calendar months.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Relations of partners to one another — the deed and the defaults

Section 11(1) sets the architecture of this whole part: "Subject to the provisions of this Act,
the mutual rights and duties of the partners of a firm may be determined by contract between the
partners, and such contract may be express or may be implied by a course of dealing." Section
11(2) adds one permitted restraint on trade — an agreement that a partner shall not carry on any
business other than that of the firm while he is a partner is **valid**, notwithstanding s. 27
of the Contract Act.

So the working rule is: **the partnership deed governs; the Act's rules in ss. 12 to 17 apply
only where the deed is silent** — except for the two duties in ss. 9 and 10, which no deed can
displace.

Section 13 opens with the words "subject to contract between the partners", and s. 12 with
"subject to contract between the partners" as well. Section 9 and s. 10 do not.

## 10. The two absolute duties — sections 9 and 10

**Section 9 — general duties.** "Partners are bound to carry on the business of the firm to the
greatest common advantage, to be just and faithful to each other, and to render true accounts
and full information of all things affecting the firm to any partner or his legal representative."

**Section 10 — duty to indemnify for fraud.** "Every partner shall indemnify the firm for any
loss caused to it by his fraud in the conduct of the business of the firm."

Both are **absolute**: they cannot be excluded by the deed, because a contract permitting fraud
or excusing bad faith between partners would defeat the very relation and would be void under
s. 23 of the Contract Act as opposed to public policy.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Contrast s. 10 with s. 13(f). Fraud → the partner indemnifies the firm, always. Ordinary
    <em>negligence</em> or wilful neglect → indemnity under s. 13(f), but that section is
    "subject to contract", so the partners may agree otherwise.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 11. Rights, duties and defaults — sections 12 and 13

**Section 12 — the conduct of the business** (all of it subject to contract):

- **(a)** Every partner has a right to take part in the conduct of the business.
- **(b)** Every partner is bound to attend diligently to his duties in the conduct of the business.
- **(c)** Any difference arising as to **ordinary matters** connected with the business may be
  decided by a **majority** of the partners, and every partner shall have the right to express
  his opinion before the matter is decided; but **no change may be made in the nature of the
  business without the consent of all the partners**.
- **(d)** Every partner has a right to have access to and to inspect and copy **any of the books
  of the firm**.
- **(e)** In the event of the death of a partner, his legal representatives, or in the event of a
  partner being adjudicated insolvent, his assignee, have a right of access to and to inspect and
  copy any of the books of the firm — but only so far as they relate to the period during which
  that partner was a partner.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Note the difference in reach between s. 12(d) — a partner may inspect <strong>any of the
    books</strong> — and s. 30(2), where a minor may inspect only <strong>the accounts</strong>.
    Examiners set that comparison as a one-liner.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 13 — the mutual rights and liabilities** (again, subject to contract). These six are
the "in the absence of an agreement" rules that every question about a firm with no deed turns on:

| Clause | Default rule |
| --- | --- |
| **13(a)** | A partner is **not entitled to receive remuneration** for taking part in the conduct of the business |
| **13(b)** | Partners are entitled to share **equally** in the profits earned, and shall contribute **equally** to the losses sustained by the firm |
| **13(c)** | Where a partner is entitled to interest on capital subscribed, that interest shall be **payable only out of profits** |
| **13(d)** | A partner making, for the purposes of the business, any **payment or advance beyond the amount of capital he has agreed to subscribe** is entitled to interest at **six per cent per annum** |
| **13(e)** | The firm shall **indemnify a partner** in respect of payments made and liabilities incurred by him in the ordinary and proper conduct of the business, and in doing any act in an emergency for protecting the firm from loss, of the kind a person of ordinary prudence would do in his own case |
| **13(f)** | A partner shall **indemnify the firm** for any loss caused to it by his wilful neglect in the conduct of the business |

Three of those six deserve a second look.

**No remuneration (13(a)).** A working partner draws a salary only if the deed says so. In an
exam, "the deed is silent" plus "A worked full time while B did nothing" gives you: A gets
**nothing** extra, and both share profits equally under 13(b).

**Interest on capital only out of profits (13(c)).** Even where the deed provides for interest on
capital, s. 13(c) makes it payable out of profits. So in a loss year, no interest on capital.

**Six per cent on advances (13(d)).** This is different in kind. Money advanced **beyond** agreed
capital is in the nature of a loan to the firm, and it carries interest at 6% p.a. **whether or
not there are profits**. It is a *charge*, not an appropriation.

<Callout kind="example">
  <p>
    Kamini agreed to bring ₹5,00,000 as capital and did. In August she lent the firm a further
    ₹3,00,000 for eight months to pay for a machine. The deed says nothing about interest. She
    gets <strong>no</strong> interest on the ₹5,00,000 (13(c): interest on capital only if agreed,
    and then only out of profits) and <strong>6% p.a. on the ₹3,00,000</strong> under 13(d) —
    that is ₹3,00,000 × 6% × 8/12 = <strong>₹12,000</strong> — even if the firm made a loss.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 12. Partnership property and personal profits — sections 14 to 17

**What is partnership property (s. 14).** Subject to contract, the property of the firm includes:

- all property and rights and interests in property **originally brought into the stock of the
  firm**;
- property **acquired by or for the firm**; and
- property acquired **with money belonging to the firm**, and
- the **goodwill** of the business.

Unless the contrary intention appears, property and rights and interests in property acquired
with money belonging to the firm are deemed to have been acquired for the firm. So the test is
essentially **the source of the money and the intention of the partners**, not whose name is on
the paper. A shop bought out of firm funds but registered in one partner's name is still
partnership property.

**Application of the property (s. 15).** "Subject to contract between the partners, the property
of the firm shall be held and used by the partners exclusively for the purposes of the business."
A partner who uses the firm's lorry for his own contract work is in breach of s. 15 and must
account for the benefit under s. 16(a).

**Personal profits earned by partners (s. 16).** Subject to contract:

- **16(a)** — If a partner **derives any profit for himself from any transaction of the firm, or
  from the use of the property or business connection of the firm or the firm name**, he shall
  account for that profit and pay it to the firm.
- **16(b)** — If a partner **carries on any business of the same nature as and competing with**
  that of the firm, he shall account for and pay to the firm all profits made by him in that
  business.

Two points examiners like. First, 16(b) bites only on a **competing** business of the **same
nature**; a partner in a stationery firm who separately runs a poultry farm keeps his profits
unless the deed forbids it. Second, both limbs make him hand over the **profits**, not merely
compensate the firm for its loss — the firm need not prove it lost anything.

**Rights and duties after a change (s. 17).** Subject to contract, and so far as they are
consistent with the new situation, the mutual rights and duties of the partners remain the same:

- **(a)** where a change occurs in the constitution of the firm — as between the continuing
  partners;
- **(b)** where a firm constituted for a **fixed term** continues after the expiry of the term —
  and the firm is now a partnership at will;
- **(c)** where a firm constituted to carry out **one or more adventures** carries out other
  adventures.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Section 14's list is often mis-stated as "everything a partner owns and uses in the
    business". A partner's own building merely <em>allowed</em> to be used by the firm, rent
    free, is not partnership property — nothing was brought into the stock, and no firm money
    bought it. The firm gets the use, not the ownership.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 13. Implied authority of a partner — section 19

We now cross from the partners' own quarrel to the outsider's claim. Section 18 makes every
partner an agent of the firm for the purposes of the business; s. 19(1) fixes how far that
agency runs:

> "Subject to the provisions of section 22, the act of a partner which is done to carry on, in
> the usual way, business of the kind carried on by the firm, binds the firm. The authority of a
> partner to bind the firm conferred by this section is called his 'implied authority'."

So an act binds the firm through implied authority only if **all three** are satisfied:

1. it is done **to carry on business of the kind carried on by the firm** — a partner in a
   textile firm buying cloth, not a tractor;
2. it is done **in the usual way** in which such business is carried on; and
3. it satisfies **s. 22** — the act is done or the instrument executed **in the firm name, or in
   any other manner expressing or implying an intention to bind the firm**.

Section 22 matters more than students expect. A partner who signs a negotiable instrument in his
**own name only** does not bind the firm, however much the money was meant for the firm.

### 13.1 The statutory exclusions — section 19(2)

In the absence of any **usage or custom of trade to the contrary**, the implied authority of a
partner does **not** empower him to —

- **(a)** submit a dispute relating to the business of the firm to **arbitration**;
- **(b)** open a **banking account** on behalf of the firm **in his own name**;
- **(c)** **compromise or relinquish** any claim or portion of a claim by the firm;
- **(d)** **withdraw a suit or proceeding** filed on behalf of the firm;
- **(e)** **admit any liability** in a suit or proceeding against the firm;
- **(f)** **acquire immovable property** on behalf of the firm;
- **(g)** **transfer immovable property** belonging to the firm;
- **(h)** **enter into partnership** on behalf of the firm.

A memory hook: the eight are the acts that either **give away the firm's rights** (compromise,
withdraw, admit, arbitrate), **deal in land** (acquire, transfer), or **change what the firm is**
(new partnership, personal bank account). Anything of that weight needs the consent of all.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The opening words "in the absence of any usage or custom of trade to the contrary" are worth
    half a mark on their own. The list is a default, not an absolute prohibition: a trade custom
    can restore any of the eight, and the partners can expressly authorise them (s. 20).
  </p>
</Callout>

### 13.2 What normally *is* within implied authority

Where the trade is a trading firm (one that buys and sells), the usual acts include: buying and
selling goods of the kind the firm deals in; receiving payment of debts due to the firm and
giving receipts; engaging employees for the business; borrowing money on the firm's credit;
pledging the firm's goods as security; drawing, accepting and endorsing negotiable instruments in
the firm name; and employing a lawyer to defend a suit against the firm. A **non-trading** firm —
a firm of professionals, say — has a narrower implied authority; borrowing and pledging are
ordinarily outside it.

## 14. Extending, restricting and ending authority — sections 20, 21, 22 and 24

**Section 20 — extension and restriction.** "The partners in a firm may, by contract between the
partners, extend or restrict the implied authority of any partner." Then comes the sting:

> "Notwithstanding any such restriction, any act done by a partner on behalf of the firm which
> falls within his implied authority binds the firm, unless the person with whom he is dealing
> knows of the restriction or does not know or believe that partner to be a partner."

So an internal restriction binds the outsider only if the outsider **knew** of it. A clause in
the deed saying "no partner shall place an order above ₹1,00,000" is enforceable *between the
partners* always, and *against a supplier* only if the supplier knew.

**Section 21 — partner's authority in an emergency.** "A partner has authority, in an emergency,
to do all such acts for the purpose of protecting the firm from loss as would be done by a person
of ordinary prudence, in his own case, acting under similar circumstances, and such acts bind the
firm." This is a genuine enlargement of authority: it can cover acts that would otherwise be
outside s. 19, provided there is a real emergency and the partner acts as a prudent man would for
himself. Pair it with s. 13(e), which gives that partner an indemnity from the firm.

**Section 24 — effect of notice to an acting partner.** "Notice to a partner who habitually acts
in the business of the firm of any matter relating to the affairs of the firm operates as notice
to the firm, except in the case of a fraud on the firm committed by or with the consent of that
partner." Three limbs to keep straight: the partner must be one who **habitually acts** in the
business; the matter must relate to the **affairs of the firm**; and the rule fails where that
partner is himself party to a **fraud on the firm** — otherwise a fraudster's knowledge would be
imputed to his victims.

<Callout kind="example">
  <p>
    A godown catches fire at midnight. Partner Rehana, without consulting anyone, hires trucks
    at three times the normal rate and moves the surviving stock to a rented shed. The deed says
    hiring above ₹25,000 needs two signatures. Section 21 protects her: a prudent person would
    have done the same with his own goods, so the firm is bound and s. 13(e) indemnifies her for
    what she paid.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 15. Liability of the firm and its partners — sections 25 to 27

**Section 25 — liability of a partner for the acts of the firm.** "Every partner is liable,
jointly with all the other partners and also severally, for all acts of the firm done while he is
a partner." Three things follow.

- The liability is **joint and several**, so a creditor may sue all the partners together or any
  one of them alone for the whole debt; the partner who pays recovers contribution from the rest.
- It is **unlimited** — the partner's private property is available to the firm's creditors.
- It is limited **in time** to acts done "while he is a partner" — hence s. 31 (an incoming
  partner is not liable for the past) and s. 32(2) (an outgoing partner remains liable for the
  past unless discharged).

**Section 26 — liability of the firm for the wrongful acts of a partner.** "Where, by the
wrongful act or omission of a partner acting in the ordinary course of the business of the firm,
or with the authority of his partners, loss or injury is caused to any third party, or any
penalty is incurred, the firm is liable therefor to the same extent as the partner."

**Section 27 — liability of the firm for misapplication by partners.** The section covers two
distinct situations:

- **(a)** where a partner, **acting within his apparent authority**, receives money or property
  from a third party and misapplies it; and
- **(b)** where a firm, **in the course of its business**, receives money or property from a
  third party, and it is misapplied by **any of the partners** while it is in the custody of the
  firm,

— in either case **the firm is liable to make good the loss**.

The difference is worth learning as a pair. In 27(a) the money never reaches the firm: one
partner takes it in, using the standing he has as a partner, and pockets it — the firm still
pays, because his apparent authority drew the money in. In 27(b) the money has already reached
the firm, and any partner then misapplies it — the firm pays because it was the firm's custody
that failed.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    A common error is to answer "the firm is not liable because the partner acted dishonestly".
    Dishonesty is exactly what ss. 26 and 27 are about. The question is never whether the act was
    wrongful — it is whether the partner was acting in the <strong>ordinary course of the
    business</strong> or within his <strong>apparent authority</strong>. If he was, the firm
    pays and then recovers from him (s. 10 for fraud).
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 23 — effect of admissions and representations.** An admission or representation made by
a partner concerning the affairs of the firm is evidence against the firm, if it is made in the
ordinary course of business.

## 16. Changes in the constitution of the firm — sections 31 to 35 and 38

A change in *who* the partners are is not the end of the firm. The firm continues; only the
constitution changes. (Contrast dissolution, §19.)

**Section 31 — introduction of a partner.** Subject to contract between the partners and to
s. 30 (minors), **no person shall be introduced as a partner into a firm without the consent of
all the existing partners**. Subject to s. 30, a person so introduced **does not thereby become
liable for any act of the firm done before he became a partner**.

**Section 32 — retirement.** A partner may retire (a) with the consent of all the other
partners, (b) in accordance with an express agreement between the partners, or (c) where the
partnership is at will, by giving notice in writing to all the other partners of his intention
to retire. A retiring partner may be discharged from liability to a third party for acts of the
firm done before his retirement by an agreement made by him with that third party and the
partners of the reconstituted firm, and such agreement may be implied by a course of dealing
between the third party and the reconstituted firm after he had knowledge of the retirement.
Notwithstanding retirement, he and the partners continue to be liable **to third parties** for
any act done by any of them which would have been an act of the firm before the retirement,
until **public notice** is given — but a retired partner is not liable to a third party who
deals with the firm without knowing that he was a partner. The notice may be given by the
retired partner or by any partner of the reconstituted firm.

**Section 33 — expulsion.** "A partner may not be expelled from a firm by any majority of the
partners, save in the exercise in good faith of powers conferred by contract between the
partners." Three conditions are therefore necessary for a valid expulsion: the **power must
exist in the contract**; it must be exercised by a **majority**; and it must be exercised in
**good faith** — which in practice means notice to the partner, a hearing, and a purpose that is
in the interest of the firm rather than personal. An expulsion that fails any of the three is
void, and the "expelled" partner remains a partner. The provisions of s. 32(2) to (4) apply to
an expelled partner as they do to a retired partner.

**Section 34 — insolvency of a partner.** Where a partner is adjudicated an insolvent, he
**ceases to be a partner on the date of the order of adjudication**, whether or not the firm is
thereby dissolved. His estate is not liable for any act of the firm done after that date, and
the firm is not liable for any act of the insolvent after that date. No public notice is needed.

**Section 35 — liability of the estate of a deceased partner.** Where under a contract the firm
is not dissolved by the death of a partner, the **estate of the deceased partner is not liable
for any act of the firm done after his death**. Again, no public notice is needed — death is
notice enough.

**Section 38 — revocation of a continuing guarantee.** A continuing guarantee given to a firm, or
to a third party in respect of the transactions of a firm, is, **in the absence of an agreement
to the contrary, revoked as to future transactions from the date of any change in the
constitution of the firm**.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Public notice is required for <strong>retirement and expulsion</strong>, and for a minor
    electing under s. 30(5), and on dissolution (s. 45). It is <strong>not</strong> required on
    the <strong>death</strong> or <strong>insolvency</strong> of a partner, nor for the
    retirement of a <strong>dormant</strong> partner. That short list is a whole question.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 17. The outgoing partner — sections 29, 36 and 37

**Section 29 — the rights of a transferee of a partner's interest.** A partner may transfer his
interest in the firm, absolutely or by mortgage or charge. The transferee gets remarkably little:

- **While the firm continues**, he is **not entitled** to interfere in the conduct of the
  business, to require accounts, or to inspect the books. He is entitled only to **receive the
  share of profits** of the transferring partner, and he must **accept the account of profits
  agreed to by the partners**.
- **If the firm is dissolved**, or if the transferring partner ceases to be a partner, the
  transferee becomes entitled to receive the share of the assets of the firm to which the
  transferring partner was entitled, and, for the purpose of ascertaining that share, **to an
  account as from the date of the dissolution**.

**Section 36 — the outgoing partner's right to compete.** An outgoing partner **may carry on a
business competing with that of the firm and may advertise such business**, but subject to
contract to the contrary he may **not** (a) use the **firm name**, (b) represent himself as
carrying on the business **of the firm**, or (c) **solicit the custom of persons who were dealing
with the firm before he ceased to be a partner**.

Section 36(2) then permits a restraint: a partner may make an agreement with his partners that on
ceasing to be a partner he will not carry on a business similar to that of the firm within a
specified period or within specified local limits, and such an agreement is **valid if the
restrictions imposed are reasonable** — an express exception to s. 27 of the Contract Act.

**Section 37 — the outgoing partner's (or dead partner's estate's) right where the business is
continued.** This is the section with the arithmetic in it.

> Where a member of a firm has died or otherwise ceased to be a partner, and the surviving or
> continuing partners carry on the business of the firm with the property of the firm **without
> any final settlement of accounts** as between them and the outgoing partner or his estate, then,
> in the absence of a contract to the contrary, the outgoing partner or his estate is entitled at
> his option to —
>
> - **the share of the profits made since he ceased to be a partner which is attributable to the
>   use of his share of the property of the firm**, **or**
> - **interest at the rate of six per cent per annum on the amount of his share in the property
>   of the firm**.

The proviso adds: where by contract the surviving or continuing partners have an **option to
purchase** the interest of an outgoing or deceased partner, and that option is **duly exercised**,
the outgoing partner or his estate is **not entitled to any further or other share of profits**;
but if any partner assuming to act in exercise of the option does not in all material respects
comply with its terms, he is liable to account under the main part of the section.

<Callout kind="example">
  <p>
    Dinesh retires on 1 April 2026, his share in the firm's property standing at ₹8,00,000. No
    settlement is made and the firm trades on. By 31 March 2027 the profits attributable to the
    use of his share come to ₹57,000. Dinesh may take <strong>₹57,000</strong> (the profit
    option) or <strong>₹48,000</strong> (₹8,00,000 × 6% for one year). He will take ₹57,000 —
    the Act gives him the choice, and he takes whichever is higher. Reverse the numbers and he
    takes the interest.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 18. Registration of firms — sections 58, 59 and 69

Registration under this Act is **optional**. No penalty attaches to not registering. But s. 69
takes away so much that in practice no sensible firm stays unregistered.

### 18.1 How a firm is registered — sections 58 and 59

**Section 58** — registration may be effected **at any time** by sending to the Registrar of
Firms of the area a **statement in the prescribed form**, with the prescribed fee, stating:

- the **firm name**;
- the **place or principal place of business** of the firm;
- the names of any **other places** where the firm carries on business;
- the **date on which each partner joined** the firm;
- the **names in full and permanent addresses** of the partners; and
- the **duration** of the firm.

The statement must be **signed by all the partners, or by their agents specially authorised**,
and each person signing must also **verify** it in the prescribed manner. A firm name shall not
contain any of the words "Crown", "Emperor", "Empress", "Empire", "Imperial", "King", "Queen",
"Royal", or words expressing or implying the sanction, approval or patronage of Government,
except when the State Government signifies its consent in writing.

**Section 59** — when the Registrar is satisfied that s. 58 has been complied with, he shall
**record an entry of the statement in the Register of Firms and file the statement**. Registration
is effective from the date the Registrar records the entry.

Later changes — in the firm name or place of business (s. 60), in branches (s. 61), in a
partner's name or address (s. 62), in the constitution or on dissolution (s. 63) — are notified
to the Registrar so that the register stays current.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    "Registration is compulsory and an unregistered firm is illegal" is wrong twice over. It is
    optional, and an unregistered firm is perfectly lawful — it simply cannot sue.
  </p>
</Callout>

### 18.2 The effect of non-registration — section 69

This is the most heavily examined provision in the chapter. Take it disability by disability.

**69(1) — a partner cannot sue the firm or a co-partner.** No suit to enforce a right arising
from a contract or conferred by this Act shall be instituted in any court by or on behalf of any
person **suing as a partner in a firm** against the firm or any person alleged to be or to have
been a partner in the firm, unless **(i)** the firm is registered **and (ii)** the person suing
is or has been shown in the Register of Firms as a partner in the firm. Both limbs must be
satisfied — registration of the firm alone is not enough if the plaintiff's own name is missing
from the register.

**69(2) — the firm cannot sue a third party.** No suit to enforce a right arising from a contract
shall be instituted in any court by or on behalf of a firm against any third party unless the
same two conditions are met: the firm is registered and the persons suing are or have been shown
in the Register of Firms as partners in the firm.

**69(3) — the same bar applies to a claim of set-off, or other proceeding to enforce a right
arising from a contract.** So an unregistered firm sued for ₹5,00,000 cannot even set off the
₹2,00,000 the plaintiff owes it; it must pay in full and stay out of court for its own claim.

### 18.3 What non-registration does **not** stop

The exceptions are the other half of the question, and they are short enough to learn whole.
Section 69 does **not** affect:

1. The right of a **third party to sue the firm or its partners** — the bar is one-way. A
   creditor of an unregistered firm has every remedy.
2. The right to sue for the **dissolution of the firm** (s. 69(3)(a)).
3. The right to sue for **accounts of a dissolved firm** (s. 69(3)(a)).
4. The right to **realise the property of a dissolved firm** (s. 69(3)(a)).
5. The powers of an **Official Assignee, Receiver or Court** under the insolvency law to realise
   the property of an insolvent partner (s. 69(3)(b)).
6. Suits or claims of value **not exceeding one hundred rupees** in value, and proceedings
   incidental to such suits (s. 69(4)(b), subject to the Presidency Small Cause Courts limits).
7. Firms or partners in an area to which, by notification under s. 56, the registration chapter
   does not apply (s. 69(4)(a)).
8. A suit to enforce a right **arising otherwise than out of a contract** — for example, a suit
   for an injunction against the infringement of the firm's trade mark, or an action in tort.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The clean way to answer a s. 69 problem: ask <strong>who is suing whom, and on what</strong>.
    Third party suing the firm → allowed. Firm or partner suing anyone on a contract → barred
    unless registered <em>and</em> named in the register. Any partner suing for dissolution,
    accounts of a dissolved firm, or realisation of its property → allowed even if unregistered.
    A non-contractual claim → allowed.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Registration after the suit is filed does not cure the defect.** The bar in s. 69 goes to the
institution of the suit, so a firm that registers midway through a pending suit must withdraw and
file afresh. Learn that; it is a favourite MCQ.

## 19. Dissolution of a firm — sections 39 to 45

**Section 39** — "The dissolution of partnership between all the partners of a firm is called the
'dissolution of the firm'." The distinction that matters:

| Dissolution of **partnership** | Dissolution of the **firm** |
| --- | --- |
| The relation between *some* partners ends; the firm continues with the rest | The relation between **all** partners ends |
| Business continues | Business is wound up |
| Books are not closed; only the retiring partner's account is settled | Assets are realised, liabilities paid, accounts finally settled (s. 48) |
| No court involvement | May be by court order (s. 44) |
| Every dissolution of the firm involves dissolution of the partnership | Not every dissolution of partnership dissolves the firm |

### 19.1 Modes of dissolution

**By agreement (s. 40).** A firm may be dissolved with the consent of all the partners, or in
accordance with a contract between the partners.

**Compulsory dissolution (s. 41).** A firm is dissolved —

- **(a)** by the adjudication of **all the partners, or of all the partners but one**, as
  insolvent; or
- **(b)** by the happening of any event which makes it **unlawful** for the business of the firm
  to be carried on, or for the partners to carry it on in partnership.

The proviso: where a firm carries on more than one adventure or undertaking, the illegality of
one does not of itself dissolve the firm in respect of its lawful adventures.

**On the happening of certain contingencies (s. 42).** Subject to contract between the partners,
a firm is dissolved —

- **(a)** if constituted for a **fixed term**, by the expiry of that term;
- **(b)** if constituted to carry out one or more **adventures or undertakings**, by their
  completion;
- **(c)** by the **death** of a partner;
- **(d)** by the **adjudication of a partner as an insolvent**.

Note the opening words. Because s. 42 is subject to contract, a deed may provide that the firm
shall continue on a partner's death or insolvency — and then s. 35 or s. 34 governs the estate.

**By notice of partnership at will (s. 43).** Where the partnership is at will, the firm may be
dissolved by any partner giving **notice in writing** to all the other partners of his intention
to dissolve the firm. The firm is dissolved as from the **date mentioned in the notice** as the
date of dissolution, or, if no date is mentioned, as from the **date of the communication** of
the notice.

### 19.2 Dissolution by the court — section 44

At the suit of a **partner**, the court may dissolve a firm on any of these grounds:

- **(a) Insanity** — that a partner has become of unsound mind. Here the suit may be brought by
  the next friend of the insane partner as well as by any other partner.
- **(b) Permanent incapacity** — that a partner, other than the partner suing, has become in any
  way permanently incapable of performing his duties as partner.
- **(c) Misconduct** — that a partner, other than the partner suing, is guilty of conduct which
  is likely to affect prejudicially the carrying on of the business, regard being had to the
  nature of the business. The misconduct need not relate to the business itself; a conviction for
  a serious offence can qualify if it damages the firm's standing.
- **(d) Persistent breach of agreement** — that a partner, other than the partner suing, wilfully
  or persistently commits a breach of agreements relating to the management or the conduct of the
  business, or otherwise so conducts himself in matters relating to the business that it is not
  reasonably practicable for the other partners to carry on the business in partnership with him.
- **(e) Transfer of interest** — that a partner, other than the partner suing, has in any way
  transferred the whole of his interest in the firm to a third party, or has allowed his share to
  be **charged** under the Code of Civil Procedure, or **sold in the recovery of arrears of land
  revenue** or of any dues recoverable as such.
- **(f) Business at a loss** — that the business of the firm cannot be carried on save at a loss.
- **(g) Just and equitable** — on any other ground which renders it just and equitable that the
  firm should be dissolved. This is the residual ground: deadlock between two equal partners, the
  disappearance of the substratum of the business, complete loss of confidence.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Grounds (b), (c), (d) and (e) all carry the words "<em>other than the partner suing</em>". A
    partner cannot petition on the strength of his own incapacity, his own misconduct or his own
    transfer. Only insanity (a) is different, because an insane partner cannot sue for himself and
    the section lets his next friend do it.
  </p>
</Callout>

### 19.3 Liability for acts done after dissolution — section 45

Notwithstanding the dissolution of a firm, the partners continue to be **liable as such to third
parties for any act done by any of them which would have been an act of the firm if done before
the dissolution**, **until public notice is given of the dissolution**. The notice may be given by
any partner.

The proviso is the exception you must quote: the estate of a partner who **dies**, or who is
adjudicated an **insolvent**, or of a partner who, not having been known to the person dealing
with the firm to be a partner, **retires** from the firm, is **not liable** under this section for
acts done after the date on which he ceases to be a partner. That is the dormant partner again,
alongside death and insolvency.

## 20. Winding up and settlement of accounts — sections 46 to 55

**Section 46 — right of partners to have the business wound up.** On the dissolution of a firm,
every partner or his representative is entitled, as against all the other partners or their
representatives, to have the property of the firm applied in payment of the debts and liabilities
of the firm, and to have the surplus distributed among the partners or their representatives
according to their rights. This is often called the **partner's lien**.

**Section 47 — continuing authority of partners for purposes of winding up.** After the
dissolution of a firm, the authority of each partner to bind the firm, and the other mutual
rights and obligations of the partners, continue notwithstanding the dissolution, so far as may
be necessary **to wind up the affairs of the firm and to complete transactions begun but
unfinished** at the time of the dissolution, but not otherwise. The proviso: the firm is in no
case bound by the acts of a partner who has been **adjudicated insolvent** — though this does not
affect the liability of a person who, after the adjudication, represents himself or knowingly
permits himself to be represented as a partner of the insolvent.

**Section 48 — mode of settlement of accounts.** In settling the accounts of a firm after
dissolution, the following rules shall, subject to agreement by the partners, be observed:

**(a) Losses**, including deficiencies of capital, shall be paid **first out of profits, next out
of capital, and lastly, if necessary, by the partners individually in the proportions in which
they were entitled to share profits**.

**(b) The assets of the firm**, including any sums contributed by the partners to make up
deficiencies of capital, shall be applied in the following manner and order:

1. in **paying the debts of the firm to third parties**;
2. in paying to each partner **rateably what is due to him from the firm for advances** as
   distinguished from capital;
3. in paying to each partner **rateably what is due to him on account of capital**; and
4. the **residue**, if any, shall be divided among the partners **in the proportions in which
   they were entitled to share profits**.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Four steps, in that order, and the order is the answer to most s. 48 questions:
    <strong>outsiders → partners' advances → partners' capital → profit-sharing residue</strong>.
    A partner's <em>loan</em> to the firm ranks above his <em>capital</em> but below an outside
    creditor. Losses run the other way: profits first, then capital, then the partners
    personally.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 49 — payment of firm debts and of separate debts.** Where there are joint debts due
from the firm and also separate debts due from any partner, the **property of the firm shall be
applied in the first instance in payment of the debts of the firm**, and, if there is any
surplus, then the share of each partner shall be applied in payment of his separate debts or paid
to him. The **separate property of any partner** shall be applied first in the payment of his
separate debts, and the surplus (if any) in the payment of the debts of the firm. Firm assets to
firm creditors first; private assets to private creditors first.

**Section 50 — personal profits earned after dissolution.** Subject to contract, the rule in
s. 16(a) applies to transactions by any surviving partner, or by the representatives of a
deceased partner, undertaken **after the firm is dissolved but before its affairs have been
completely wound up**. There is an exception: where a partner or his representative has bought
the **goodwill** of the firm, he may use the firm name.

**Section 51 — return of premium on premature dissolution.** Where a partner has paid a premium
on entering into a partnership **for a fixed term**, and the firm is dissolved before the expiry
of that term otherwise than by the death of a partner, he is entitled to **repayment of the
premium, or of such part of it as may be reasonable**, having regard to the terms on which he
became a partner and to the length of time during which he was a partner. But **no** repayment is
due where the dissolution is —

- **(a)** mainly due to **his own misconduct**, or
- **(b)** in pursuance of an agreement containing **no provision for the return of the premium or
  any part of it**.

**Section 52 — rights where the partnership contract is rescinded for fraud or
misrepresentation.** A partner entitled to rescind is, without prejudice to any other right,
entitled to (a) a **lien** on the surplus assets after the firm's debts are paid, for any sum he
paid for the purchase of a share and for any capital contributed; (b) to **rank as a creditor** of
the firm in respect of any payment he made towards the firm's debts; and (c) to be **indemnified**
by the partner or partners guilty of the fraud or misrepresentation against all the debts of the
firm.

**Section 53 — right to restrain from use of firm name or firm property.** After a firm is
dissolved, every partner or his representative may, in the absence of a contract to the contrary,
**restrain any other partner or his representative from carrying on a similar business in the
firm name, or from using any of the property of the firm** for his own benefit, until the affairs
of the firm have been completely wound up. The exception again is the partner who has **bought
the goodwill**.

**Section 55 — sale of goodwill after dissolution.** In settling the accounts of a firm after
dissolution, the **goodwill shall, subject to contract between the partners, be included in the
assets, and it may be sold either separately or along with other property of the firm**. Where
the goodwill is sold:

- Every partner may carry on a **business competing** with that of the buyer and may advertise
  it, but, subject to agreement, he may not (i) use the firm name, (ii) represent himself as
  carrying on the business of the firm, or (iii) **solicit the custom of persons who were dealing
  with the firm before its dissolution**.
- Any partner may make an agreement with the buyer that he will not carry on a similar business
  within a specified period or within specified local limits, and such an agreement is **valid if
  the restrictions are reasonable**, notwithstanding s. 27 of the Contract Act.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The s. 55 restrictions are the same three as in s. 36 — do not use the firm name, do not
    pass yourself off as the firm, do not solicit old customers. What a seller of goodwill may
    always do is <strong>compete and advertise</strong>. Students routinely write that he cannot
    compete at all; he can, unless there is a reasonable express restraint.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 21. Public notice — section 72

A "public notice" under this Act is not a letter. Section 72 requires it to be given —

- **by publication in the Official Gazette**, and
- **in at least one vernacular newspaper circulating in the district where the firm has its
  place or principal place of business**, and
- where the firm is **registered**, **by notice to the Registrar of Firms** under s. 63.

All the applicable limbs must be complied with; a Gazette notice alone will not do for a
registered firm. Public notice is needed for: **retirement or expulsion of a partner** (s. 32(3),
s. 33(2)), **dissolution of the firm** (s. 45), and the **election of a former minor** under
s. 30(5).

## 22. Application vignettes with working notes

### Vignette 1 — Is there a partnership at all? (ss. 4, 6)

**Facts.** Anwar owns a printing press in Bhopal. In April 2025 he takes ₹12,00,000 from Bela,
agreeing to pay her 25% of the annual profits until the money is repaid, plus 25% thereafter for
three years "as her return". Bela's name appears nowhere; she does not deal with customers or
suppliers; she may inspect the books quarterly. Separately, Anwar's press manager Chandran is
paid a fixed ₹40,000 a month **plus 10% of net profits**. In March 2026 a paper supplier who is
owed ₹6,00,000 sues Anwar, Bela and Chandran, arguing that all three share profits and are
therefore partners.

**Working notes.**

1. **The test is not profit-sharing.** Section 6 requires the court to look at the real relation
   shown by all relevant facts taken together, and its Explanation says the receipt of a share of
   profits does not of itself make the receiver a partner.
2. **Bela.** Money advanced to a business, repaid with a return measured by profits, is the
   lender's case named in the Explanation. She has no authority to bind Anwar, does not appear to
   the trade, and takes no part in the business. Inspection rights protect her money; they are not
   management. **Not a partner.**
3. **Chandran.** An employee remunerated by a share of profits is the second named case. He works
   under Anwar's direction and cannot bind the firm on his own account. **Not a partner.**
4. **Mutual agency, the deciding element.** Neither Bela nor Chandran can carry on the business
   "for all" within s. 4. There is one proprietor, Anwar.

**Answer.** The suit succeeds against **Anwar alone** for the whole ₹6,00,000. Bela and Chandran
are not partners and are not liable. *(If instead Bela had been named on the letterhead as a
partner and the supplier had extended credit on the faith of that, s. 28 would catch her —
see Vignette 4.)*

### Vignette 2 — The minor's six-month window (s. 30)

**Facts.** With the consent of all four partners of M/s Sundar Traders, **Ishan** is admitted to
the benefits of the firm on **10 June 2022**, when he is 14. He is entitled to a 1/8 share of
profits. Ishan attains majority on **12 March 2026**. He is at boarding school and is told about
his admission to the firm only on **20 July 2026**, when his guardian hands him the papers. He
gives no notice at all. On **2 September 2026** the firm defaults on a ₹20,00,000 loan taken in
2023; the bank sues every partner personally.

**Working notes.**

1. **Start date of the six months (s. 30(5)).** The period runs from the **later** of the date he
   attains majority (12 March 2026) and the date he obtains knowledge of his admission
   (20 July 2026). The later date is **20 July 2026**.
2. **Expiry.** Six months from 20 July 2026 → **20 January 2027**. Until that date Ishan may give
   public notice electing in or out.
3. **Position on 2 September 2026.** The window is still open, and he has not elected. Until the
   six months expire he remains a person admitted to the benefits: his **share is liable** for the
   acts of the firm, but he is **not personally liable** (s. 30(3)). The bank may reach his 1/8
   share in the firm's property, not his personal assets.
4. **If he lets 20 January 2027 pass in silence.** The proviso to s. 30(5) makes him a
   **partner** on that date, and s. 30(7)(b) makes him personally liable for **all acts of the
   firm done since 10 June 2022** — including the 2023 loan. His whole personal estate is then
   exposed.
5. **Burden of proof (s. 30(6)).** If the bank disputes that he learned of his admission only on
   20 July 2026, **Ishan** must prove that fact; the burden is on the person asserting want of
   knowledge.

**Answer.** Deadline **20 January 2027**. Sued on 2 September 2026, Ishan's liability is confined
to his share. Silence past 20 January 2027 converts that into unlimited personal liability
backdated to **10 June 2022**.

### Vignette 3 — How far does implied authority run? (ss. 19, 20, 21, 22)

**Facts.** M/s Vayu Textiles is a registered firm of three partners dealing in cotton fabric. The
deed says: "No partner shall place a single order exceeding ₹2,00,000 without the written consent
of the other two." In one week partner **Prakash**, without any consent —

- **(i)** buys 8,000 metres of cotton fabric from a mill for ₹6,50,000. The mill has dealt with
  the firm for years and knows nothing of the deed;
- **(ii)** signs a memorandum agreeing to refer the firm's pending dispute with a dyer to
  **arbitration**;
- **(iii)** agrees to **sell the firm's godown** at Sanganer to a developer;
- **(iv)** during a flood, hires two trucks for ₹1,80,000 to move stock out of a low-lying
  warehouse overnight; and
- **(v)** borrows ₹3,00,000 for the firm from a moneylender, signing the promissory note
  **"Prakash"** and nothing more.

**Working notes.**

1. **(i) Bound.** Buying fabric is business of the kind carried on by the firm, done in the usual
   way (s. 19(1)). The deed's ₹2,00,000 cap is a restriction under s. 20 — effective between the
   partners, but against the mill only if the mill **knew** of it. It did not. **The firm pays
   ₹6,50,000**; Prakash answers to his partners internally.
2. **(ii) Not bound.** Submitting a dispute to arbitration is expressly outside implied authority,
   s. 19(2)(a), absent a trade usage to the contrary.
3. **(iii) Not bound.** Transfer of immovable property belonging to the firm is outside implied
   authority, s. 19(2)(g). The developer cannot enforce the sale against the firm.
4. **(iv) Bound.** A flood is an emergency, and hiring trucks to save the stock is what a person
   of ordinary prudence would do with his own goods — s. 21 enlarges his authority and the deed's
   cap does not defeat it. Prakash is also indemnified under s. 13(e).
5. **(v) Not bound on the instrument.** Borrowing is within a trading firm's implied authority,
   but s. 22 requires the act to be done in the firm name or in a manner expressing an intention
   to bind the firm. A promissory note signed in his personal name binds **Prakash personally**.
   (If the money actually reached and benefited the firm, the lender's remedy is against the firm
   in restitution, not on the note.)

**Answer.** Firm bound on (i) and (iv); not bound on (ii), (iii) and (v).

### Vignette 4 — Liability by holding out (s. 28)

**Facts.** M/s Ratnam Hardware has two partners. **Vasanth**, a well-known local builder, is not a
partner, but for two years the firm's signboard, letterhead and GST correspondence have described
him as "Partner — Business Development". Vasanth has seen the board many times and has said
nothing. In January 2026 a steel supplier, **Neeraj**, who had refused credit to the firm a year
earlier, agrees to supply ₹9,00,000 of steel on 90 days' credit **after** the partners show him
the letterhead with Vasanth's name and after a bank manager confirms Vasanth's standing. The firm
defaults. Separately, the firm's old letterhead still carries the name of **Ratnam**, a partner
who died in 2023; his son now runs an unrelated business.

**Working notes.**

1. **Representation.** The signboard, letterhead and correspondence all represent Vasanth as a
   partner. He need not have made the representation himself — s. 28(1) covers a person who
   **knowingly permits** himself to be represented, and two years of silence with knowledge is
   exactly that.
2. **Credit given on the faith of it.** Neeraj had refused credit before and gave it only after
   seeing the letterhead. The causal link that s. 28 demands is established. It does not matter
   that Vasanth did not know the representation had reached Neeraj — the section says so
   expressly.
3. **Extent of liability.** Vasanth is liable **as a partner** for the ₹9,00,000, jointly and
   severally with the two real partners, out of his personal estate.
4. **No rights in return.** He gets no share of profits, no right to accounts, no interest in the
   firm's property. Section 28 is an estoppel, not an admission to the firm.
5. **The deceased partner's name (s. 28(2)).** Continued use of "Ratnam" in the firm name does
   **not** make the deceased's estate or his legal representative liable for anything the firm
   does after his death. The son is not liable at all.

**Answer.** Neeraj may recover ₹9,00,000 from Vasanth personally, or from either continuing
partner, or from all three. Ratnam's estate and his son are not liable.

### Vignette 5 — An unregistered firm in court, and the settlement that follows (ss. 69, 48)

**Facts.** M/s Deep Enterprises is a firm of three partners — **Deepa, Elango and Farhan** — which
has never been registered. In 2026 four disputes land at once:

- **(i)** The firm wants to sue a customer, Ganesh, for ₹7,50,000 due on a supply contract.
- **(ii)** A landlord sues the firm for ₹2,20,000 of unpaid rent, and the firm wants to **set off**
  ₹90,000 that the landlord owes it on a separate contract.
- **(iii)** Farhan wants to sue Deepa and Elango for his share of profits under the deed.
- **(iv)** Farhan, in the alternative, wants to sue for **dissolution** of the firm and for
  **accounts** after dissolution.

The firm is eventually dissolved by consent on 30 June 2026. Its assets realise **₹47,00,000**.
Outside creditors are owed **₹18,00,000**. Deepa had earlier advanced **₹5,00,000** to the firm
over and above her agreed capital. Capitals stand at Deepa ₹9,00,000, Elango ₹6,00,000, Farhan
₹3,00,000. Profits are shared **3 : 2 : 1**.

**Working notes on the suits.**

1. **(i) Barred.** Section 69(2): a suit by a firm against a third party to enforce a contractual
   right cannot be instituted unless the firm is registered and the partners suing are shown in
   the Register of Firms. Registering *now* will not save a suit filed today; the firm must
   register first and then sue.
2. **(ii) The landlord's suit proceeds; the set-off is barred.** Section 69 does not touch a
   **third party's** right to sue the firm, so the landlord recovers his ₹2,20,000 in full. But
   s. 69(3) applies the same bar to a **claim of set-off**, so the firm cannot deduct the ₹90,000
   and must chase it separately after registering.
3. **(iii) Barred.** Section 69(1): a person suing **as a partner** cannot sue the firm or a
   co-partner to enforce a right arising from the contract or conferred by the Act unless the firm
   is registered and he is shown in the register.
4. **(iv) Allowed.** Section 69(3)(a) expressly saves a suit for the **dissolution of a firm**,
   for **accounts of a dissolved firm**, and for **realisation of the property of a dissolved
   firm**. This is the standard escape route for a partner of an unregistered firm.

**Working notes on the settlement (s. 48(b)).**

| Step | Applied to | Amount (₹) | Balance left (₹) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Assets realised | — | — | 47,00,000 |
| 1 | Debts of the firm to third parties | 18,00,000 | 29,00,000 |
| 2 | Partners' **advances** — Deepa's loan | 5,00,000 | 24,00,000 |
| 3 | Partners' **capital** — Deepa 9,00,000, Elango 6,00,000, Farhan 3,00,000 | 18,00,000 | 6,00,000 |
| 4 | **Residue** in the profit-sharing ratio 3 : 2 : 1 | 6,00,000 | Nil |

Residue: Deepa ₹3,00,000, Elango ₹2,00,000, Farhan ₹1,00,000.

**Total received:** Deepa ₹5,00,000 + ₹9,00,000 + ₹3,00,000 = **₹17,00,000**; Elango ₹6,00,000 +
₹2,00,000 = **₹8,00,000**; Farhan ₹3,00,000 + ₹1,00,000 = **₹4,00,000**.

**Answer.** Only the dissolution and accounts suit can be filed as things stand. On winding up,
Deepa takes ₹17,00,000, Elango ₹8,00,000 and Farhan ₹4,00,000. Note that Deepa's ₹5,00,000 loan
ranked **above** everyone's capital but **below** the outside creditors — that ordering is the
whole point of s. 48(b).

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

1. **"A firm is a separate legal person."** It is not. Only a company (and an LLP) is. Say
   instead: a firm is a collective name for the partners and has no legal personality.
2. **Treating profit-sharing as conclusive.** Section 6's Explanation lists the lender, the
   employee, the widow or child taking an annuity, the seller of goodwill and the sharer of gross
   returns. The missing element in each is **mutual agency**, not loss-sharing.
3. **Saying a minor "is a partner".** He is admitted to the **benefits** of partnership under
   s. 30. And a partnership cannot be constituted between a minor and a major alone — there must
   be an existing firm.
4. **Getting the minor's clock wrong.** Six months from the **later** of majority and knowledge.
   Silence means he **becomes** a partner. On becoming a partner his personal liability runs back
   to the date he was **admitted to the benefits**, not to the date of majority.
5. **Confusing s. 12(d) with s. 30(2).** A partner may inspect **any of the books**; a minor may
   inspect **any of the accounts**.
6. **Forgetting that s. 13 is "subject to contract" but ss. 9 and 10 are not.** No deed can
   licence bad faith or excuse a partner's fraud.
7. **Mixing up 13(c) and 13(d).** Interest on **capital** is payable only out of profits, and only
   if agreed. Interest at **6% p.a. on advances beyond capital** is payable whether or not there
   are profits.
8. **Reciting implied authority without s. 22.** An instrument signed in a partner's own name does
   not bind the firm, even if the transaction was for the firm.
9. **Treating the s. 19(2) exclusions as absolute.** They apply "in the absence of any usage or
   custom of trade to the contrary", and the partners may expressly confer the authority (s. 20).
10. **Letting an internal restriction defeat an outsider.** Under s. 20 a restriction binds a
    third party only if he **knew** of it, or did not believe the person to be a partner.
11. **Answering "the firm is not liable because the partner was dishonest."** Sections 26 and 27
    exist precisely for wrongful acts and misapplication. Ask instead whether he acted in the
    ordinary course of business or within his apparent authority.
12. **Getting the public-notice list wrong.** Needed on retirement, expulsion, dissolution and a
    former minor's election. **Not** needed on death or insolvency, and not for a dormant
    partner's retirement.
13. **Confusing dissolution of partnership with dissolution of the firm.** Only the second winds
    up the business and triggers ss. 46 to 55.
14. **Dropping the words "other than the partner suing" from s. 44(b) to (e).** A partner cannot
    rely on his own incapacity, misconduct, breach or transfer.
15. **Reversing the s. 48(b) order.** Third-party debts → partners' advances → partners' capital →
    residue in the profit-sharing ratio. Losses go the other way: profits → capital → partners
    individually in their profit-sharing proportions.
16. **Saying registration is compulsory.** It is optional; s. 69 is the sanction. And a suit filed
    while unregistered is not cured by registering later.
17. **Forgetting that a third party can always sue an unregistered firm.** The s. 69 bar runs one
    way only.
18. **Saying a seller of goodwill cannot compete.** Under ss. 36 and 55 he may compete and
    advertise; he may not use the firm name, pass himself off as the firm, or solicit its old
    customers — unless a reasonable express restraint says more.
19. **Missing the s. 37 option.** The outgoing partner chooses between the profits attributable to
    his share and 6% p.a. interest on it — he is not confined to the interest.
20. **Assuming a sub-partner has rights against the firm.** He has none; his only claim is against
    the partner who admitted him.

## One-page revision summary

**Definition (s. 4).** Relation between persons who agreed to share the profits of a business
carried on by all or any of them **acting for all**. Partners individually; **firm** collectively;
**firm name** is the trading style. A firm is not a legal person.

**Five essentials.** Two or more persons (max 50) · agreement (s. 5 — contract, not status) ·
business (s. 2(b)) · sharing of profits · **mutual agency** (s. 18) — the true test.

**Existence (s. 6).** Look at the real relation from all facts. Profit-sharing is not conclusive.
Non-partners who share profits: **lender, employee/agent, widow or child taking an annuity, seller
of goodwill, sharer of gross returns**.

**Kinds of firm.** At will (s. 7 — no term and no mode of determination; endable by written notice
under s. 43) · particular (s. 8 — one adventure).

**Kinds of partner.** Active (public notice needed on retirement) · sleeping (no public notice) ·
nominal (liable, no profits) · in profits only · sub-partner (no rights against the firm) ·
holding out (s. 28 — liable, no rights) · incoming (s. 31 — not liable for the past) · outgoing.

**Minor (s. 30).** Admitted to benefits with consent of all · share in property and profits · may
inspect **accounts** · share liable, **no personal liability** · no management, no suit except on
severance · election by **public notice within 6 months of the later of majority and knowledge** ·
silence → becomes a partner, personally liable **from the date of admission** · burden of proving
want of knowledge on him (s. 30(6)).

**Absolute duties.** s. 9 — greatest common advantage, just and faithful, true accounts and full
information. s. 10 — indemnify the firm for **fraud**.

**Defaults (s. 13, subject to contract).** No remuneration · profits and losses **equal** ·
interest on capital only out of profits · **6% p.a. on advances** beyond capital · firm indemnifies
the partner (ordinary conduct and emergencies) · partner indemnifies the firm for **wilful
neglect**. Section 12: right to participate · majority decides ordinary matters, but **no change in
the nature of the business without unanimous consent** · access to **all books**.

**Property (ss. 14 to 16).** Stock brought in, property acquired by or for the firm or with firm
money, and **goodwill** · used exclusively for the business (s. 15) · account for personal profits
from firm transactions or firm property (s. 16(a)) and from a **competing** business (s. 16(b)).

**Third parties.** s. 19(1) — act to carry on, **in the usual way**, business of the kind carried
on by the firm. s. 19(2) exclusions: **arbitration · bank account in own name · compromise ·
withdraw suit · admit liability · acquire immovable property · transfer immovable property · enter
into partnership**. s. 20 — restrictions bind outsiders only if known. s. 21 — emergency powers.
s. 22 — act must be in the firm name. s. 24 — notice to a habitually acting partner is notice to
the firm, except in a fraud on the firm. s. 25 — **joint and several, unlimited**, for acts done
while a partner. s. 26 — wrongful acts. s. 27 — misapplication, (a) apparent authority, (b) firm's
custody.

**Changes.** s. 31 introduction (consent of all; no past liability) · s. 32 retirement · s. 33
expulsion (**power in contract + majority + good faith**) · s. 34 insolvency (ceases on the date of
adjudication) · s. 35 estate not liable after death · s. 38 continuing guarantee revoked as to
future transactions. **Outgoing partner:** s. 36 may compete and advertise but not use the firm
name, pass off, or solicit old customers; reasonable restraints valid · **s. 37 option — profits
attributable to his share OR 6% p.a. interest**, unless a purchase option was duly exercised.

**Registration.** Optional · s. 58 statement (firm name, principal place, other places, **date each
partner joined**, names and addresses, duration) signed and verified by all · s. 59 entry in the
Register of Firms. **s. 69:** (1) partner cannot sue firm or co-partner; (2) firm cannot sue a
third party; (3) no set-off or other proceeding — all on contractual rights, and only if the firm
is registered **and** the plaintiff is named in the register. **Saved:** third party suing the firm
· dissolution · accounts of a dissolved firm · realisation of its property · powers of the Official
Assignee or Receiver · claims up to ₹100 · non-contractual claims · notified areas (s. 56).

**Dissolution.** s. 39 firm vs partnership · s. 40 agreement · s. 41 compulsory (all or all but one
insolvent; business becomes unlawful) · s. 42 contingencies (expiry of term, completion of
adventure, death, insolvency — subject to contract) · s. 43 notice in a partnership at will ·
**s. 44 court: insanity, permanent incapacity, misconduct, persistent breach, transfer of interest,
business only at a loss, just and equitable** · s. 45 liability continues until **public notice**,
except the estate of a dead or insolvent partner and an unknown (dormant) retiring partner.

**Winding up.** s. 46 partner's lien · s. 47 authority continues for winding up only ·
**s. 48: losses — profits, then capital, then partners in the profit-sharing ratio; assets —
(1) outside debts, (2) partners' advances rateably, (3) capital rateably, (4) residue in the
profit-sharing ratio** · s. 49 firm property to firm debts first, separate property to separate
debts first · s. 50 personal profits during winding up · **s. 51 premium returned on premature
dissolution**, except where due to his own misconduct or where the agreement makes no provision ·
s. 52 rescission for fraud (lien, rank as creditor, indemnity) · s. 53 restraint on use of the firm
name or property · **s. 55 goodwill included in the assets and saleable**.

**Public notice (s. 72).** Official Gazette **and** a vernacular newspaper in the district **and**,
for a registered firm, notice to the Registrar. Required for retirement, expulsion, dissolution and
a former minor's election.
