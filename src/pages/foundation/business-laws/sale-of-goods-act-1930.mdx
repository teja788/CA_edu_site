A trader in Ludhiana buys 200 bales of hosiery yarn. The bales are lying in a public warehouse,
still mixed with the seller's other stock. Before anyone moves a bale, the warehouse burns down.
Who loses the yarn — the man who paid, or the man who was paid?

Nothing in the Indian Contract Act answers that. The answer sits in one idea that runs through this
whole chapter: **the loss follows the ownership, not the possession and not the payment**. Work out
who owned the goods at the moment of the fire and you have the answer. That single question —
*has the property passed?* — is the spine of the Sale of Goods Act 1930, and it is what the examiner
tests in scenario after scenario.

The Act is short, tidy and heavily examined. It was carved out of the Contract Act in 1930 (until
then it was Chapter VII, sections 76 to 123, of the 1872 Act), and by **section 3** the Contract Act
still applies to everything the 1930 Act does not specifically cover — capacity, free consent,
frustration, damages. So this chapter does not replace Chapter 2; it sits on top of it.

## 1. Where this Act sits and what it governs

The Act extends to the whole of India and came into force on **1 July 1930**. It governs contracts
for the **sale of goods** — nothing else. Land, buildings and other immovable property are outside
it; so are actionable claims and money. Get this gate right before anything else, because if the
subject matter is not "goods", none of the rules that follow apply.

The definitions you must be able to reproduce:

- **Goods — section 2(7).** Every kind of **movable property other than actionable claims and
  money**, and it *includes* stock and shares, growing crops, grass, and things attached to or
  forming part of the land which are agreed to be **severed before sale or under the contract of
  sale**. So standing timber sold to be cut is goods; the land under it is not.
- **Buyer — section 2(1).** A person who buys **or agrees to buy** goods. Notice the second limb: a
  person is a buyer from the moment of the agreement, before any property passes.
- **Seller — section 2(13).** A person who sells **or agrees to sell** goods.
- **Delivery — section 2(2).** **Voluntary transfer of possession** from one person to another. If
  possession is taken by force or by theft, that is not delivery in law.
- **Document of title to goods — section 2(4).** A bill of lading, dock warrant, warehouse-keeper's
  certificate, railway receipt, multimodal transport document, warrant or order for the delivery of
  goods — any document used in the ordinary course of business as **proof of the possession or
  control of goods**, or authorising its holder to transfer or receive the goods it represents.
- **Price — section 2(10).** The **money consideration** for a sale of goods. Money only. If the
  consideration is other goods it is a **barter or exchange** and this Act does not apply; if part
  money and part goods, it is a sale of the part paid for in money.
- **Insolvent — section 2(8).** A person who has ceased to pay his debts in the ordinary course of
  business, or cannot pay them as they become due, **whether or not** he has been adjudged insolvent.
  This wide meaning matters for stoppage in transit.
- **Mercantile agent — section 2(9).** An agent having in the customary course of business as such
  agent authority either to sell goods, or to consign goods for the purpose of sale, or to buy goods,
  or to raise money on the security of goods.

**Essentials of a contract of sale.** Six elements, and a one-mark question usually asks which is
missing:

1. **Two parties** — a buyer and a seller, who must be different persons. A person cannot buy his
   own goods. (The narrow exception recognised in the cases is a part-owner selling to a co-owner,
   and a sale by a person's agent in execution or under a decree.)
2. **Goods** — movable property as defined above.
3. **Transfer of property** in the goods — ownership, not merely possession.
4. **Price** — money consideration.
5. **All the essentials of a valid contract** under section 10 of the Contract Act — free consent,
   competent parties, lawful object, and so on.
6. **Includes both a sale and an agreement to sell** — section 4(1) says so in terms.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Two traps in the definition of goods. <strong>Money as currency is not goods, but money as a
    collector's item is</strong> — old coins sold for their rarity are goods. And <strong>stocks and
    shares ARE goods</strong> under s. 2(7), even though we do not think of them as movables in
    everyday speech. An actionable claim (a debt, a lottery ticket claim) is <em>not</em> goods.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. Sale and agreement to sell — section 4

Section 4 is the hinge of the whole Act.

- **4(1).** A contract of sale of goods is a contract whereby the seller transfers or agrees to
  transfer the property in goods to the buyer for a price. There may be a contract of sale between
  one part-owner and another.
- **4(2).** A contract of sale may be **absolute or conditional**.
- **4(3).** Where under a contract of sale the property in the goods is **transferred at the time of
  the contract**, the contract is called a **sale**; where the transfer of the property is to take
  place **at a future time or subject to some condition** thereafter to be fulfilled, the contract is
  called an **agreement to sell**.
- **4(4).** An agreement to sell **becomes a sale** when the time elapses or the conditions are
  fulfilled subject to which the property in the goods is to be transferred.

So "sale" and "agreement to sell" are not two different contracts. They are the same contract at two
different stages, and the only thing that separates them is **whether the property has passed yet**.

<div class="formula">Contract of sale = SALE (property has passed — an executed contract, a jus in rem) or AGREEMENT TO SELL (property is still to pass — an executory contract, a jus in personam)</div>

**The seven consequences that follow.** This is the standard 6-mark distinction question, and every
row of it is really a consequence of who owns the goods.

| | **Sale** | **Agreement to sell** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nature of contract | Executed | Executory |
| Nature of the right | Right *in rem* — good against the whole world | Right *in personam* — good only against the seller |
| Transfer of property | Passes at once | Passes at a future time or on a condition |
| **Risk of loss** | On the buyer, even if goods are still with the seller | Stays with the seller, even if goods are with the buyer |
| Seller's insolvency (buyer has paid) | Buyer can claim the **goods** from the Official Assignee | Buyer ranks as an ordinary creditor for a **dividend** on his money |
| Buyer's insolvency (price unpaid) | Seller must deliver and prove for the price; he gets a dividend only | Seller may **refuse to deliver** unless paid in full |
| Seller's breach | Buyer may sue for **damages and for the goods** (conversion / specific relief) | Buyer may sue for **damages only** |
| Buyer's breach | Seller may sue for the **price** (s. 55) | Seller may sue only for **damages** for non-acceptance (s. 56) |
| Resale by seller to a third party | Third party gets **no title**, as the seller had none | Third party gets a **good title**; the original buyer's remedy is damages |

Read the insolvency rows slowly. They are the ones examiners love, and they follow mechanically:
whoever owns the goods bears their fate.

## 3. Sale distinguished from its neighbours

**Sale versus hire-purchase.** The heaviest of these distinctions, because a hire-purchase agreement
looks exactly like a sale by instalments.

| | **Sale (including instalment sale)** | **Hire-purchase** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ownership | Passes to the buyer at once | Stays with the owner until the last instalment is paid and the option to purchase is exercised |
| Position of the person in possession | Buyer, from the start | **Bailee** first, and buyer only if he exercises the option |
| Can he end the contract? | No — he is bound to pay the whole price | Yes — the hirer may **return the goods** at any time and stop paying |
| Instalments are | Part payments of a price | **Hire charges**, adjusted against the price if the option is exercised |
| If he resells to an innocent buyer | Buyer gets a good title | The third party gets **no title**, because the hirer never owned the goods and is not a person who has "agreed to buy" under s. 30(1) |
| Governing law | Sale of Goods Act 1930 | Hire Purchase Act 1972 and the law of bailment |

**Sale versus a contract for work and materials.** Ask what the **substance** of the contract is. If
the essence is the **delivery of a chattel**, with skill and labour merely incidental, it is a sale;
if the essence is the **exercise of skill and labour**, with the material incidental, it is a
contract for work and materials.

- A painter commissioned to paint a portrait — **work and materials** (you are buying his skill; the
  canvas and paint are incidental).
- A tailor stitching a suit from cloth the customer supplies — **work and materials**.
- A furniture maker who builds a wardrobe from his own timber to your measurements and delivers it —
  **sale** (you end up with a chattel).
- A printer supplying printed letterheads on his own paper — **sale** of the printed stationery.

**Sale versus bailment.** In bailment possession moves but ownership never does, and the *same goods*
(or the goods in an altered form) must come back. In a sale, ownership moves and nothing comes back.

**Sale versus exchange or barter.** The consideration must be **money** for a sale. Goods for goods
is exchange, and this Act does not apply. Where a customer trades in an old car worth ₹2,00,000 and
pays ₹6,00,000 in cash for a new one priced at ₹8,00,000, the courts treat it as a **sale with a
part-exchange**, not a barter, because a money price was fixed for the whole.

**Sale versus gift.** A gift has no price at all; it is governed by the Transfer of Property Act.

**Sale versus a contract of agency.** A commission agent who sells your goods to third parties never
owns them; a distributor who buys from you and resells on his own account does. The test is whether
the intermediary is **accountable for the price received** (agency) or liable for **an agreed price
whatever he resells at** (sale).

## 4. Kinds of goods, and goods that perish — sections 6 to 8

**The classification (section 6 read with section 2).** Goods are of three kinds, and the whole of
the passing-of-property topic depends on which one you are looking at.

1. **Existing goods** — owned or possessed by the seller at the time the contract is made. These
   split further into:
   - **Specific goods — s. 2(14):** goods **identified and agreed upon** at the time the contract is
     made. "The blue Alto bearing registration MH-04-AB-1234."
   - **Ascertained goods:** goods **identified after the contract is made**, by later selection or
     appropriation. The Act does not define the phrase, but sections 19 to 24 use it constantly.
   - **Unascertained or generic goods:** described only by kind and quantity — "100 quintals of
     Sharbati wheat", with no particular sacks marked out.
2. **Future goods — s. 2(6):** goods **to be manufactured, produced or acquired** by the seller
   after the making of the contract. Section 6(3) is emphatic: a contract about future goods, even
   if the parties call it a sale, **operates only as an agreement to sell**. You cannot pass
   ownership of what you do not yet own.
3. **Contingent goods — s. 6(2):** a species of future goods, where the acquisition depends on a
   **contingency which may or may not happen**. "I will sell you 50 crates of Alphonso if the ship
   *Vasanti* arrives loaded." Again, an agreement to sell only.

**Section 7 — goods perishing before the contract is made.** Where there is a contract for the sale
of **specific goods**, and the goods, **without the knowledge of the seller**, have at the time the
contract was made **perished** or become **so damaged as no longer to answer their description**, the
contract is **void**. This is section 20 of the Contract Act (bilateral mistake as to the existence
of the subject matter) applied to goods.

**Section 8 — goods perishing after the agreement to sell but before the risk passes.** Where there
is an **agreement to sell specific goods**, and the goods **without any fault** of the seller or the
buyer perish or become so damaged as no longer to answer their description **before the risk passes
to the buyer**, the agreement is thereby **avoided**. This is frustration (Contract Act s. 56) applied
to goods.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Four conditions gate both sections and options are written by removing one of them:
    the goods must be <strong>specific</strong>, they must have <strong>perished</strong> (or been so
    damaged as not to answer their description), it must be <strong>without the seller's knowledge</strong>
    (s. 7) or <strong>without the fault of either party</strong> (s. 8), and under s. 8 it must be
    <strong>before the risk passes</strong>. Neither section applies to <strong>unascertained</strong>
    goods — if 100 quintals of "any Sharbati wheat" is destroyed, the seller must simply source more
    or pay damages, because the market has not perished.
  </p>
</Callout>

"Perished" includes goods that have been **stolen and not traced**, goods that have lost their
commercial identity (cement set hard into stone, a consignment of potatoes gone rotten), and
**partial destruction** where the remainder no longer answers the contract description.

## 5. The price — sections 9, 10 and 11

**Section 9 — how the price is fixed.** Four routes, and the section states them in a fixed order:

1. **Fixed by the contract** itself, or
2. **Left to be fixed in a manner agreed** by the contract (an index, a formula, a valuer), or
3. **Determined by the course of dealing** between the parties.
4. Where the price is not determined by any of those three, the buyer must pay a **reasonable
   price** — and section 9(2) adds that what is a reasonable price is a **question of fact dependent
   on the circumstances of each particular case**. It is *not* automatically the market price.

So a contract does **not** fail merely because no price was named. This is the opposite of what
students expect.

**Section 10 — agreement to sell at a valuation.** Where there is an agreement to sell goods on the
terms that the price is to be fixed by the **valuation of a third party**, and that third party
**cannot or does not** make the valuation, the **agreement is thereby avoided**. Two important
riders:

- If the goods **or any part of them** have been **delivered to and appropriated by the buyer**, he
  must pay a **reasonable price** for them. The avoidance does not let him keep goods for nothing.
- Where the third party is **prevented from making the valuation by the fault of the seller or the
  buyer**, the party **not in fault** may maintain a **suit for damages** against the party in fault.

**Section 11 — stipulations as to time.** Unless a different intention appears from the terms of the
contract, stipulations as to **time of payment are not deemed to be of the essence** of a contract
of sale. Whether any *other* stipulation as to time is of the essence depends on the terms of the
contract.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The commonest error in the whole chapter: reading s. 11 as "time is never of the essence in a
    contract of sale". It says only that <strong>time of PAYMENT</strong> is presumed not to be of
    the essence. <strong>Time of DELIVERY is ordinarily treated as of the essence</strong> in
    commercial contracts — a late delivery therefore lets the buyer repudiate, while a late payment
    ordinarily does not let the seller repudiate (he sues for the price and interest instead). And
    even the payment presumption yields to "a different intention appearing from the terms".
  </p>
</Callout>

**Earnest money and deposits.** A sum paid as **earnest** is part payment plus a guarantee of
performance: if the buyer defaults, a reasonable earnest may be forfeited; if the seller defaults, it
must be refunded. A sum described merely as an **advance** is only part payment and is refundable on
a lawful rescission. The label the parties use is not conclusive; the intention is.

## 6. Conditions and warranties — sections 12 and 13

Not every term of a contract carries the same weight. Section 12 sorts them.

- **12(1).** A stipulation in a contract of sale with reference to goods may be a **condition or a
  warranty**.
- **12(2).** A **condition** is a stipulation **essential to the main purpose** of the contract, the
  breach of which gives rise to a **right to treat the contract as repudiated**.
- **12(3).** A **warranty** is a stipulation **collateral to the main purpose** of the contract, the
  breach of which gives rise to a **claim for damages** but **not** to a right to reject the goods
  and treat the contract as repudiated.
- **12(4).** Whether a stipulation is a condition or a warranty **depends in each case on the
  construction of the contract**. A stipulation may be a condition **though called a warranty** in
  the contract. The label does not decide it; the substance does.

| | **Condition** | **Warranty** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Importance | Essential to the main purpose | Collateral to the main purpose |
| Remedy on breach | Repudiate the contract, reject the goods, **and** claim damages | **Damages only** |
| Can it be treated as the other? | A breach of condition **may be treated as** a breach of warranty (s. 13) | A breach of warranty can **never** be raised to a condition |

**Section 13 — when a breach of condition sinks to a breach of warranty.** Three situations, and
they are examined as a set:

1. **13(1) — voluntary waiver.** Where a contract of sale is subject to any condition to be fulfilled
   by the seller, the buyer **may waive** the condition, **or elect to treat** the breach of the
   condition as a breach of warranty and claim damages instead of rejecting the goods. This is the
   buyer's own choice.
2. **13(2) — compulsory, by acceptance.** Where the contract of sale is **not severable** and the
   buyer has **accepted the goods or part of them**, the breach of any condition to be fulfilled by
   the seller can **only be treated as a breach of warranty** — the buyer has lost the right to
   reject, unless there is a term of the contract, express or implied, to the contrary. (What
   counts as acceptance is section 42 — see §14.)
3. **13(3) — impossibility excuse.** Nothing in the section affects the case where the fulfilment of
   any condition or warranty is **excused by law by reason of impossibility or otherwise**.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Students write "the buyer accepted the goods, so he gets nothing". Wrong. Under s. 13(2) he
    loses only the <strong>right to reject</strong>. His claim for <strong>damages</strong>
    survives, and he may also set the damages off against the price under s. 59(1)(a). And the rule
    bites only where the contract is <strong>not severable</strong> — where each instalment is
    separately priced and separately deliverable, accepting instalment 1 does not bar rejection of
    instalment 2.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. Implied conditions and warranties — sections 14 to 17

Sections 14 to 17 write terms into every contract of sale unless the parties exclude them. Section
62 lets the parties negative or vary any of them by express agreement, by the course of dealing, or
by usage. Learn them as **five implied conditions and two implied warranties**.

**Section 14 — title.** In a contract of sale, unless the circumstances show a different intention,
there is:

- **14(a) — an implied CONDITION** that in the case of a **sale** the seller **has a right to sell
  the goods**, and in the case of an **agreement to sell** he **will have a right to sell** the goods
  **at the time when the property is to pass**. Note the split tense: for an agreement to sell, the
  seller need not own the goods today.
- **14(b) — an implied WARRANTY** that the buyer shall have and enjoy **quiet possession** of the
  goods.
- **14(c) — an implied WARRANTY** that the goods shall be **free from any charge or encumbrance** in
  favour of any third party **not declared or known** to the buyer before or at the time the contract
  is made.

The classic on 14(a) is **Rowland v Divall**: the buyer of a car that turned out to be stolen
recovered the **entire price** even after months of use, because there had been a **total failure of
consideration** — the seller had no right to sell, so the buyer never got what he paid for, namely
ownership. Depreciation is irrelevant.

**Section 15 — sale by description.** Where there is a contract for the sale of goods **by
description**, there is an implied **condition** that the goods shall **correspond with the
description**. And where the sale is **by sample as well as by description**, it is not sufficient
that the bulk of the goods corresponds with the sample if the goods **do not also correspond with
the description**.

A sale is "by description" whenever the buyer has **not seen the goods** and relies on words, and
also where he has seen them but **relies on a description** identifying something not apparent on
inspection — "1998 Bentley", "new" goods, "Sharbati wheat". The condition is strict: goods that are
perfectly good but not what was described may be rejected. In *Re Moore and Landauer*, tinned fruit
described as packed 30 tins to a case was delivered in cases of 24; the total quantity was right and
the fruit was sound, and the buyer was still entitled to reject.

**Section 16 — quality or fitness.** The section opens with the rule and then states two exceptions
to it. Subject to the Act and any other law for the time being in force, there is **no implied
condition or warranty as to the quality or fitness for any particular purpose** of goods supplied
under a contract of sale — that is *caveat emptor*, and §8 deals with it. Then:

**Section 16(1) — fitness for the buyer's particular purpose.** Where the buyer, **expressly or by
implication**, makes known to the seller the **particular purpose** for which the goods are required,
**so as to show that he relies on the seller's skill or judgment**, and the goods are of a
description which it is in the **course of the seller's business** to supply (whether he is the
manufacturer or producer or not), there is an implied **condition** that the goods shall be
**reasonably fit** for that purpose.

- **The proviso.** In the case of a contract for the sale of a **specified article under its patent
  or other trade name**, there is **no implied condition** as to fitness for any particular purpose.
  The reason is that a buyer who orders by trade name is relying on the name, not on the seller's
  judgment. But if he *also* makes his purpose known and relies on the seller's advice about which
  branded article to choose, the condition revives.
- Where goods have **only one obvious purpose** — a hot-water bottle, food, a cycle — the purpose is
  made known "by implication" merely by asking for the article. *Priest v Last*: a hot-water bottle
  that burst was unfit; *Frost v Aylesbury Dairy*: milk carrying typhoid germs was unfit.

**Section 16(2) — merchantable quality.** Where goods are bought **by description from a seller who
deals in goods of that description** (whether he is the manufacturer or producer or not), there is
an implied **condition** that the goods shall be of **merchantable quality**.

- **Merchantable quality** means the goods are **fit for the ordinary purpose** for which such goods
  are used and are **saleable in the market under that description** to a buyer who knows their
  quality, without any abatement of price. Wool contaminated with anthrax, or underwear containing a
  chemical irritant (*Grant v Australian Knitting Mills*), is not merchantable.
- **The proviso.** If the buyer has **examined the goods**, there is **no implied condition as
  regards defects which such examination ought to have revealed**. Read every word: the proviso
  covers only defects a **reasonable examination would have shown**, so **latent** defects remain the
  seller's problem even after inspection. And it applies only where the buyer **actually examined**;
  a mere *opportunity* to examine that he did not take does not trigger it.

**Section 16(3) — implied terms by usage of trade.** An implied warranty or condition as to quality
or fitness for a particular purpose may be **annexed by the usage of trade**.

**Section 16(4) — express terms.** An express warranty or condition does **not negative** an implied
warranty or condition unless it is **inconsistent** with it.

**Section 17 — sale by sample.** A contract of sale is a contract for sale by sample where there is a
**term in the contract, express or implied, to that effect**. Merely showing a sample during
negotiation does not make it a sale by sample. In such a contract there are **three implied
conditions**:

1. The **bulk shall correspond with the sample in quality**;
2. The buyer shall have a **reasonable opportunity of comparing the bulk with the sample**;
3. The goods shall be **free from any defect rendering them unmerchantable**, which **would not be
   apparent on reasonable examination of the sample** — that is, free from **latent** defects. A
   defect that the sample itself would have revealed is the buyer's own lookout.

**The two implied warranties in the Act** are therefore only s. 14(b) quiet possession and s. 14(c)
freedom from encumbrances; everything else in ss. 14 to 17 is a **condition**. Examiners ask this
directly.

## 8. Caveat emptor and its exceptions

*Caveat emptor* means **let the buyer beware**. The opening words of section 16 state it: subject to
the Act and any other law in force, there is **no implied condition or warranty as to the quality or
fitness for any particular purpose** of goods supplied under a contract of sale. The buyer must use
his own skill and judgment; if the goods turn out unsuitable for his own private purpose, that is his
risk. In *Ward v Hobbs*, pigs sold "with all faults" carried typhoid; the seller was not liable
because he had made no representation and the buyer had bought with his eyes open.

The doctrine has been eaten away by seven exceptions, and a 4- to 6-mark question asks for them:

1. **Fitness for the buyer's particular purpose — s. 16(1).** Purpose made known, reliance on the
   seller's skill, goods of a description the seller deals in.
2. **Sale under a patent or trade name — the proviso to s. 16(1)**, which is really an exception *to
   the exception*: caveat emptor comes back where the buyer orders by brand and does not rely on the
   seller.
3. **Merchantable quality — s. 16(2)**, on a sale by description by a dealer in such goods.
4. **Usage of trade — s. 16(3)**, where a condition or warranty is annexed by trade usage.
5. **Sale by sample — s. 17**, the three conditions above.
6. **Sale by sample as well as by description — s. 15**, where the goods must answer both.
7. **Fraud or misrepresentation by the seller**, including **active concealment** of a defect. A
   seller who conceals a defect that a reasonable examination would have revealed cannot shelter
   behind caveat emptor, and consent so obtained is defective under ss. 17 to 19 of the Contract Act.

A modern eighth point worth a line in an answer: **consumer-protection legislation** and the law on
unfair trade practices have narrowed the doctrine still further for consumer sales, though the
Foundation syllabus tests it through the 1930 Act.

<Callout kind="example" label="Vignette 1 — description, fitness and merchantability pulled apart (moderate)">
  <p>
    Meera runs a small bakery. She tells Sundar, who deals in commercial ovens, that she needs an
    oven "able to bake 40 kg of bread in a batch for a commercial bakery running twelve hours a day".
    Sundar recommends and sells her a "Thermax BakePro 40" for ₹1,85,000, describing it in the
    invoice as "commercial deck oven, capacity 40 kg". The oven arrives; its actual batch capacity is
    28 kg, and after three weeks its thermostat fails because the model is built for four hours of
    daily use. Meera had glanced at the oven in the showroom but did not run it. Advise Meera.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 1 — is there a breach of s. 15 (sale by description)?</div>
    Yes. The oven was sold as a "commercial deck oven, capacity 40 kg" and delivers 28 kg. The goods
    do not correspond with the description, and s. 15 makes correspondence an implied
    <strong>condition</strong>. Meera may reject on this ground alone.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 2 — s. 16(1) fitness for a particular purpose</div>
    Meera made the particular purpose known <em>expressly</em> (twelve hours a day, commercial), she
    relied on Sundar's skill and judgment (he recommended the model), and ovens are goods Sundar
    deals in. The thermostat failure shows the oven is not reasonably fit for that purpose, so the
    s. 16(1) condition is broken. The <strong>trade-name proviso does not save Sundar</strong>: Meera
    did not walk in and order a BakePro by name — she stated a purpose and took his recommendation,
    so the reliance the proviso assumes to be absent is plainly present.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 3 — s. 16(2) merchantable quality</div>
    Sold by description by a dealer in such goods, so the condition applies. An oven whose thermostat
    fails in three weeks under ordinary commercial use is not saleable under that description without
    an abatement of price. Sundar will point to the <strong>proviso</strong> — Meera examined the
    goods. It does not help him: her glance in the showroom could never have revealed either the true
    batch capacity or the duty rating, and the proviso excludes only defects <em>such examination
    ought to have revealed</em>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Conclusion</div>
    Three implied conditions are broken. Meera may <strong>repudiate the contract, reject the oven
    and recover the ₹1,85,000</strong>, plus damages for the lost baking. If instead she has already
    accepted the oven within s. 42 and the contract is not severable, s. 13(2) reduces all three
    breaches to <strong>breaches of warranty</strong> — she keeps the oven and claims damages, which
    she may also set off against any unpaid part of the price under s. 59(1)(a).
  </div>
</Callout>

## 9. Passing of property — why it matters, and the rules for specific goods

**Why it matters.** Four consequences hang on the moment the property passes, and every scenario
question in this part is really asking you to fix that moment:

1. **Risk follows property (s. 26)** — the owner bears the accidental loss.
2. **The seller can sue for the price (s. 55)** only where the property has passed (or the price is
   payable on a day certain).
3. **Third-party rights** — only an owner can pass ownership (s. 27).
4. **Insolvency** — the Official Assignee of an insolvent party takes only what that party owned.

**Section 18 — the gate for unascertained goods.** Where there is a contract for the sale of
**unascertained goods**, **no property in the goods is transferred to the buyer unless and until the
goods are ascertained**. This is absolute; no intention of the parties can override it. Ascertainment
must happen first, and only then do the rules of intention operate.

**Section 19 — intention is the master rule.**

- **19(1).** Where there is a contract for the sale of **specific or ascertained** goods, the
  property in them is transferred to the buyer **at such time as the parties to the contract intend**
  it to be transferred.
- **19(2).** For ascertaining the intention, regard shall be had to the **terms of the contract, the
  conduct of the parties and the circumstances of the case**.
- **19(3).** Unless a different intention appears, the rules in **sections 20 to 24** are rules for
  ascertaining the intention of the parties. So sections 20 to 24 are only defaults, and an express
  term beats every one of them.

**Section 20 — specific goods in a deliverable state.** Where there is an **unconditional** contract
for the sale of specific goods in a **deliverable state**, the property passes to the buyer **when
the contract is made**, and it is **immaterial whether the time of payment of the price or the time
of delivery of the goods, or both, is postponed**. "Deliverable state" (s. 2(3)) means such a state
that the buyer would under the contract be bound to take delivery of them.

**Section 21 — specific goods to be put into a deliverable state.** Where there is a contract for the
sale of specific goods and the **seller is bound to do something to the goods for the purpose of
putting them into a deliverable state**, the property does not pass until that thing is **done and
the buyer has notice thereof**. Both limbs: doing it is not enough; the buyer must know.

**Section 22 — specific goods in a deliverable state, but the seller must weigh or measure them to
fix the price.** Where the goods are in a deliverable state but the seller is bound to **weigh,
measure, test or do some other act or thing with reference to the goods for the purpose of
ascertaining the price**, the property does **not** pass until that act is done **and the buyer has
notice thereof**.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Section 22 is about weighing done <strong>by the seller</strong> and done <strong>to fix the
    price</strong>. If the <strong>buyer</strong> is the one who has to weigh the goods, or if the
    weighing is only to check a quantity already priced, s. 22 does not apply and the property
    passes under s. 20. This single switch decides a whole family of MCQs.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. Unascertained goods, appropriation, and sale or return

**Section 23(1) — sale of unascertained goods and appropriation.** Where there is a contract for the
sale of unascertained or future goods **by description**, and goods of that description and **in a
deliverable state** are **unconditionally appropriated** to the contract, either **by the seller with
the assent of the buyer** or **by the buyer with the assent of the seller**, the property in the
goods thereupon passes to the buyer. The assent may be **express or implied**, and may be given
**before or after** the appropriation is made.

The four ingredients, all of which must be present:

1. The goods must be of the **contract description**;
2. They must be in a **deliverable state**;
3. The appropriation must be **unconditional** — the seller must have put it out of his power to
   substitute other goods;
4. There must be **assent** of the other party.

**Section 23(2) — delivery to a carrier.** Where, in pursuance of the contract, the seller **delivers
the goods to the buyer or to a carrier or other bailee** (whether named by the buyer or not) **for
the purpose of transmission to the buyer**, and does **not reserve the right of disposal**, he is
deemed to have **unconditionally appropriated** the goods to the contract. Delivery to the carrier is
therefore ordinarily the moment the property passes in a distance sale — which is why the buyer bears
the loss if the lorry overturns.

**Section 24 — goods sent on approval or "on sale or return".** When goods are delivered to the buyer
**on approval or on sale or return or other similar terms**, the property therein passes to the
buyer:

- **(a)** when he **signifies his approval or acceptance** to the seller, **or does any other act
  adopting the transaction**; or
- **(b)** if he does not signify his approval or acceptance to the seller but **retains the goods
  without giving notice of rejection**, then, **if a time has been fixed for the return of the goods,
  on the expiration of that time**, and, **if no time has been fixed, on the expiration of a
  reasonable time**.

"Any other act adopting the transaction" is the limb that catches students out. **Pledging** the
goods, **reselling** them, **using** them in a way inconsistent with returning them — each adopts the
transaction and passes the property at once, whatever the return period says. In *Kirkham v
Attenborough*, a man who received jewellery on sale or return and pawned it was held to have adopted
the transaction, so the property had passed and he owed the price.

<div class="formula">Sale or return timeline: property passes on the EARLIEST of — (i) approval or acceptance signified, (ii) an act adopting the transaction, (iii) expiry of the fixed return period, or (iv) expiry of a reasonable time where none is fixed. A notice of rejection given inside the period stops the clock.</div>

**Section 25 — reservation of the right of disposal.**

- **25(1).** Where there is a contract for the sale of **specific goods** or where goods are
  **subsequently appropriated** to the contract, the seller may, by the terms of the contract or
  appropriation, **reserve the right of disposal** of the goods until certain conditions are
  fulfilled. In such a case, notwithstanding the delivery of the goods to the buyer, to a carrier or
  to any other bailee for transmission, the property in the goods **does not pass** to the buyer until
  the conditions imposed by the seller are fulfilled.
- **25(2).** Where goods are shipped or delivered to a railway administration for carriage, and by
  the **bill of lading or railway receipt the goods are deliverable to the order of the seller or his
  agent**, the seller is **prima facie deemed to reserve the right of disposal**.
- **25(3).** Where the seller of goods **draws on the buyer for the price and transmits to him the
  bill of exchange together with the bill of lading** to secure acceptance or payment, the buyer is
  bound to return the bill of lading if he does not honour the bill of exchange; and if he
  **wrongfully retains** the bill of lading, the property in the goods **does not pass** to him.

## 11. Risk follows the property — section 26

**Section 26.** Unless otherwise agreed, the goods remain at the **seller's risk until the property
therein is transferred to the buyer**, but when the property therein is transferred to the buyer, the
goods are at the **buyer's risk whether delivery has been made or not**. Two provisos qualify it:

- Where **delivery has been delayed through the fault of either buyer or seller**, the goods are at
  the risk of the **party in fault** as regards any loss which **might not have occurred but for
  such fault**.
- Nothing in the section affects the **duties or liabilities of either party as a bailee** of the
  goods of the other party. So a seller holding goods that already belong to the buyer must still
  take reasonable care of them, and is liable for loss caused by his negligence even though the risk
  is on the buyer.

<div class="formula">Risk prima facie passes with PROPERTY, not with possession, not with payment, and not with delivery — subject to (i) a contrary agreement, (ii) the fault-in-delay proviso, and (iii) the bailee's duty of care.</div>

<Callout kind="example" label="Vignette 2 — three warehouses, three answers (moderate-hard)">
  <p>
    On <strong>3 March 2026</strong> Harish, a Ludhiana yarn merchant, makes three contracts and a
    fire destroys everything in the warehouse on <strong>10 March 2026</strong>.
  </p>
  <p>
    (a) He sells to Ashwin "the 60 bales of yarn lying in Bay 3, marked lot H-19", ₹4,80,000,
    payment and delivery both due on 31 March. The bales are ready to go and nothing remains to be
    done. (b) He sells to Bimal "200 quintals of cotton yarn" out of a heap of 900 quintals lying
    loose in Bay 5; nothing has been separated or weighed. (c) He sells to Chetna "the 40 drums of
    dyed yarn in Bay 7 at ₹900 per kilogram, weight to be ascertained by me on the weighbridge"; he
    weighed the drums on <strong>8 March</strong> but never told Chetna. Who bears each loss?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(a) — s. 20</div>
    Specific goods (identified and agreed upon, s. 2(14)), in a deliverable state, unconditional
    contract. Property passed <strong>on 3 March, when the contract was made</strong>; s. 20 says in
    terms that postponing the time of payment or of delivery, or both, is immaterial. By s. 26 the
    risk was Ashwin's on 10 March. <strong>Ashwin bears the loss and must still pay ₹4,80,000.</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(b) — ss. 18 and 23</div>
    Unascertained goods out of a larger bulk. Section 18 blocks the passing of property "unless and
    until the goods are ascertained", and nothing was separated or appropriated. Property never
    passed, so the risk stayed with the seller. <strong>Harish bears the loss.</strong> Note that
    s. 8 does not avoid the contract either — the goods were not specific — so Harish must source
    200 quintals elsewhere or pay damages for non-delivery.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(c) — s. 22</div>
    The goods are specific and in a deliverable state, but the seller was bound to weigh them
    <em>for the purpose of ascertaining the price</em>. Section 22 requires two things: the act done
    <strong>and notice to the buyer</strong>. Harish weighed on 8 March but gave no notice, so the
    property had not passed by 10 March. <strong>Harish bears the loss.</strong> Had he telephoned
    Chetna with the weight on 8 March, the property — and with it the risk — would have passed that
    day and the answer flips.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">The pattern to carry into the exam</div>
    Same seller, same warehouse, same fire, three different answers — because the only question that
    ever matters is <strong>whose goods were they at the moment of the loss</strong>. Never reason
    from who paid or who held the keys.
  </div>
</Callout>

## 12. Transfer of title by a non-owner — section 27 and its exceptions

**The rule — section 27.** Subject to the provisions of this Act and of any other law for the time
being in force, where goods are sold **by a person who is not the owner** thereof, and who does not
sell them **under the authority or with the consent of the owner**, the buyer acquires **no better
title** to the goods than the seller had, unless the owner of the goods is by his conduct
**precluded** from denying the seller's authority to sell. This is *nemo dat quod non habet* — no one
gives what he does not have. A thief and everyone who buys from him gets nothing, however innocent
and however much he paid.

The Act then tilts back towards the innocent buyer through **nine exceptions**. The list is a
guaranteed question, so learn it as a list.

1. **Estoppel — the closing words of s. 27.** Where the owner by his **conduct, words or omission**
   leads the buyer to believe the seller has authority to sell, he is precluded from denying it. He
   must have made or allowed the representation; mere carelessness in letting someone hold the goods
   is not enough.
2. **Sale by a mercantile agent — the proviso to s. 27.** Where a mercantile agent is, with the
   **consent of the owner**, in **possession of the goods or of a document of title** to them, any
   sale made by him **when acting in the ordinary course of business of a mercantile agent** is as
   valid as if he were expressly authorised — **provided** the buyer acts in **good faith** and has
   **no notice** at the time of the contract that the seller had no authority to sell. All four
   limbs are tested: mercantile agent, consent of the owner, ordinary course of business, good faith
   without notice.
3. **Sale by one of several joint owners — s. 28.** If one of several joint owners of goods has the
   **sole possession** of them **by permission of the other co-owners**, the property in the goods is
   transferred to any person who buys them from him in **good faith** and **without notice** of the
   fact that the seller has no authority to sell.
4. **Sale by a person in possession under a voidable contract — s. 29.** When the seller has obtained
   possession under a contract **voidable** under section 19 or 19A of the Contract Act (coercion,
   undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation), but the contract **has not been rescinded at the time
   of the sale**, the buyer acquires a **good title** provided he buys in **good faith and without
   notice** of the seller's defect of title. The timing is everything: rescind before he resells and
   the buyer gets nothing; rescind after, and the innocent buyer keeps the goods. Note this covers a
   **voidable** contract only — goods obtained under a **void** agreement, or by theft, pass no title
   at all.
5. **Sale by a seller in possession after sale — s. 30(1).** Where a person, having **sold** goods,
   continues or is **in possession of the goods or of the documents of title** to them, the delivery
   or transfer by him (or by a mercantile agent acting for him) of the goods or documents under any
   **sale, pledge or other disposition** to a person who receives them in **good faith and without
   notice of the previous sale** has the same effect as if he were expressly authorised by the owner.
   The first buyer's remedy is against the seller in damages.
6. **Sale by a buyer in possession before property passes — s. 30(2).** Where a person having
   **bought or agreed to buy** goods obtains, **with the consent of the seller**, possession of the
   goods or documents of title, his delivery or transfer under any sale, pledge or other disposition
   to a person receiving them in **good faith and without notice of any lien or other right of the
   original seller** has effect as if he were a mercantile agent in possession with the owner's
   consent. This is where **hire-purchase** is distinguished: a hirer has an **option** to buy, he
   has not "agreed to buy", so s. 30(2) does not protect his transferee.
7. **Resale by an unpaid seller — s. 54(3).** Where an unpaid seller who has exercised his right of
   lien or stoppage in transit **resells** the goods, the new buyer acquires a **good title** as
   against the original buyer, **notwithstanding that no notice of the resale has been given** to
   him.
8. **Sale by a finder of goods — Contract Act s. 169.** A finder of lost goods may sell them where
   the owner cannot with reasonable diligence be found, or refuses to pay the lawful charges, and
   either the goods are in danger of perishing or of losing the greater part of their value, or the
   lawful charges amount to two-thirds of their value.
9. **Sale by a pawnee or pledgee — Contract Act s. 176**, on default by the pawnor after reasonable
   notice; and sales under statutory powers such as by an **official assignee or liquidator**, or
   under an **order of a court**, which pass a good title independently of s. 27.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The pair students reverse: <strong>s. 30(1) is the SELLER left in possession, s. 30(2) is the
    BUYER let into possession</strong>. A memory hook — in 30(1) the seller has already been paid
    once and sells again; in 30(2) the buyer has not yet paid and sells on. Both protect only a
    transferee in <strong>good faith without notice</strong>, and in neither case does the innocent
    first party lose his personal action for damages.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 13. Performance of the contract — delivery rules in sections 31 to 40

**Sections 31 and 32 — the two duties.** It is the duty of the **seller to deliver** the goods and of
the **buyer to accept and pay** for them, in accordance with the terms of the contract of sale
(s. 31). Unless otherwise agreed, **delivery of the goods and payment of the price are concurrent
conditions** (s. 32) — the seller must be ready and willing to give possession in exchange for the
price, and the buyer ready and willing to pay in exchange for possession. Neither can insist that the
other go first.

**Section 33 — modes of delivery.** Delivery may be made by **doing anything which the parties agree
shall be treated as delivery**, or which has the effect of putting the goods in the possession of the
buyer or of a person authorised to hold them on his behalf. Three kinds:

- **Actual (physical)** — the goods are handed over.
- **Symbolic** — the means of control is handed over: the key of the godown, a bill of lading, a
  railway receipt.
- **Constructive (attornment)** — a third party in possession **acknowledges** to the buyer that he
  now holds the goods on the buyer's behalf. Section 36(3) puts this in statutory form: where the
  goods are in the possession of a third person, there is no delivery unless and until that person
  **acknowledges to the buyer** that he holds the goods on his behalf, without prejudice to the
  operation of any document of title.

**Section 34 — effect of part delivery.** A delivery of **part** of the goods, in progress of the
delivery of the whole, has the same effect for the purpose of passing the property as a delivery of
the whole. But a part delivery made with the **intention of severing it from the whole** does not
operate as a delivery of the remainder.

**Section 35 — the buyer must apply for delivery.** Apart from any express contract, the seller is
**not bound to deliver until the buyer applies for delivery**. So a buyer who sits silent and then
sues for non-delivery generally fails.

**Section 36 — rules as to delivery.** The workhorse section:

- **36(1) — place of delivery.** Whether it is for the buyer to take possession or for the seller to
  send the goods is a question depending in each case on the contract. Apart from any such contract,
  goods sold are to be delivered at the **place at which they are at the time of the sale**; goods
  agreed to be sold are to be delivered at the place at which they are at the time of the **agreement
  to sell**, or, if not then in existence, at the place at which they are **manufactured or
  produced**.
- **36(2) — time of delivery.** Where the seller is bound to send the goods but no time is fixed, he
  must send them within a **reasonable time**.
- **36(3) — goods with a third person.** Delivery only on **acknowledgement** (above).
- **36(4) — hour of demand or tender.** Demand or tender of delivery may be treated as ineffectual
  unless made at a **reasonable hour**; what is a reasonable hour is a question of fact.
- **36(5) — expenses of delivery.** Unless otherwise agreed, the **expenses of and incidental to
  putting the goods into a deliverable state** must be borne by the **seller**. (The expenses of
  taking delivery, by contrast, fall on the buyer.)

**Section 37 — delivery of the wrong quantity.** Three cases, and the buyer's options differ:

| Seller delivers | Buyer may | And if he keeps the goods |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Less** than contracted, s. 37(1) | **Reject** the whole | If he **accepts** the short delivery, he must **pay at the contract rate** |
| **More** than contracted, s. 37(2) | Accept only the contract quantity and reject the rest, **or reject the whole** | If he accepts the **whole** of the goods delivered, he must **pay for all of them at the contract rate** |
| Goods **mixed** with goods of a different description not in the contract, s. 37(3) | Accept the goods which are in accordance with the contract and **reject the rest**, or **reject the whole** | — |

Section 37(4) adds that these rules are **subject to any usage of trade, special agreement or course
of dealing** between the parties. And *de minimis* applies: a trivial excess or shortfall that a
commercial person would ignore does not entitle rejection.

**Section 38 — instalment deliveries.** Unless otherwise agreed, the buyer is **not bound to accept
delivery by instalments**. Where the contract *does* provide for delivery by stated instalments,
separately paid for, and either the seller makes a defective delivery of one or more instalments or
the buyer neglects or refuses to take delivery of or pay for one or more instalments, it is a
question **in each case depending on the terms of the contract and the circumstances** whether the
breach is a **repudiation of the whole contract** or a **severable breach** giving a right to
compensation but not a right to treat the whole contract as repudiated. The two things to weigh are
the **ratio the breach bears to the whole contract** and the **likelihood of the breach being
repeated**.

**Section 39 — delivery to a carrier or wharfinger.** Where, in pursuance of a contract, the seller
is authorised or required to send the goods to the buyer, **delivery to a carrier** (whether named by
the buyer or not) for the purpose of transmission to the buyer, or delivery to a **wharfinger** for
safe custody, is **prima facie deemed to be a delivery to the buyer**. But:

- **39(2).** Unless otherwise authorised by the buyer, the seller must make **such contract with the
  carrier or wharfinger on behalf of the buyer as is reasonable** having regard to the nature of the
  goods and the other circumstances. If he omits to do so, and the goods are lost or damaged in
  transit, the buyer may **decline to treat the delivery to the carrier as a delivery to himself**,
  or may **hold the seller responsible in damages**.
- **39(3).** Where goods are sent by a route involving **sea transit**, in circumstances in which it
  is usual to insure, the seller must give the buyer **such notice as may enable him to insure them**
  during their sea transit; if he fails to do so, the goods are at the **seller's risk** during that
  sea transit.

**Section 40 — risk where goods are delivered at a distant place.** Where the seller of goods agrees
to deliver them at his own risk at a place other than that where they are when sold, the buyer must
nevertheless, unless otherwise agreed, take any risk of **deterioration in the goods necessarily
incident to the course of transit**. Natural wastage on a long journey is the buyer's.

## 14. Acceptance, refusal and the buyer's duties — sections 41 to 44

**Section 41 — buyer's right of examining the goods.** Where goods are delivered to the buyer which
he has **not previously examined**, he is **not deemed to have accepted them** unless and until he
has had a **reasonable opportunity of examining them** for the purpose of ascertaining whether they
are in conformity with the contract. Unless otherwise agreed, the seller is bound, **on request**, to
afford the buyer a reasonable opportunity of examining the goods.

**Section 42 — acceptance.** The buyer is deemed to have accepted the goods in **three** situations,
and only three:

1. When he **intimates to the seller** that he has accepted them; **or**
2. When the goods have been delivered to him and he **does any act in relation to them which is
   inconsistent with the ownership of the seller** — reselling, consuming, altering, pledging; **or**
3. When, **after the lapse of a reasonable time**, he **retains the goods without intimating to the
   seller that he has rejected them**.

Why it matters: once acceptance under s. 42 has happened, s. 13(2) converts every breach of condition
in a non-severable contract into a **breach of warranty**. The right to reject dies; the right to
damages lives.

**Section 43 — buyer not bound to return rejected goods.** Unless otherwise agreed, where goods are
delivered to the buyer and he **refuses to accept them**, having the right so to do, he is **not
bound to return them to the seller**; it is sufficient if he **intimates to the seller that he
refuses to accept them**. The buyer holds them meanwhile as a bailee, but the carriage back is the
seller's problem.

**Section 44 — buyer's liability for neglecting or refusing delivery.** When the seller is ready and
willing to deliver the goods and **requests the buyer to take delivery**, and the buyer does not
within a reasonable time after such request take delivery, he is liable to the seller for **any loss
occasioned by his neglect or refusal to take delivery**, and also for a **reasonable charge for the
care and custody of the goods**. The proviso preserves the seller's other remedy: nothing in the
section affects the seller's rights where the neglect or refusal amounts to a **repudiation** of the
contract.

## 15. The unpaid seller and his rights — sections 45 to 47

**Section 45 — who is an "unpaid seller".** The seller of goods is deemed to be an unpaid seller:

- **(a)** when the **whole of the price has not been paid or tendered**; or
- **(b)** when a **bill of exchange or other negotiable instrument** has been received as conditional
  payment, and the condition on which it was received has not been fulfilled by reason of the
  **dishonour** of the instrument or otherwise.

Sub-section (2) extends "seller" to include any person **in the position of a seller** — an agent of
the seller to whom the bill of lading has been indorsed, a consignor or agent who has himself paid or
is directly responsible for the price.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Read "the <strong>whole</strong> of the price". A seller who has received 99% of the price is
    still an unpaid seller with the full set of rights. But a <strong>tender</strong> of the price
    which the seller wrongfully refuses ends his status just as payment would — the section says
    "paid <em>or tendered</em>". And a seller who took a cheque is <em>not</em> unpaid until it
    bounces, at which point limb (b) puts him back in the box.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 46 — the unpaid seller's rights.** Notwithstanding that the property in the goods may have
passed to the buyer, the unpaid seller has, by implication of law:

**Rights against the goods (rights *in rem*):**

- **(a) a lien** on the goods for the price while he is in possession of them (ss. 47 to 49);
- **(b) a right of stopping the goods in transit** after he has parted with possession, in case of
  the **insolvency** of the buyer (ss. 50 to 52);
- **(c) a right of resale** as limited by the Act (s. 54).

Where the **property has NOT passed** to the buyer, the unpaid seller has, in addition, a right of
**withholding delivery** similar to and co-extensive with his rights of lien and stoppage in transit
(s. 46(2)).

**Rights against the buyer personally (rights *in personam*):**

- **suit for the price** (s. 55);
- **suit for damages for non-acceptance** (s. 56);
- **suit for interest and special damages** (s. 61);
- **repudiation of the contract before the due date** (s. 60).

<div class="formula">Unpaid seller: rights against the GOODS (lien · stoppage in transit · resale · withholding delivery) and rights against the BUYER (price · damages for non-acceptance · interest and special damages · repudiation before due date). Both sets survive the passing of property.</div>

## 16. Lien, stoppage in transit and resale — sections 47 to 54

**Section 47 — the unpaid seller's lien.** The unpaid seller of goods **who is in possession of
them** is entitled to retain possession until payment or tender of the price, in **three** cases
only:

1. Where the goods have been sold **without any stipulation as to credit**;
2. Where the goods have been sold **on credit, but the term of credit has expired**;
3. Where the **buyer becomes insolvent**.

Sub-section (2) is important: the seller may exercise his right of lien **notwithstanding that he is
in possession of the goods as agent or bailee for the buyer**. So a seller who agreed to hold the
goods for the buyer does not lose the lien by that fact alone.

**Section 48 — part delivery.** Where an unpaid seller has made **part delivery**, he may exercise
his right of lien on the **remainder**, unless the part delivery has been made under such
circumstances as to show an **agreement to waive the lien**.

**Section 49 — termination of lien.** The unpaid seller loses his lien:

- **(a)** when he **delivers the goods to a carrier or other bailee for transmission to the buyer
  without reserving the right of disposal**;
- **(b)** when the **buyer or his agent lawfully obtains possession** of the goods;
- **(c)** by **waiver** thereof.

And s. 49(2) adds that the seller does not lose his lien **by reason only that he has obtained a
decree for the price** of the goods.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    A lien is a right over <strong>possession</strong>, and it secures the <strong>price</strong>
    only — not storage charges, not damages, not another debt on a different contract. Two more
    traps: the lien is <strong>indivisible</strong> in the sense that part payment does not
    extinguish it (the seller may retain everything still with him until the whole price is paid),
    but it <strong>is</strong> lost once possession goes, and it revives if the seller happens to
    get the goods back only where he regains possession in his character as seller — not, for
    instance, as a repairer.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 50 — right of stoppage in transit.** Subject to the Act, when the buyer of goods becomes
**insolvent**, the unpaid seller who has **parted with the possession** of the goods has the right of
**stopping them in transit** — that is, he may resume possession of the goods as long as they are in
the course of transit, and may retain them until payment or tender of the price. Four conditions:
the seller must be **unpaid**; the buyer must be **insolvent** (s. 2(8) meaning, no adjudication
needed); the seller must have **parted with possession**; and the goods must be **in transit**.

**Section 51 — duration of transit.** Goods are deemed to be in course of transit **from the time
they are delivered to a carrier or other bailee for the purpose of transmission to the buyer, until
the buyer or his agent takes delivery of them**. Transit **ends** when:

- The buyer or his agent obtains delivery **before** the goods arrive at the appointed destination;
- After the arrival of the goods at the appointed destination, the **carrier or bailee acknowledges
  to the buyer or his agent** that he holds the goods on his behalf and continues in possession as
  bailee for him — even if a further destination is indicated by the buyer;
- The **carrier wrongfully refuses** to deliver the goods to the buyer or his agent.

Two refinements: if the goods are **rejected by the buyer** and the carrier continues in possession
of them, the transit is **not deemed to be at an end**, even if the seller has refused to receive
them back; and where part delivery has been made, the remainder may be stopped **unless** the part
delivery shows an agreement to give up possession of the whole.

**Section 52 — how stoppage is effected.** The unpaid seller may exercise the right either by
**taking actual possession** of the goods or by **giving notice of his claim to the carrier or other
bailee** in whose possession the goods are. The notice may be given either to the person in actual
possession or to his principal, and in the latter case must be given **in sufficient time to enable
the principal, by the exercise of reasonable diligence, to communicate it to his servant or agent in
time to prevent a delivery to the buyer**. On receiving the notice the carrier must **redeliver the
goods to, or according to the directions of, the seller**, and the **expenses of such redelivery are
borne by the seller**.

| | **Lien (ss. 47 to 49)** | **Stoppage in transit (ss. 50 to 52)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Possession | Seller **has** possession | Seller has **parted with** possession; goods are with a carrier |
| Buyer's insolvency | **Not** required (one of three grounds only) | **Essential** |
| What it does | Retain possession | **Regain** possession |
| Ends when | Possession is lost | Transit ends (s. 51) |
| Relationship | Where a lien ends because goods went to a carrier, stoppage may begin | Where stoppage succeeds, the seller is back in possession and holds a **lien** again |

**Section 53 — effect of sub-sale or pledge by the buyer.** The unpaid seller's right of lien or
stoppage in transit is **not affected** by any sale or other disposition of the goods which the buyer
may have made, **unless the seller has assented thereto**. Two provisos:

- Where a **document of title** to goods has been issued or lawfully transferred to any person as
  buyer or owner, and that person **transfers the document to a person who takes it in good faith and
  for consideration**, then if the last-mentioned transfer was **by way of sale**, the right of lien
  or stoppage is **defeated**; and if it was **by way of pledge or other disposition for value**, the
  right can be exercised only **subject to the rights of the transferee**.
- Where the transfer is by way of pledge, the unpaid seller may require the pledgee to have the
  amount secured **satisfied in the first instance, as far as possible, out of any other goods or
  securities** of the buyer in the hands of the pledgee.

**Section 54 — resale, the four rules.**

- **54(1).** A contract of sale is **not rescinded** by the mere exercise of a lien or a stoppage in
  transit. The contract survives; the seller merely holds the goods.
- **54(2) — the right to resell.** Where the goods are of a **perishable nature**, or where the
  unpaid seller who has exercised his right of lien or stoppage **gives notice to the buyer of his
  intention to resell**, and the buyer does not within a reasonable time pay or tender the price, the
  seller may **resell** the goods and **recover from the original buyer damages for any loss
  occasioned by his breach of contract**. But **if such notice was not given**, the seller may
  **not** recover such damages **and the buyer is entitled to the profit, if any, on the resale**.
- **54(3) — title of the new buyer.** Where the seller **resells after exercising lien or stoppage**,
  the **new buyer acquires a good title** as against the original buyer, **notwithstanding that no
  notice of the resale has been given** to the original buyer.
- **54(4) — express right of resale.** Where the seller **expressly reserves a right of resale** in
  case the buyer should make default, and on default resells the goods, the **original contract of
  sale is thereby rescinded**, but **without prejudice to any claim which the seller may have for
  damages**.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The most valuable rule in this section, and the one worth marks in a numerical: <strong>notice
    decides who keeps the profit on a resale</strong>. Notice given — the seller keeps any surplus
    and recovers any deficiency as damages. <strong>No notice — the seller may keep nothing; the
    profit belongs to the original buyer and the loss cannot be recovered.</strong> Note also that
    the seller may resell <strong>without any notice</strong> where the goods are
    <strong>perishable</strong>, and that s. 54(3) protects the new buyer's title in every case,
    notice or no notice.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Vignette 3 — the unpaid seller's ladder, with the resale arithmetic (hard)">
  <p>
    Vaibhav Steels sells 30 tonnes of TMT bars to Konark Constructions for <strong>₹18,00,000</strong>,
    payment due <strong>within 30 days of delivery</strong>. The bars are despatched by lorry on
    <strong>4 May 2026</strong> to Konark's site at Nashik. On <strong>7 May</strong>, while the lorry
    is still on the road, Vaibhav learns that Konark has stopped paying its suppliers and its cheques
    are bouncing, though no insolvency petition has been filed. Vaibhav telephones and then writes to
    the transporter, who turns the lorry back on <strong>8 May</strong>. On <strong>12 May</strong>
    Vaibhav writes to Konark: "Pay within seven days or I shall resell." Nothing is paid. On
    <strong>25 May</strong> Vaibhav resells the same 30 tonnes to another builder for
    <strong>₹19,20,000</strong>, having spent <strong>₹35,000</strong> on the redelivery of the lorry
    and ₹18,000 on storage. Advise on every step.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 1 — is Vaibhav an unpaid seller?</div>
    Yes. The whole of the price is unpaid, so s. 45(1)(a) applies. It is irrelevant that the credit
    period of 30 days had not expired — that fact goes to which <em>right</em> he may use, not to
    his status.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 2 — lien or stoppage?</div>
    He has parted with possession to a carrier for transmission to the buyer, so the <strong>lien is
    gone</strong> under s. 49(1)(a). What is available is <strong>stoppage in transit</strong>
    (s. 50). Its trigger is the buyer's <strong>insolvency</strong>, and s. 2(8) defines an insolvent
    as a person who <em>has ceased to pay his debts in the ordinary course of business</em>, whether
    or not he has been adjudged insolvent. Bounced cheques and stopped payments satisfy that; no
    petition is needed. The goods were in transit on 8 May because the buyer had not taken delivery
    (s. 51), so the stoppage was valid, and notice to the carrier is a permitted mode under s. 52.
    The <strong>₹35,000 redelivery expense is Vaibhav's own</strong> — s. 52(2) puts it on the seller.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 3 — was the resale lawful, and does notice matter?</div>
    Stopping in transit does <strong>not</strong> rescind the contract (s. 54(1)); Vaibhav is simply
    back in possession, holding a lien. He then gave <strong>notice of his intention to resell</strong>
    on 12 May and waited thirteen days. That satisfies s. 54(2): notice given, a reasonable time
    allowed, no payment. The resale on 25 May is lawful and the second buyer takes a
    <strong>good title</strong> (s. 54(3)).
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 4 — who keeps the ₹1,20,000 surplus?</div>
    Resale price ₹19,20,000 less contract price ₹18,00,000 = <strong>surplus ₹1,20,000</strong>.
    Because notice <em>was</em> given, Vaibhav <strong>retains the surplus</strong> and could also
    have recovered a deficiency as damages had the resale gone the other way. Had he resold
    <strong>without</strong> notice, s. 54(2) would have entitled Konark to the whole
    <strong>₹1,20,000</strong> profit and barred Vaibhav from recovering any loss.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 5 — the personal claims</div>
    Storage of ₹18,000 is recoverable from Konark under <strong>s. 44</strong> as a reasonable charge
    for the care and custody of goods the buyer neglected to take, and <strong>s. 61(2)</strong>
    supports interest on the price from the date of tender or of the price falling due. Vaibhav
    cannot sue for the <strong>price</strong> here: he has taken the goods back and resold them, so
    the s. 55 action is spent — his claim is for damages and charges, not ₹18,00,000.
  </div>
</Callout>

## 17. Suits for breach — sections 55 to 61

**Section 55 — suit for price.** Where under a contract of sale the **property in the goods has
passed** to the buyer and he **wrongfully neglects or refuses to pay** according to the terms of the
contract, the seller may sue him **for the price**. And where the price is payable **on a day
certain** irrespective of delivery, and the buyer wrongfully neglects or refuses to pay, the seller
may sue for the price **although the property has not passed and the goods have not been
appropriated** to the contract.

**Section 56 — damages for non-acceptance.** Where the buyer wrongfully neglects or refuses to accept
and pay for the goods, the seller may sue him for **damages for non-acceptance**.

**Section 57 — damages for non-delivery.** Where the seller wrongfully neglects or refuses to deliver
the goods to the buyer, the buyer may sue the seller for **damages for non-delivery**.

**Section 58 — specific performance.** In a suit for breach of contract to deliver **specific or
ascertained goods**, the court may, if it thinks fit, on the plaintiff's application, **direct that
the contract shall be performed specifically**, without giving the defendant the option of retaining
the goods on payment of damages. The decree may be unconditional or on such terms as to damages,
payment of the price or otherwise as the court thinks just. This is discretionary, and in practice it
is granted where damages are inadequate — a unique painting, a rare machine.

**Section 59 — remedy for breach of warranty.** Where there is a breach of warranty by the seller, or
where the buyer elects or is compelled to treat a breach of a condition as a breach of warranty, the
buyer is **not by reason only of such breach entitled to reject the goods**; but he may:

- **(a) set up against the seller the breach of warranty in diminution or extinction of the price**;
  or
- **(b) sue the seller for damages for breach of warranty.**

Sub-section (2) makes clear that a buyer who has set up the breach in diminution or extinction of the
price is **not thereby prevented** from suing for the same breach of warranty **if he has suffered
further damage**.

**Section 60 — repudiation of the contract before the due date.** Where either party to a contract of
sale repudiates the contract before the date of delivery, the other may either **treat the contract
as subsisting and wait till the date of delivery**, or **treat the contract as rescinded and sue for
damages** for the breach. This is anticipatory breach, matching s. 39 of the Contract Act.

**Section 61 — interest by way of damages and special damages.** Nothing in the Act affects the right
of the seller or the buyer to recover **interest or special damages** in any case where by law
interest or special damages may be recoverable, or to recover the money paid where the consideration
for its payment has **failed**. In the absence of a contract to the contrary, the court **may award
interest at such rate as it thinks fit** on the amount of the price — to the seller, from the date of
the tender of the goods or from the date on which the price was payable; to the buyer, in a suit for
refund of the price on a breach by the seller, from the date on which the payment was made.

**The measure of damages** comes from sections 73 and 74 of the Contract Act, applied through s. 3 of
this Act: the ordinary measure is the difference between the **contract price and the market price on
the date of the breach**, the aggrieved party is under a **duty to mitigate**, and remote or indirect
loss is not recoverable unless it was in the contemplation of both parties.

## 18. Auction sale — section 64

In the case of a sale by auction, the following rules apply **unless a different intention appears**:

- **64(1) — each lot is a separate contract.** Where goods are put up for sale in **lots**, each lot
  is prima facie deemed to be the subject of a **separate contract of sale**.
- **64(2) — when the sale is complete.** The sale is complete when the auctioneer announces its
  completion by the **fall of the hammer or in other customary manner**; and **until such
  announcement is made, any bidder may retract his bid**. A bid is only an **offer**; the fall of the
  hammer is the acceptance.
- **64(3) — the seller's right to bid.** A right to bid may be **reserved expressly** by or on behalf
  of the seller, and where such a right is expressly so reserved, but not otherwise, the seller or
  any one person on his behalf may **bid at the auction**.
- **64(4) — no secret bidding by the seller.** Where the sale is **not notified to be subject to a
  right to bid** on behalf of the seller, it is **not lawful** for the seller to bid himself or to
  employ any person to bid at such sale, or for the auctioneer knowingly to take any bid from the
  seller or any such person; and any sale contravening this rule may be treated as **fraudulent by
  the buyer**.
- **64(5) — reserve price.** The sale may be notified to be **subject to a reserved or upset price**.
  A bid below the reserve binds nobody, and an auctioneer who knocks the goods down below it acts
  without authority.
- **64(6) — pretended bidding.** If the seller makes use of **pretended bidding to raise the price**,
  the sale is **voidable at the option of the buyer**. (Puffing, in the older books.)

A **knock-out agreement** — bidders agreeing among themselves that only one of them will bid, and
that they will share the spoils afterwards — is not made void by s. 64 itself, though it may be
attacked under the Contract Act if fraud on the seller is shown, and it engages competition law.

**Section 64A — effect of a change in duty.** Unless a different intention appears, where after a
contract is made but before it is performed there is a **change in the rate of any customs or excise
duty or tax** on the goods, the party liable to pay may **add the increase to the price** or the buyer
may **deduct the decrease**, as the case may be. In an examination answer, treat this as the tax
adjustment clause that operates automatically when the parties are silent.

<Callout kind="example" label="Vignette 4 — sale or return, and an act adopting the transaction (moderate)">
  <p>
    On <strong>2 March 2026</strong> Ratan, a jeweller, sends a diamond necklace priced at
    <strong>₹4,50,000</strong> to Shalini "on sale or return, to be returned within
    <strong>fifteen days</strong> if not approved". On <strong>9 March</strong> Shalini pledges the
    necklace with a moneylender for ₹2,00,000 and never redeems it. On <strong>14 March</strong> she
    writes to Ratan rejecting the necklace. Ratan sues her for the price; she says she rejected in
    time and, in any event, the necklace is now with the moneylender. Advise. Would the answer change
    if she had done nothing at all and simply kept the necklace until 25 March?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 1 — the return window on the facts</div>
    Fifteen days from 2 March, counted excluding the day of the trigger, expires at the end of
    <strong>17 March 2026</strong>. Her letter of 14 March is inside that window, so on the face of
    it s. 24(b) had not yet passed the property.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 2 — but s. 24(a) came first</div>
    Section 24(a) passes the property the moment the buyer "does any other act adopting the
    transaction". <strong>Pledging the goods on 9 March is such an act</strong> — it is inconsistent
    with Ratan's ownership and it destroys her ability to return them
    (<em>Kirkham v Attenborough</em>). The property passed on <strong>9 March</strong>, five days
    before her letter, and a rejection after the property has passed is worth nothing.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 3 — the consequence</div>
    Property having passed, Ratan may sue for the <strong>price of ₹4,50,000</strong> under s. 55,
    not merely for damages, and the risk of the necklace being lost to the moneylender is Shalini's
    (s. 26). The moneylender's position is separately protected: Shalini was a person who had
    "bought or agreed to buy" and was in possession with the seller's consent, so under
    <strong>s. 30(2)</strong> a pledgee taking in good faith without notice holds a valid pledge.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 4 — the variation</div>
    Had she simply kept the necklace and said nothing, s. 24(b) would pass the property on the
    <strong>expiry of the fixed period, that is at the end of 17 March 2026</strong> — not on
    25 March, and not on a "reasonable time", because a time <em>was</em> fixed. Ratan's suit for the
    price would then succeed on that later date instead.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Vignette 5 — title, quantity and the measure of damages (hard)">
  <p>
    Nandini agrees on <strong>1 April 2026</strong> to buy <strong>500 quintals</strong> of turmeric
    from Prakash at <strong>₹7,200 per quintal</strong>, delivery on <strong>15 May 2026</strong>.
    On 15 May Prakash delivers only <strong>440 quintals</strong>, and Nandini accepts the short
    delivery. On the same day the market price of turmeric is <strong>₹7,900 per quintal</strong>.
    Separately, Prakash had sold Nandini a second-hand grading machine for
    <strong>₹1,40,000</strong> in January 2026; in June the true owner reclaims it, Nandini having
    used it for five months. Advise Nandini on both matters.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 1 — the short delivery, s. 37(1)</div>
    Where the seller delivers less than the contracted quantity, the buyer <strong>may reject the
    whole</strong>; if she <strong>accepts</strong> the goods so delivered, she must
    <strong>pay for them at the contract rate</strong>. Nandini accepted, so she owes
    440 × ₹7,200 = <strong>₹31,68,000</strong>, and no more.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 2 — damages for the 60 quintals not delivered</div>
    Accepting a short delivery does not forgive the shortfall. Section 57 gives a suit for damages
    for non-delivery, measured under Contract Act s. 73 as the difference between the market price on
    the date of breach and the contract price: (₹7,900 − ₹7,200) × 60 =
    <strong>₹42,000</strong>. The date that matters is <strong>15 May</strong>, the date of the
    breach — not the day she eventually buys a substitute, because of the duty to mitigate.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 3 — the machine, s. 14(a)</div>
    The implied <strong>condition</strong> that the seller has a right to sell is broken: Prakash had
    no title, so nothing passed to Nandini and nothing passed to her from the true owner's point of
    view (s. 27, <em>nemo dat</em>). Following <em>Rowland v Divall</em>, there has been a
    <strong>total failure of consideration</strong> and Nandini recovers the
    <strong>whole ₹1,40,000</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 4 — must she give credit for five months' use?</div>
    <strong>No.</strong> She contracted to buy <em>ownership</em>, and she never received any part of
    it; the use she had is not something she bargained for and no deduction is made for depreciation
    or for the benefit enjoyed. This is the counter-intuitive result the examiner is testing.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Conclusion</div>
    Nandini pays ₹31,68,000 for what she received, recovers <strong>₹42,000</strong> in damages for
    the shortfall, and recovers <strong>₹1,40,000</strong> for the machine, plus interest under
    s. 61 at the court's discretion. Net position: ₹1,82,000 receivable against a price liability of
    ₹31,68,000.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

- **"Risk passes with delivery."** It does not. **Risk passes with property** (s. 26), unless the
  parties agree otherwise or one of the two provisos applies. Goods can be in the seller's godown
  and at the buyer's risk, and vice versa.
- **Applying s. 7 or s. 8 to unascertained goods.** Both sections need **specific** goods. If a
  bulk of generic goods is destroyed, the contract stands and the seller must perform or pay.
- **Confusing s. 7 with s. 8.** Section 7 — perishing **before** the contract, **without the
  seller's knowledge**, contract is **void**. Section 8 — perishing **after** an agreement to sell
  but before risk passes, **without the fault of either party**, agreement is **avoided**.
- **"A contract with no price is void."** Section 9 supplies a **reasonable price**, which is a
  question of fact, not automatically the market price.
- **"Time is never of the essence in a sale."** Section 11 says only that **time of payment** is
  presumed not to be of the essence. **Time of delivery ordinarily is.**
- **Calling every implied term a condition, or every one a warranty.** Only **two** implied
  **warranties** exist in the Act: quiet possession, s. 14(b), and freedom from encumbrances,
  s. 14(c). Title, description, sample, fitness and merchantability are all **conditions**.
- **Forgetting the trade-name proviso to s. 16(1)** — no implied condition of fitness where the
  buyer orders a specified article under its patent or trade name and does not rely on the seller.
- **Over-reading the examination proviso to s. 16(2).** It excludes only defects **such examination
  ought to have revealed**, and only where the buyer **actually examined**. Latent defects still
  bind the seller.
- **Treating a sale where a sample was merely shown as a "sale by sample".** Section 17 requires a
  **term of the contract**, express or implied, to that effect.
- **Thinking acceptance destroys all remedies.** Section 13(2) takes away only the right to
  **reject**, and only in a **non-severable** contract. Damages survive, and s. 59 lets the buyer
  set them off against the price.
- **Mixing up s. 30(1) and s. 30(2)** — seller left in possession versus buyer let into possession.
  And remember a **hire-purchase hirer has not "agreed to buy"**, so s. 30(2) does not protect his
  transferee.
- **Section 29 applied to void contracts or to theft.** It rescues a buyer only where the seller's
  own contract was **voidable and not yet rescinded** at the time of the resale.
- **Insolvency read as "adjudicated insolvent".** Section 2(8) needs only that the buyer has
  **ceased to pay his debts in the ordinary course of business** or cannot pay them as they fall due.
- **Claiming a lien after the goods have gone to a carrier.** That ends the lien, s. 49(1)(a); what
  is left is **stoppage in transit**, and it needs the buyer's **insolvency**.
- **Resale without notice, then claiming the profit.** Section 54(2) gives the profit to the
  **original buyer** and bars the seller's damages claim where no notice of intention to resell was
  given. Notice is not needed only where the goods are **perishable** — and s. 54(3) still gives the
  new buyer a good title either way.
- **Confusing s. 54(2) resale with s. 54(4) resale.** A resale after lien or stoppage under 54(2)
  does **not** rescind the contract; a resale under an **express reserved right**, s. 54(4),
  **does** rescind it, without prejudice to damages.
- **Suing for the price when the property has not passed.** Section 55 needs the property to have
  passed, or a price payable **on a day certain**. Otherwise the claim is damages for
  **non-acceptance** under s. 56.
- **Giving credit for use in a breach of s. 14(a).** *Rowland v Divall* — the buyer recovers the
  **whole price** with no allowance for the benefit he had, because the consideration failed
  totally.
- **Thinking a bid is binding.** Under s. 64(2) a bid may be **retracted** at any time until the
  hammer falls, and each **lot** is a separate contract under s. 64(1).
- **Treating a hire-purchase as an instalment sale.** The hirer may **return the goods and walk
  away**; a buyer on instalments cannot. And the hirer can pass no title.

## One-page revision summary

**The gate.** Goods = movable property except actionable claims and money; includes stock and
shares, growing crops, and things agreed to be severed before or under the contract (s. 2(7)). Price
must be **money** (s. 2(10)).

**Section 4.** Property passed = **sale**; property still to pass = **agreement to sell**. Sale is
executed, a right in rem; agreement to sell is executory, a right in personam. Consequences follow
ownership: risk, insolvency, remedy, and whether a resale to a third party passes title.

**Kinds of goods.** Existing (specific / ascertained / unascertained), future (s. 2(6), agreement to
sell only), contingent (s. 6(2)). **s. 7** — specific goods perished before the contract without the
seller's knowledge, contract **void**. **s. 8** — specific goods perished after the agreement to sell,
without fault, before risk passes, agreement **avoided**.

**Price.** s. 9 — contract, agreed manner, course of dealing, else **reasonable price**. s. 10 —
third-party valuation fails, agreement **avoided**; goods delivered and appropriated, pay a
reasonable price; party at fault liable in damages. s. 11 — **time of payment** presumed not of the
essence.

**Conditions and warranties.** s. 12 — condition is essential (repudiate + damages); warranty is
collateral (damages only); construction, not the label, decides. s. 13 — condition sinks to warranty
by (1) waiver or election, (2) acceptance in a non-severable contract, (3) legal impossibility.

**Implied terms.** **Conditions:** title s. 14(a); description s. 15; sample s. 17 (bulk to
correspond, opportunity to compare, no latent defect); fitness for purpose s. 16(1) — purpose known,
reliance on skill, seller's course of business, *except* a specified article under a patent or trade
name; merchantable quality s. 16(2) — sale by description by a dealer, *except* defects a
buyer's actual examination ought to have revealed. **Warranties:** quiet possession s. 14(b);
freedom from encumbrances s. 14(c). s. 16(3) usage of trade; s. 16(4) express does not negative
implied unless inconsistent.

**Caveat emptor** — s. 16 opening words. Exceptions: fitness for purpose, merchantable quality,
usage of trade, sale by sample, sample and description together, fraud or concealment, and the
trade-name proviso operating the other way.

**Passing of property.** s. 18 — unascertained goods, **no property until ascertained**. s. 19 —
**intention** governs; ss. 20 to 24 are only default rules. s. 20 — specific, deliverable state,
unconditional: passes **at the contract**, payment or delivery being postponed is immaterial. s. 21 —
seller must put into a deliverable state: on doing it **and notice**. s. 22 — seller must weigh or
measure **to fix the price**: on doing it **and notice**. s. 23(1) — unconditional appropriation with
assent. s. 23(2) — delivery to a carrier without reserving disposal is appropriation. s. 24 — sale or
return: approval, or an **act adopting the transaction**, or expiry of the fixed time, or a
reasonable time. s. 25 — **reservation of the right of disposal** blocks the passing; RR or bill of
lading to the seller's order is prima facie a reservation. **s. 26 — risk follows property**, subject
to agreement, the fault-in-delay proviso, and bailee duties.

**Title.** s. 27 — *nemo dat*. Exceptions: estoppel; mercantile agent (proviso to s. 27); joint owner
in sole possession (s. 28); voidable contract not yet rescinded (s. 29); seller in possession after
sale (s. 30(1)); buyer in possession before property passes (s. 30(2)); resale by an unpaid seller
(s. 54(3)); finder (Contract Act s. 169); pledgee (Contract Act s. 176) and sales under statute or
court order.

**Performance.** ss. 31 to 32 — deliver, accept and pay; delivery and payment are **concurrent
conditions**. s. 33 — actual, symbolic, constructive. s. 35 — the buyer must **apply** for delivery.
s. 36 — place, reasonable time, acknowledgement by a third person, reasonable hour, **seller bears
the cost of putting goods into a deliverable state**. s. 37 — short delivery (reject all, or accept
and pay at the contract rate), excess (accept the contract quantity, reject the rest, or reject all;
accept the whole and pay for all), mixed (accept the conforming part or reject all). s. 38 — no duty
to accept instalments unless agreed. s. 39 — delivery to a carrier is prima facie delivery to the
buyer, but the seller must make a reasonable contract of carriage and give notice enabling sea
insurance. s. 40 — the buyer bears deterioration necessarily incident to transit. s. 41 — right to
examine. **s. 42 — acceptance: intimation, an act inconsistent with the seller's ownership, or
retention beyond a reasonable time without intimating rejection.** s. 43 — no duty to return
rejected goods. s. 44 — liability for neglecting delivery: loss plus a reasonable charge for care and
custody.

**Unpaid seller.** s. 45 — whole price unpaid or a negotiable instrument dishonoured. s. 46 —
against the goods: **lien, stoppage in transit, resale** (and withholding delivery where property has
not passed); against the buyer: **price (s. 55), damages for non-acceptance (s. 56), interest and
special damages (s. 61), repudiation before the due date (s. 60)**. s. 47 — lien in three cases: no
credit stipulated, credit term expired, buyer insolvent. s. 49 — lien lost by delivery to a carrier
without reserving disposal, by the buyer lawfully obtaining possession, or by waiver; not lost by
obtaining a decree for the price. ss. 50 to 52 — stoppage needs **insolvency**, goods still in
transit; effected by taking possession or notice; **redelivery expenses on the seller**. s. 53 —
sub-sale does not affect the rights unless assented to, subject to the document-of-title provisos.
**s. 54 — lien or stoppage does not rescind; resale on notice (or of perishables) lets the seller
keep the surplus and recover the deficiency; resale without notice gives the profit to the buyer and
bars damages; the new buyer always gets a good title; an express reserved right of resale rescinds
the contract.**

**Suits.** s. 55 price (property passed, or price due on a day certain); s. 56 non-acceptance; s. 57
non-delivery; s. 58 specific performance for specific or ascertained goods; s. 59 breach of warranty
— diminish or extinguish the price, or sue; s. 60 anticipatory breach; s. 61 interest and special
damages. Measure of damages: **contract price versus market price on the date of breach**, with a
duty to mitigate.

**Auction — s. 64.** Each lot a separate contract; sale complete on the fall of the hammer, and a bid
may be **retracted** until then; the seller may bid only if the right is expressly reserved; secret
bidding by the seller may be treated as fraudulent; a reserve price may be notified; **pretended
bidding makes the sale voidable at the buyer's option**. s. 64A — a change in duty or tax after the
contract is added to or deducted from the price.
