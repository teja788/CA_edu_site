Four architects in Kochi set up a practice as a partnership. One of them, working alone on a
mall project, signs off a drawing with an error nobody else ever saw. The slab cracks. The
claim is ₹3,20,00,000 and the firm's assets are ₹40,00,000.

Under the Indian Partnership Act 1932 the answer is brutal and short. Section 25 makes every
partner **liable jointly with all the others and also severally** for every act of the firm.
The three architects who never touched the drawing lose their flats, their cars and their
savings for a mistake made by the fourth.

That single sentence is why the Limited Liability Partnership Act 2008 exists. It offers the
internal flexibility of a partnership — partners writing their own rules in their own
agreement — sitting inside the external shell of a body corporate, so that **the firm's debts
are the firm's, not the partners'**. The whole chapter is an elaboration of that trade: what you
gain (limited liability, perpetual succession, a separate legal person), what you pay for it
(public filings, a Registrar, statutory deadlines), and the narrow set of situations in which the
shell is set aside and a partner pays personally after all.

The Act is Act 6 of 2009, it received assent on **7 January 2009**, its main provisions came into
force on **31 March 2009**, and it extends to the whole of India. It was substantially amended by
the **LLP (Amendment) Act 2021**, which introduced the "small LLP", converted a long list of
criminal offences into civil penalties, and changed the residence test for a designated partner.
Read the numbers in this chapter as they stand after that amendment.

## 1. Why a third form of organisation was needed

Before 2009 a person carrying on business in India chose between two shapes, and each one failed
a different kind of user.

- **A partnership firm** is cheap, private and flexible. Nothing needs to be filed, the partners
  write their own deed, and no auditor is compelled on them. But the firm is **not a legal
  person**: it has no existence apart from the partners, it cannot own property in its own name,
  it does not survive a change of partners, and — the fatal point — **every partner's personal
  estate answers for every other partner's business acts** under s. 25 of the 1932 Act.
- **A company** solves the liability problem completely. It is a separate legal person with
  perpetual succession, and a shareholder risks only what he has agreed to put in. But it is a
  rigid instrument: board meetings, general meetings, directors' duties, a memorandum and
  articles, statutory registers, compulsory audit, and a regulatory load designed for enterprises
  with outside investors.

The gap between them is occupied by exactly the businesses India has most of: professional
practices, family enterprises, small service firms, start-ups with two or three founders and no
outside shareholder. They want the internal informality of a firm and the external protection of
a company. The LLP is the statutory answer.

**The three ideas that define the form.**

1. **A body corporate — s. 3(1).** An LLP is a body corporate formed and incorporated under the
   Act and is **a legal entity separate from that of its partners**. It sues and is sued in its
   own name, owns its own property, and its debts are its own.
2. **Perpetual succession — ss. 3(2) and 3(3).** An LLP has perpetual succession, and **any
   change in the partners does not affect the existence, rights or liabilities of the LLP**.
   Death, retirement or insolvency of a partner does not dissolve it.
3. **Internal freedom — s. 23.** The mutual rights and duties of the partners, and of the LLP and
   its partners, are governed by the **LLP agreement**. The Act supplies defaults (the First
   Schedule) only where the agreement is silent.

**Section 4 — the Partnership Act is switched off.** Save as otherwise provided, the provisions
of the **Indian Partnership Act 1932 shall not apply to an LLP**. This is a one-line section with
enormous consequences, and it is the single most-tested proposition in the chapter. Nothing you
learnt in Chapter 4 about implied authority, about a partner being the agent of the other
partners, or about six per cent interest on advances, carries over unless the LLP Act says so
itself.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Students write that an LLP is "a partnership with limited liability". That sentence is a
    useful slogan and a wrong statement of law. An LLP is <strong>a body corporate</strong>
    (s. 3) to which the Partnership Act <strong>does not apply</strong> (s. 4). Its partners are
    partners because s. 2(1)(q) and the LLP agreement say so, not because the 1932 Act says so.
    Start every LLP answer from s. 3 and s. 4 and you will not go wrong.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. What an LLP is — the definitions and the salient features

**The definitions you must be able to reproduce.** They sit in s. 2(1) and the examiner picks
them off one at a time.

- **Limited liability partnership — s. 2(1)(n).** A partnership **formed and registered under
  this Act**. Registration is not optional as it is for a firm; without registration there is no
  LLP at all.
- **LLP agreement — s. 2(1)(o).** Any **written** agreement between the partners of the LLP, or
  between the LLP and its partners, which determines the **mutual rights and duties of the
  partners** and their **rights and duties in relation to that LLP**. Note the word *written*: an
  oral understanding is not an LLP agreement, and where there is none the First Schedule steps in.
- **Partner — s. 2(1)(q).** In relation to an LLP, **any person who becomes a partner in the LLP
  in accordance with the LLP agreement**.
- **Designated partner — s. 2(1)(j).** Any partner **designated as such pursuant to section 7**.
- **Business — s. 2(1)(d).** Includes **every trade, profession, service and occupation**, except
  any activity which the Central Government may by notification exclude. Wider than the 1932 Act's
  "business", and wide enough to cover professional practice.
- **Body corporate — s. 2(1)(e).** A company as defined in s. 2(20) of the Companies Act 2013,
  and **includes** an LLP registered under this Act, an LLP incorporated outside India, and a
  company incorporated outside India. It **does not include** a corporation sole, a co-operative
  society registered under any law, or any other body corporate which the Central Government
  notifies as excluded.
- **Foreign LLP — s. 2(1)(m).** An LLP **formed, incorporated or registered outside India** which
  **establishes a place of business within India**.
- **Financial year — s. 2(1)(l).** The period from **1 April of a year to 31 March of the
  following year**, with a proviso: where an LLP is incorporated **after 30 September** of a year,
  the financial year **may end on 31 March of the year next following**. So an LLP incorporated in
  November 2026 may run its first accounting period to 31 March 2028.
- **Small LLP — s. 2(1)(ta), inserted in 2021.** An LLP whose **contribution does not exceed
  ₹25,00,000** (extendable by rules up to ₹5,00,00,000) **and** whose **turnover, as per the
  Statement of Account and Solvency for the immediately preceding financial year, does not exceed
  ₹40,00,000** (extendable by rules up to ₹50,00,00,000). The category matters because s. 76A
  halves the penalties payable by a small LLP and its partners.

**The salient features, as a list.** This is a guaranteed 4- to 6-mark question, and it is best
learnt as eleven short points, each tied to a section.

1. **A body corporate and a separate legal entity** from its partners — s. 3(1).
2. **Perpetual succession** — s. 3(2); a change of partners does not touch the LLP — s. 3(3).
3. **Created only by registration** under the Act — s. 2(1)(n) read with s. 12; the certificate of
   incorporation is **conclusive evidence** of incorporation — s. 12(4).
4. **The Partnership Act 1932 does not apply** — s. 4.
5. **Minimum two partners**, with no maximum — s. 6(1). Compare the ceiling of **50** persons that
   binds a general partnership through s. 464 of the Companies Act 2013 read with Rule 10 of the
   Companies (Miscellaneous) Rules 2014.
6. **At least two designated partners who are individuals, one of them resident in India** —
   s. 7(1).
7. **Every partner is the agent of the LLP, but not of the other partners** — s. 26. This is the
   hinge of the whole liability scheme.
8. **Liability of a partner is limited**; an obligation of the LLP is **solely the obligation of
   the LLP**, met out of the property of the LLP — ss. 27(3), 27(4) and 28(1).
9. **Mutual rights and duties are governed by the LLP agreement**, with the First Schedule as the
   default — s. 23.
10. **It can sue and be sued, own property, and have a common seal if it chooses** — s. 14. Note
    "if it decides to have one": the common seal is optional.
11. **Contribution may be in cash or in kind, including services** — s. 32; and the LLP must file
    a **Statement of Account and Solvency** and an **annual return** every year — ss. 34 and 35.

<div class="formula">The LLP identity in one line: BODY CORPORATE (s. 3) + PARTNERSHIP ACT SWITCHED OFF (s. 4) + AGENT OF THE LLP ONLY (s. 26) + OBLIGATION SOLELY THAT OF THE LLP (s. 27(3)) + LIABILITY NOT PERSONAL MERELY BY BEING A PARTNER (s. 28(1)).</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Two definitional traps that cost easy marks. First, <strong>an LLP has no maximum number of
    partners</strong> — the cap of 20 (or 10 for banking) belongs to a general partnership, not to
    an LLP. Second, the <strong>common seal is optional</strong> under s. 14(c) ("if it decides to
    have one"), so an option saying an LLP "must have a common seal" is always wrong.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. LLP compared with a general partnership firm

This is the comparison the examiner asks most often, and every row of it flows from one fact: an
LLP is a body corporate and a firm is not.

| Point | **Partnership firm (Act of 1932)** | **LLP (Act of 2008)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Governing law | Indian Partnership Act 1932 | LLP Act 2008; the 1932 Act does **not** apply (s. 4) |
| Legal status | **No separate legal entity**; the firm is its partners | A **body corporate**, a legal entity separate from its partners (s. 3(1)) |
| How it comes into existence | By **agreement**; registration is optional | Only by **registration** with the Registrar (ss. 11, 12) |
| Perpetual succession | None; death or retirement affects the firm | **Perpetual succession**; a change of partners does not affect it (s. 3(2), (3)) |
| Liability of a partner | **Unlimited**, joint and several, for all acts of the firm (s. 25 of 1932) | **Limited**; the obligation is solely that of the LLP (ss. 27(3), 28(1)) |
| Whose agent is a partner | Agent of the firm **and of the other partners** (s. 18 of 1932) | Agent of the **LLP only, not of the other partners** (s. 26) |
| Maximum partners | 50 (Companies Act 2013 read with the rules) | **No limit** |
| Who may be a partner | Only persons competent to contract; **a company cannot** be a partner in most readings | An **individual or a body corporate** (s. 5) |
| Property | Belongs to the partners jointly | Belongs to the **LLP** (s. 14(b)) |
| Written agreement | Not compulsory; a deed is usual | The LLP agreement must be **in writing** (s. 2(1)(o)) and filed (s. 23(2)) |
| Filing of accounts | Not required under the 1932 Act | **Statement of Account and Solvency** and **annual return** filed every year (ss. 34, 35) |
| Audit | Not required under the 1932 Act | Required unless turnover and contribution are both under the prescribed thresholds |
| Public inspection | Books are private | Incorporation document, partner details, SAS and annual return are **open to inspection** (s. 36) |
| Minor | May be **admitted to the benefits** of partnership (s. 30 of 1932) | **No corresponding provision**; a minor cannot be a partner |
| Effect of non-registration | s. 69 of 1932 bars the firm's suits | Does not arise — an unregistered LLP simply does not exist |
| Winding up | Dissolution under ss. 39 to 55 of the 1932 Act | Winding up voluntarily or by the **Tribunal** (ss. 63, 64) |

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The row that decides the most MCQs is <strong>whose agent a partner is</strong>. Under s. 18
    of the 1932 Act a partner is the agent of the firm <em>and</em> of the other partners — which
    is exactly why every partner is personally on the hook. Section 26 of the LLP Act keeps the
    first half and deletes the second: <strong>agent of the LLP, but not of other partners</strong>.
    Every liability question in this chapter can be answered by asking who the partner was acting
    as agent for.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. LLP compared with a company

The LLP borrows the corporate shell, so the differences here are about **internal governance and
compliance load**, not about legal personality.

| Point | **Company (Companies Act 2013)** | **LLP (LLP Act 2008)** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Legal status | Separate legal person, body corporate | Separate legal person, body corporate |
| Charter documents | **Memorandum and Articles of Association** | **LLP agreement** (s. 23), or the First Schedule by default |
| Who manages | The **Board of Directors**; members as such do not manage | **Every partner may take part** in management (First Schedule, cl. 4), unless the agreement says otherwise |
| Relation of owner to entity | A member is **not an agent** of the company | Every partner **is an agent** of the LLP (s. 26) |
| Minimum owners | 2 (private), 7 (public), 1 (OPC) | **2 partners** (s. 6(1)) |
| Persons responsible for compliance | Directors, KMP | **Designated partners** (s. 8) |
| Identification number | DIN for a director | **DPIN** for a designated partner (s. 7(6)) |
| Meetings | Board meetings and general meetings are **compulsory**, with statutory notice and quorum | **No statutory meetings at all**; decisions by a majority in number, each partner one vote (First Schedule, cl. 7) |
| Statutory registers and minutes | An extensive set is prescribed | Only that decisions be **recorded in minutes within 30 days** (First Schedule, cl. 8) |
| Audit | **Compulsory for every company** | Only above the prescribed turnover or contribution thresholds |
| Distribution to owners | Dividend, out of profits, under the Companies Act rules | **Profit share** under the LLP agreement; no dividend machinery |
| Transfer of interest | Shares are transferable; the transferee becomes a member | A partner's **economic rights** are transferable (s. 42) but the transferee gets **no management rights** |
| Capital | Share capital, with maintenance rules | **Contribution** (s. 32), which may include services |
| Charge on assets | Registrable under s. 77 of the Companies Act | No corresponding charge-registration provision in the LLP Act |
| Winding up | Companies Act 2013 and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code | ss. 63 to 65 and the rules made under them |

<div class="formula">Reading of the three forms: FIRM = flexible + unlimited liability + no legal person · LLP = flexible + limited liability + legal person · COMPANY = rigid + limited liability + legal person. The LLP is the middle column, and every one of its rules is an attempt to buy the second and third boxes without the first one's cost.</div>

## 5. Advantages and disadvantages of the LLP form

**Advantages.**

1. **Limited liability.** A partner's personal estate is not exposed for the LLP's obligations
   merely because he is a partner (s. 28(1)). This is the point of the whole statute.
2. **Separate legal personality and perpetual succession.** The LLP owns its own contracts,
   licences, leases and intellectual property, and they survive every change of partners
   (ss. 3, 14).
3. **Internal flexibility.** No board, no general meetings, no quorum rules. The partners write
   what they like into the LLP agreement, and the First Schedule fills the gaps.
4. **No cap on the number of partners**, so the form scales in a way a general partnership cannot.
5. **Lower compliance cost than a company** — two annual filings, and audit only above the
   thresholds.
6. **A body corporate may be a partner** (s. 5), which lets group structures and joint ventures
   use the form.
7. **Contribution may be in kind or in services** (s. 32), which suits professional and
   knowledge-based practices where the valuable input is not cash.
8. **Easy conversion routes in** — a firm, a private company or an unlisted public company may
   convert (ss. 55 to 57), and the whole undertaking vests in the LLP by operation of law
   (s. 58(3)).

**Disadvantages.**

1. **Publicity.** The incorporation document, the partner particulars, the Statement of Account
   and Solvency and the annual return are all open to public inspection (s. 36). A firm's affairs
   are private; an LLP's are not.
2. **Statutory deadlines with penalties attached**, several of which run per day (ss. 13(4), 69).
3. **The shell can be pierced.** Section 30 makes liability **unlimited** for the LLP and for
   partners who acted with intent to defraud creditors, and s. 28(2) leaves a partner personally
   liable for **his own** wrongful act or omission.
4. **No equity fund-raising machinery.** An LLP cannot issue shares, cannot list, and cannot take
   in an equity investor in the way a company can — which is why venture-funded start-ups
   generally choose a private company.
5. **A minimum of two partners at all times**, and s. 6(2) attaches personal liability to a sole
   surviving partner who carries on for more than six months.
6. **Tax and regulatory treatment differs from a company's**, and several sectoral regulators and
   lenders still write their forms for companies and firms only.
7. **Every partner may bind the LLP** as its agent (s. 26), so the internal restrictions in the
   agreement protect the partners against each other but not always against an outsider who does
   not know of them (s. 27(1)).

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    "Limited liability means a partner can never be made to pay anything." No. Section 28(1)
    protects him from liability arising <strong>solely by reason of being a partner</strong>.
    It leaves untouched (i) his liability for <strong>his own</strong> wrongful act or omission —
    s. 28(2); (ii) <strong>unlimited</strong> liability where he acted with intent to defraud —
    s. 30(1); (iii) personal liability of a <strong>sole remaining partner</strong> after six
    months — s. 6(2); (iv) liability by <strong>holding out</strong> — s. 29; and (v) the
    designated partner's liability for penalties imposed on the LLP — s. 8(b).
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. Incorporation — sections 11 and 12

**Section 11 — the incorporation document.** Sub-section (1) sets three requirements, and an MCQ
is usually built by removing one of them:

- **11(1)(a).** **Two or more persons associated for carrying on a lawful business with a view to
  profit** shall subscribe their names to an incorporation document. Read all three limbs:
  *lawful*, *business* as widely defined in s. 2(1)(d), and *with a view to profit*. A body
  formed for charity cannot be an LLP.
- **11(1)(b).** The incorporation document shall be filed, in the prescribed manner and with the
  prescribed fee, with the **Registrar of the State in which the registered office of the LLP is
  to be situated**.
- **11(1)(c).** There shall be filed along with it a **statement in the prescribed form** that all
  the requirements of the Act and the rules in respect of incorporation and matters precedent and
  incidental have been complied with. It must be made by **two people**: (i) an **advocate, a
  Company Secretary, a Chartered Accountant or a Cost Accountant engaged in the formation** of the
  LLP, and (ii) **anyone who subscribed his name** to the incorporation document.

**Section 11(2) — what the incorporation document must state.** Seven items:

1. It shall be **in the prescribed form**;
2. the **name** of the LLP;
3. the **proposed business** of the LLP;
4. the **address of the registered office**;
5. the **name and address of each person who is to be a partner** on incorporation;
6. the **name and address of each person who is to be a designated partner** on incorporation;
7. **such other information** concerning the proposed LLP as may be prescribed.

**Section 11(3) — a false statement.** A person who makes the s. 11(1)(c) statement which he
**knows to be false**, or **does not believe to be true**, is punishable with **imprisonment up to
two years** and with a **fine of not less than ₹10,000 and up to ₹5,00,000**. Note that this is
one of the provisions the 2021 amendment left as an offence rather than converting into a
penalty, because it involves dishonesty.

**Section 12 — incorporation by registration.**

- **12(1).** When the requirements of s. 11(1)(b) and (c) have been complied with, the Registrar
  shall **retain the incorporation document** and, unless the requirement of s. 11(1)(a) has not
  been complied with, shall **within fourteen days** (a) **register** the incorporation document,
  and (b) **give a certificate that the LLP is incorporated** by the name specified in it.
- **12(2).** The Registrar **may accept** the s. 11(1)(c) statement as **sufficient evidence** that
  the s. 11(1)(a) requirement has been complied with. He is not obliged to go behind it.
- **12(3).** The certificate shall be **signed by the Registrar and authenticated by his official
  seal**.
- **12(4).** The certificate is **conclusive evidence** that the LLP is incorporated by the name
  specified in it. Once issued, nobody may argue in any proceeding that the LLP does not exist or
  that some step in its formation was defective.

**Section 14 — the effect of registration.** On registration an LLP shall, **by its name**, be
capable of:

- **(a)** suing and being sued;
- **(b)** acquiring, owning, holding and developing or disposing of **property**, whether movable
  or immovable, tangible or intangible;
- **(c)** having a **common seal, if it decides to have one**;
- **(d)** doing and suffering such other acts and things as **bodies corporate may lawfully do and
  suffer**.

**The e-filing route in practice.** The Act speaks of "the prescribed form"; the LLP Rules 2009
and the MCA portal supply it, and the examiner expects the names.

| Step | Instrument | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Digital signature | DSC for the proposed designated partners | Every LLP filing is signed electronically |
| Name | **RUN-LLP** (Reserve Unique Name — LLP) | A stand-alone web service to reserve a name under s. 16 |
| Incorporation | **FiLLiP** (Form for Incorporation of LLP) | The integrated form: it can reserve the name, apply for **DPIN** for up to two proposed designated partners who do not have one, and file the incorporation document, all in one filing |
| Certificate | Certificate of incorporation with the **LLPIN** | Issued under s. 12(1)(b); conclusive evidence under s. 12(4) |
| Agreement | **Form 3** | The LLP agreement, filed under s. 23(2) within **30 days** of incorporation |
| Partner details | **Form 4** | Consent and particulars of partners and designated partners under ss. 7(4) and 25(2) |

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Three numbers from this section are worth memorising because options are built out of them:
    the Registrar acts within <strong>fourteen days</strong> (s. 12(1)); the incorporation
    certificate is <strong>conclusive evidence</strong> (s. 12(4)); and the LLP agreement goes in
    within <strong>thirty days</strong> of incorporation (r. 21). Note also that FiLLiP allows
    DPIN to be applied for by <strong>at most two</strong> proposed designated partners who do not
    already hold one — a third must obtain a DPIN separately before he can be appointed.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. Name and registered office — sections 13 and 15 to 21

**Section 15 — the name.**

- **15(1).** Every LLP shall have either the words **"limited liability partnership"** or the
  acronym **"LLP"** as the **last words of its name**.
- **15(2).** No LLP shall be registered by a name which in the opinion of the Central Government
  is **(a) undesirable**, or **(b) identical with or too nearly resembling** the name of any other
  partnership firm, LLP, body corporate, a registered trade mark, or a trade mark which is the
  subject matter of a pending application under the Trade Marks Act 1999.

**Section 16 — reservation of name.** A person may apply to the Registrar, in the prescribed form
and on the prescribed fee, to reserve a name as **(a) the name of a proposed LLP** or **(b) the
name to which an existing LLP proposes to change**. If satisfied that the name is not liable to be
rejected on any s. 15(2) ground, the Registrar **reserves it for three months** from the date of
intimation.

**Section 17 — direction to change the name.** Where the Central Government is satisfied that an
LLP has been registered — whether through inadvertence or otherwise, and whether originally or by
a change of name — under a name which falls foul of s. 15(2) or which is identical with or too
nearly resembles the name of any other LLP or body corporate so as to be likely to be mistaken
for it, the Central Government may **direct the LLP to change its name**, and the LLP shall comply
**within three months** of the direction. If it does not, the Central Government **allots a new
name** to the LLP, which the Registrar enters in the register in place of the old name and for
which a **fresh certificate of incorporation** is issued. The LLP may afterwards change that
allotted name by following the ordinary s. 16 and s. 19 route.

**Section 18 — application by a person aggrieved.** A person whose registered trade mark, or whose
own earlier name, is nearly resembled by the name of an LLP registered later may apply for a
direction requiring the LLP to change its name. The application must be made within the period
prescribed for it, and the direction, if given, is complied with in the same way as under s. 17.

**Section 19 — voluntary change of name.** Any LLP may change its registered name by filing notice
of the change with the Registrar in the prescribed form and on payment of the prescribed fee.

**Section 20 — improper use of the words.** A person or persons carrying on business under a name
or title of which "limited liability partnership" or "LLP" or any contraction or imitation is the
last word, when not so incorporated, is **punishable with a fine of not less than ₹50,000 and up
to ₹5,00,000**.

**Section 21 — publication of name and limited liability.** Every LLP shall ensure that its
**invoices, official correspondence and publications** bear:

- **(a)** the **name**, the **address of its registered office** and the **registration number** of
  the LLP; and
- **(b)** a **statement that it is registered with limited liability**.

The point of s. 21(b) is notice: anyone dealing with the LLP is told, on the face of the paper,
that he is dealing with an entity of limited liability and should look to its assets, not to the
partners'. Contravention attracts a fine under s. 21(2).

**Section 13 — the registered office.**

- **13(1).** Every LLP shall have a **registered office** to which all communications and notices
  may be addressed and where they shall be received.
- **13(2).** A document may be served on an LLP, or on a partner or designated partner, by
  **post under a certificate of posting, by registered post, or in any other prescribed manner**,
  at the **registered office** and at any **other address specifically declared** by the LLP for
  the purpose.
- **13(3).** An LLP may **change the place of its registered office** and shall **file notice of
  the change with the Registrar** in the prescribed form and manner and subject to the prescribed
  conditions; and **any such change takes effect only upon such filing**. The rules give the LLP
  **thirty days** from complying with the requirements to file that notice.
- **13(4).** On default, the LLP **and its every partner** are liable to a penalty of **₹500 for
  each day** during which the default continues, **subject to a maximum of ₹50,000** for the LLP
  and for every partner.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Read the last words of s. 13(3): a change of registered office <strong>takes effect only upon
    filing</strong>, not on the day the partners resolve on it and not on the day the furniture
    moves. Until the notice is filed, a summons served at the old address is good service. And the
    s. 13(4) penalty is <strong>capped</strong> at ₹50,000, so a very long default does not run up
    an unlimited figure — the cap is exactly what a numerical option will leave out.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. Partners — who may be one, and the minimum of two — sections 5, 6 and 22

**Section 5 — who may be a partner.** **Any individual or body corporate** may be a partner in an
LLP. The proviso disqualifies an **individual** in three cases:

1. He has been **found to be of unsound mind** by a court of competent jurisdiction and the
   finding is **in force**;
2. He is an **undischarged insolvent**; or
3. He has **applied to be adjudicated an insolvent and his application is pending**.

Two observations the examiner tests. First, the disqualifications bite on **individuals only** —
they say nothing about a body corporate. Second, a **discharged** insolvent is not disqualified;
the bar is on the *undischarged* insolvent and on the applicant whose petition is still pending.
There is **no provision in the LLP Act corresponding to s. 30 of the Partnership Act**, so a
**minor cannot be admitted** even to the benefits of an LLP.

**Section 6 — the minimum number of partners.**

- **6(1).** Every LLP shall have **at least two partners**. The Act sets **no maximum**.
- **6(2).** If at any time the number of partners is **reduced below two**, and the LLP **carries
  on business for more than six months** while the number is so reduced, then the person **who is
  the only partner** during the time it so carries on business after those six months **and who
  knows** that it is carrying on business with him alone shall be **liable personally for the
  obligations of the LLP incurred during that period**.

Take s. 6(2) apart, because every one of its limbs supplies a wrong option:

| Limb | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| "reduced below two" | The trigger is a single partner, not a vacancy in the designated partners |
| "carries on business for more than six months" | The **first six months are free**; liability attaches only to obligations incurred **after** that period |
| "the only partner ... during the time that it so carries on business" | Only the sole partner is exposed; a partner who left is not |
| "and has the knowledge of the fact" | **Knowledge is an ingredient.** A sole partner who genuinely does not know is not caught |
| "liable personally for the obligations of the LLP incurred during that period" | The liability is **personal and additional**; the LLP remains liable too, and it does not extend to obligations incurred before the six months ran out |

Note also what s. 6(2) does **not** do: it does **not dissolve** the LLP. The LLP goes on existing
as a body corporate. What it does is give the Tribunal a **ground to wind it up** under s. 64(b)
where the number has been below two for more than six months.

**Section 22 — who the partners are.** On incorporation, the persons who **subscribed their names
to the incorporation document** are the partners; **any other person may become a partner by and
in accordance with the LLP agreement**. So admission of a new partner is an agreement question
first and a filing question second.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Compare the three "two-person" rules and keep them apart. <strong>Section 6(1)</strong> — at
    least two <em>partners</em>. <strong>Section 7(1)</strong> — at least two
    <em>designated partners</em>, who must be <em>individuals</em>, one of them
    <em>resident in India</em>. <strong>Section 9 proviso</strong> — if there is no designated
    partner, or only one, <em>every partner is deemed to be a designated partner</em>. An MCQ that
    reduces an LLP to one designated partner is testing s. 9, not s. 6.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Designated partners and the DPIN — sections 7, 8, 9 and 10

A designated partner is a partner who has been loaded with the LLP's **compliance obligations**.
He is not a superior class of owner; his extra status is a burden, not a privilege.

**Section 7 — appointment.**

- **7(1).** Every LLP shall have **at least two designated partners who are individuals**, and **at
  least one of them shall be a resident in India**. Where **all the partners are bodies
  corporate**, or where the partners are a mix of individuals and bodies corporate, **at least two
  individuals who are partners of the LLP, or nominees of those bodies corporate**, shall act as
  designated partners. So an LLP whose partners are two companies still produces two individual
  designated partners, by nomination.
- **The Explanation** defines **"resident in India"** as a person who has **stayed in India for
  not less than 120 days during the financial year**. The LLP (Amendment) Act 2021 reduced this
  from 182 days; use **120** for the attempts this chapter covers.
- **7(2).** Subject to 7(1): if the **incorporation document** (a) specifies who are to be
  designated partners, those persons are designated partners on incorporation; or (b) states that
  **each of the partners from time to time is to be a designated partner**, then **every partner
  is** one. And any partner **may become** a designated partner, or **cease to be** one, **in
  accordance with the LLP agreement**.
- **7(3).** An individual shall not become a designated partner unless he has given his **prior
  consent** to act as such, in the prescribed form and manner.
- **7(4).** Every LLP shall file with the Registrar the particulars of **every individual who has
  given consent** to act as designated partner **within thirty days of his appointment**.
- **7(5).** An individual eligible to be a designated partner shall satisfy the prescribed
  conditions and requirements.
- **7(6).** Every designated partner shall obtain a **Designated Partner Identification Number
  (DPIN)** from the Central Government, and **ss. 153 to 159 of the Companies Act 2013 apply
  mutatis mutandis**. A person who already holds a **DIN** does not need a separate DPIN; the two
  numbers are interchangeable for this purpose.

**Section 8 — liabilities of designated partners.** Unless expressly provided otherwise, a
designated partner is:

- **(a) responsible for the doing of all acts, matters and things** required to be done by the LLP
  to comply with the Act — including **filing any document, return, statement and the like report**
  — and as may be specified in the LLP agreement; and
- **(b) liable to all penalties imposed on the LLP** for any contravention of those provisions.

Clause (b) is severe and is often the answer to a scenario question: the penalty falls on the LLP,
and the designated partner carries it too, whether or not he personally did the thing left undone.

**Section 9 — changes in designated partners.** An LLP **may appoint a designated partner within
thirty days of a vacancy** arising for any reason, and ss. 7(4) and 7(5) apply to the new
appointee. The **proviso** is the examinable half: **if no designated partner is appointed, or if
at any time there is only one designated partner, each partner shall be deemed to be a designated
partner**. The consequence is that every partner then carries the s. 8 responsibilities and the
s. 8(b) exposure to penalties.

**Section 10 — the consequence of contravention.** After the 2021 amendment these are **civil
penalties**, not offences:

| Contravention | Penalty on the LLP and on every partner | Continuing | Maximum |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| s. 7(1) — fewer than two designated partners, or none resident | **₹10,000** | **₹100 for each day** after the first | **₹1,00,000** for the LLP; **₹50,000** for every partner |
| s. 7(4), s. 7(5) or s. 9 — particulars not filed, conditions not met, vacancy not filled | **₹5,000** | **₹100 for each day** after the first | **₹50,000** for the LLP; **₹25,000** for every partner |

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Four points students reverse. (i) A designated partner must be an <strong>individual</strong>;
    a body corporate can never itself be one — it <em>nominates</em> an individual. (ii) The
    residence test is <strong>120 days in the financial year</strong> after the 2021 amendment, not
    182. (iii) <strong>Prior consent</strong> under s. 7(3) comes before appointment; the
    <strong>30-day filing</strong> under s. 7(4) comes after. (iv) The s. 9 proviso deems every
    partner to be a designated partner where there is <strong>one or none</strong> — it does not
    dissolve the LLP and it does not stop the s. 10 penalty running.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. The LLP agreement and the First Schedule defaults — section 23

**Section 23 — the relationship of partners.**

- **23(1).** Save as otherwise provided by the Act, the **mutual rights and duties of the
  partners**, and the **mutual rights and duties of the LLP and its partners**, shall be governed
  by the **LLP agreement** between the partners, or between the LLP and its partners.
- **23(2).** The LLP agreement **and any changes made in it** shall be **filed with the Registrar**
  in the prescribed form and manner and on the prescribed fee. The rules require the filing within
  **thirty days** of incorporation, and within thirty days of any change.
- **23(3).** An agreement in writing made **before incorporation** between the persons who
  subscribe their names to the incorporation document **may impose obligations on the LLP**,
  **provided it is ratified by all the partners after incorporation**. Without ratification the
  pre-incorporation agreement binds only its signatories, because the LLP did not exist to
  contract.
- **23(4).** **In the absence of agreement as to any matter**, the mutual rights and duties of the
  partners, and of the LLP and the partners, **shall be determined by the provisions relating to
  that matter as are set out in the First Schedule**.

Read s. 23(4) precisely: the First Schedule operates **matter by matter**, not all-or-nothing. An
LLP agreement that fixes profit shares but says nothing about admitting new partners takes its
profit shares from the agreement and its admission rule from the Schedule.

**The First Schedule — the thirteen default provisions.** Learn them as a list; a 6-mark question
asks for them, and MCQs are written by changing one word.

1. **All the partners are entitled to share equally in the capital, profits and losses** of the
   LLP. Note "equally", and note that it is **regardless of what each contributed**.
2. The **LLP shall indemnify each partner** in respect of payments made and personal liabilities
   incurred by him (a) in the **ordinary and proper conduct** of the business of the LLP, or
   (b) in or about anything **necessarily done for the preservation** of the business or property
   of the LLP.
3. **Every partner shall indemnify the LLP** for any loss caused to it **by his fraud** in the
   conduct of the business of the LLP.
4. **Every partner may take part in the management** of the LLP.
5. **No partner is entitled to remuneration** for acting in the business or management of the LLP.
6. **No person may be introduced as a partner without the consent of all the existing partners.**
7. Any matter or issue relating to the LLP shall be decided by a **resolution passed by a majority
   in number of the partners**, and for this purpose **each partner has one vote**. But **no change
   may be made in the nature of the business** of the LLP **without the consent of all partners**.
8. Every LLP shall ensure that **decisions taken by it are recorded in the minutes within thirty
   days** of taking the decision, and that the minutes are kept and maintained at the **registered
   office**.
9. Each partner shall **render true accounts and full information** of all things affecting the LLP
   to any partner or his legal representatives.
10. If a partner, **without the consent of the LLP**, carries on any **business of the same nature
    as and competing with** the LLP, he must **account for and pay over to the LLP all profits**
    made by him in that business.
11. Every partner shall **account to the LLP for any benefit derived by him without the consent of
    the LLP** from any transaction concerning the LLP, or from any use by him of the **property,
    name or business connection** of the LLP.
12. **No partner may be expelled by a majority** of the partners **unless a power to do so has been
    conferred by express agreement** between the partners.
13. All disputes between the partners arising out of the LLP agreement which cannot be resolved in
    terms of that agreement shall be **referred for arbitration** under the Arbitration and
    Conciliation Act 1996.

<div class="formula">First Schedule shortcuts worth memorising: EQUAL share of capital, profits and losses whatever the contribution (cl. 1) · NO remuneration (cl. 5) · UNANIMOUS consent to admit a partner (cl. 6) and to change the nature of the business (cl. 7) · MAJORITY IN NUMBER, one vote each, for everything else (cl. 7) · NO expulsion without an express power (cl. 12) · MINUTES within 30 days (cl. 8) · ARBITRATION for partner disputes (cl. 13).</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The most valuable trap in the chapter: <strong>the First Schedule gives no interest on
    contribution and no interest on advances</strong>. Students import the <strong>six per
    cent</strong> from s. 13(d) of the Partnership Act 1932 and lose the mark. Section 4 of the LLP
    Act switched the 1932 Act off; if the partners want interest they must write it into the LLP
    agreement. Two more: the default profit share is <strong>equal, not in proportion to
    contribution</strong>, and voting is by a <strong>majority in NUMBER of partners</strong>, not
    by value of contribution.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Vignette 1 — the LLP with a half-written agreement (moderate)">
  <p>
    Nirmala, Osman and Preetha register <strong>Trikona Design LLP</strong> on
    <strong>6 April 2026</strong>. Contributions are Nirmala ₹12,00,000, Osman ₹6,00,000 and
    Preetha ₹6,00,000. Their LLP agreement, filed in Form 3, deals only with the registered
    office, the business, and a clause that Nirmala shall be the managing partner. Nothing is said
    about profits, remuneration, admission of partners or voting. For the year ended
    <strong>31 March 2027</strong> the LLP earns a profit of <strong>₹21,60,000</strong>. Nirmala
    claims (i) profits in the ratio 2:1:1 because she put in half the money, (ii) a managing
    partner's remuneration of ₹3,00,000, and (iii) the right to admit her nephew as a fourth
    partner because she holds the largest contribution. Osman and Preetha object to all three.
    Advise.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 1 — which instrument governs each disputed matter?</div>
    Section 23(1) makes the LLP agreement the governing instrument, but s. 23(4) supplies the
    First Schedule <strong>for any matter on which the agreement is silent</strong>. Profits,
    remuneration, admission and voting are all silent, so all three claims are decided by the
    Schedule, not by the agreement and certainly not by the Partnership Act 1932, which s. 4 puts
    out of play.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 2 — the profit share (cl. 1)</div>
    Clause 1 says the partners share <strong>equally in the capital, profits and losses</strong>.
    Contribution is irrelevant to the default. Each partner therefore takes
    ₹21,60,000 ÷ 3 = <strong>₹7,20,000</strong>. Nirmala's 2:1:1 claim would have given her
    ₹10,80,000, which is the wrong option a question would build.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 3 — remuneration (cl. 5)</div>
    <strong>No partner is entitled to remuneration</strong> for acting in the business or
    management of the LLP. Being described as "managing partner" in the agreement confers the
    role, not the pay; the agreement would have had to fix a sum. Nirmala's ₹3,00,000 claim fails.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 4 — admitting the nephew (cl. 6)</div>
    <strong>No person may be introduced as a partner without the consent of all the existing
    partners.</strong> Osman and Preetha have each refused, so the admission cannot happen —
    and note that even a majority would not do, because clause 6 requires unanimity. Had the
    matter been an ordinary one, clause 7 would have applied and each partner would have had
    <strong>one vote</strong>, so Nirmala's larger contribution would still not have carried it.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Conclusion</div>
    All three claims fail. Nirmala receives <strong>₹7,20,000</strong> as her profit share and
    nothing else. If she wants a 2:1:1 split, remuneration and a contribution-weighted vote, the
    partners must <strong>amend the LLP agreement</strong> and file the change with the Registrar
    under s. 23(2) — which, on these facts, needs Osman's and Preetha's agreement.
  </div>
</Callout>

## 11. Cessation of partnership interest and changes in partners — sections 24 and 25

**Section 24 — cessation.**

- **24(1) — voluntary exit.** A person may cease to be a partner **in accordance with an agreement
  with the other partners** or, **in the absence of such an agreement**, by giving a **notice in
  writing of not less than thirty days** to the **other partners** of his intention to resign. Note
  that the notice goes to the other partners, not to the Registrar.
- **24(2) — automatic cessation.** A person **shall cease** to be a partner:
  - **(a)** on his **death** or on the **dissolution of the LLP**;
  - **(b)** if he is **declared to be of unsound mind** by a competent court; or
  - **(c)** if he has **applied to be adjudged an insolvent**, or has been **declared an
    insolvent**.
- **24(3) — the former partner is still a partner to the outside world.** Where a person has
  ceased to be a partner, the **former partner is to be regarded, in relation to any person dealing
  with the LLP, as still being a partner**, **unless** — (a) that person **has notice** that the
  former partner has ceased to be a partner, **or** (b) **notice** of the cessation **has been
  delivered to the Registrar**. Either limb is enough, and the second is the practical one: filing
  the notice protects the outgoing partner against the whole world at once.
- **24(4) — no clean break.** Cessation **does not by itself discharge** the partner from any
  obligation to the LLP, to the other partners, or to any other person **which he incurred while
  being a partner**.
- **24(5) — what he is paid.** Unless the LLP agreement provides otherwise, the former partner —
  or the person entitled to his share on his death or insolvency — is entitled to receive from the
  LLP:
  - **(a)** an amount **equal to the capital contribution actually made** by the former partner to
    the LLP; **and**
  - **(b)** his **right to share in the accumulated profits of the LLP, after deducting accumulated
    losses**, **determined as at the date he ceased to be a partner**.
- **24(6) — no continuing say.** A former partner, or a person entitled to his share on death or
  insolvency, **has no right to interfere in the management** of the LLP.

**Section 25 — registration of changes in partners.** Three separate clocks, and students mix them:

| Who must act | What | Time limit | Provision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **The partner** | Inform the **LLP** of a change in **his name or address** | **15 days** of the change | s. 25(1) |
| **The LLP** | File a notice with the **Registrar** where a person **becomes or ceases to be a partner** | **30 days** from the date he becomes or ceases | s. 25(2)(a) |
| **The LLP** | File a notice with the **Registrar** of a change in a partner's **name or address** | **30 days** of the change | s. 25(2)(b) |
| **A former partner** | May **himself file** the notice if he reasonably believes the LLP will not | Registrar then seeks the LLP's confirmation; if none within **15 days**, he registers the partner's notice | s. 25(6) |

Section 25(3) adds that the notice must be in the prescribed form and fee, be **signed by a
designated partner** and authenticated as prescribed, and, where it relates to an **incoming
partner**, contain a **statement of consent** by that partner, signed and authenticated by him.

**The penalties.** Contravention of s. 25(2) makes the **LLP and its every designated partner**
liable to a penalty of **₹10,000**; contravention of s. 25(1) makes **the partner** liable to a
penalty of **₹10,000**.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Section 24(3) is the LLP's version of the "public notice" idea, and it is the difference
    between an outgoing partner sleeping well and not. Until either the third party has actual
    notice <strong>or</strong> the notice reaches the <strong>Registrar</strong>, the former partner
    is treated as still a partner by anyone dealing with the LLP. That is why s. 25(6) lets him file
    the notice himself when he suspects the LLP will not — a provision with no counterpart in the
    Partnership Act 1932.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 12. Extent of liability of the LLP and of a partner — sections 26, 27 and 28

Everything in this part follows from one sentence, and it is worth writing out in full.

**Section 26 — partner as agent.** "Every partner of a limited liability partnership is, **for the
purpose of the business of the limited liability partnership, the agent of the limited liability
partnership, but not of other partners**."

Set that beside **s. 18 of the Partnership Act 1932** — a partner is the agent of the firm **for
the purpose of the business of the firm** — and the additional four words in the LLP Act, "but not
of other partners", are the whole of limited liability. Because a partner is not the agent of his
co-partners, nothing he does can create an obligation binding on them personally.

**Section 27 — extent of liability of the LLP.**

- **27(1) — when the LLP is NOT bound.** An LLP is **not bound** by anything done by a partner in
  dealing with a person if **both** of the following are true:
  - **(a)** the partner **in fact has no authority** to act for the LLP in doing that particular
    act; **and**
  - **(b)** the person **knows that he has no authority**, **or** does **not know or believe him
    to be a partner** of the LLP.
- **27(2) — wrongful acts.** The LLP **is liable** if a partner is liable to any person **as a
  result of a wrongful act or omission on his part in the course of the business of the LLP or
  with its authority**.
- **27(3) — the key words.** An obligation of the LLP, **whether arising in contract or
  otherwise**, shall be **solely the obligation of the LLP**.
- **27(4).** The liabilities of the LLP shall be **met out of the property of the LLP**.

Section 27(1) needs care. Both limbs must be satisfied before the LLP escapes. If the outsider
**did not know** of the want of authority **and** believed the man to be a partner, the LLP **is**
bound — the internal restriction in the LLP agreement is the partners' business, not the
outsider's. This is the LLP's version of the doctrine that protects a person dealing in good
faith with an apparent agent.

**Section 28 — extent of liability of a partner.**

- **28(1).** A partner is **not personally liable, directly or indirectly**, for an obligation
  referred to in s. 27(3) **solely by reason of being a partner** of the LLP.
- **28(2).** Sections 27(3) and 28(1) **shall not affect the personal liability of a partner for
  his own wrongful act or omission**, **but a partner shall not be personally liable for the
  wrongful act or omission of any other partner** of the LLP.

<div class="formula">The liability ladder: s. 26 (agent of the LLP only) → s. 27(3) (the obligation is SOLELY the LLP's) → s. 27(4) (met out of the LLP's property) → s. 28(1) (no personal liability merely by being a partner) → s. 28(2) (BUT a partner always answers for HIS OWN wrongful act, and never for another partner's) → s. 30 (fraud makes the liability of the LLP and of the guilty partners UNLIMITED).</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The single most common wrong answer in this chapter: "an LLP partner has no liability for
    professional negligence". <strong>Section 28(2) makes him personally liable for his own
    wrongful act or omission</strong> without limit. What the Act protects him from is
    <em>another</em> partner's wrong and the LLP's own contractual obligations. So in the Kochi
    architects' scenario, the architect who signed the drawing pays; the other three do not. That
    is the whole reform, and it is not the same thing as nobody paying.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 13. Holding out, fraud and whistle blowing — sections 29, 30 and 31

**Section 29 — holding out.**

- **29(1).** Any person who **by words spoken or written or by conduct represents himself, or
  knowingly permits himself to be represented, to be a partner** in an LLP is **liable to any
  person who has on the faith of any such representation given credit to the LLP**, **whether or
  not** the person representing himself or represented to be a partner **knows that the
  representation has reached the person giving credit**.
- **The proviso.** Where **credit is received by the LLP** as a result of such a representation,
  the **LLP shall also be liable, to the extent of the credit received by it or any financial
  benefit derived on it** — without prejudice to the liability of the person held out.
- **29(2).** Where, **after a partner's death**, the business is continued in the same LLP name,
  the **continued use of that name, or of the deceased partner's name as part of it, shall not of
  itself make his legal representative or his estate liable** for any act of the LLP done after his
  death.

Three limbs are needed for s. 29(1): a **representation** (words, writing or conduct, including
knowingly permitting it), **credit given to the LLP on the faith of it**, and the giving of credit
**by the person suing**. Notice what is not needed: the person held out need not know that his
representation reached the creditor, and there need be no intention to deceive.

**Section 30 — unlimited liability in case of fraud.**

- **30(1).** Where an act is carried out **by the LLP, or by any of its partners, with intent to
  defraud creditors** of the LLP or any other person, **or for any fraudulent purpose**, the
  liability of the **LLP** and of the **partners who acted with that intent** shall be
  **unlimited** for all or any of the debts or other liabilities of the LLP. The **proviso**
  qualifies it: where such an act is carried out **by a partner**, the **LLP is liable to the same
  extent as the partner unless the LLP establishes that the act was without its knowledge or
  authority**. The burden of that proof is on the LLP.
- **30(2).** Every person **knowingly a party** to carrying on the business with that intent or for
  that purpose is punishable with **imprisonment up to five years** and with a **fine of not less
  than ₹50,000 and up to ₹5,00,000**.
- **30(3).** Where an LLP, or any partner, designated partner or employee, has **conducted the
  affairs of the LLP in a fraudulent manner**, then without prejudice to any criminal proceedings,
  the LLP and that person **shall be liable to pay compensation** to any person who has suffered
  loss or damage by reason of that conduct. The **proviso** exonerates the LLP where the partner,
  designated partner or employee **acted fraudulently without the knowledge of the LLP**.

**Section 31 — whistle blowing.**

- **31(1).** The **Court or Tribunal may reduce or waive any penalty** leviable against a partner
  or employee of an LLP if it is satisfied that (a) he has **provided useful information during an
  investigation** of the LLP, or (b) **information given by him — whether or not during an
  investigation — leads to the LLP, or any partner or employee, being convicted** under this Act or
  any other Act.
- **31(2).** No partner or employee may be **discharged, demoted, suspended, threatened, harassed
  or in any other manner discriminated against** in the terms and conditions of his partnership or
  employment **merely because** he provided, or caused to be provided, such information.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Keep the three fault-based sections apart by asking <em>what the wrong was</em>.
    <strong>Section 28(2)</strong> — an ordinary wrongful act or omission: the partner alone is
    personally liable, and the LLP is liable too under s. 27(2) if it was in the course of business.
    <strong>Section 29</strong> — a representation of partnership: liability to a creditor who gave
    credit on the faith of it. <strong>Section 30</strong> — intent to defraud: liability becomes
    <em>unlimited</em>, and the LLP shares it unless it proves absence of knowledge or authority.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Vignette 2 — four claims against one LLP (hard)">
  <p>
    <strong>Vaidya Consulting LLP</strong> has four partners: Rukmini, Sanjay, Tarun and Uzma.
    The LLP agreement says that no partner may commit the LLP to a contract above ₹5,00,000
    without a written resolution. Four things happen in 2026.
  </p>
  <p>
    (a) Sanjay signs a ₹9,00,000 software licence with a vendor who has dealt with the LLP for
    years and knows nothing of the internal cap. (b) Tarun, doing a valuation for a client, misses
    an obvious defect in the title of a property; the client loses ₹28,00,000. (c) Rukmini and an
    outside associate raise ₹40,00,000 of supplier credit on invoices for services the LLP never
    intended to render, splitting the money; the other partners knew nothing. (d) Uzma's
    father-in-law, a retired banker, is described on the LLP's proposal documents as "our senior
    partner"; he never objected, and a lender advanced ₹15,00,000 on the strength of the name.
    Who is liable for what?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(a) — ss. 26 and 27(1): the software licence</div>
    Sanjay is the <strong>agent of the LLP</strong> (s. 26). He had no actual authority, so limb
    (a) of s. 27(1) is met. But limb (b) is <strong>not</strong>: the vendor neither knew of the
    want of authority nor doubted that Sanjay was a partner. <strong>Both</strong> limbs must be
    satisfied before the LLP escapes, so the <strong>LLP is bound</strong> and must pay
    ₹9,00,000. Sanjay's breach of the internal cap is a matter between him and his partners under
    the LLP agreement — the vendor is untouched by it. Rukmini, Tarun and Uzma have
    <strong>no personal liability</strong>: s. 27(3) makes the obligation solely the LLP's and
    s. 28(1) keeps it off them.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(b) — ss. 27(2) and 28: the negligent valuation</div>
    Two liabilities run in parallel. The <strong>LLP is liable</strong> under s. 27(2), because
    Tarun's wrongful omission was in the course of the LLP's business. <strong>Tarun is
    personally liable</strong> under s. 28(2) for his own wrongful act or omission — that
    liability is not capped by anything in the Act. <strong>Rukmini, Sanjay and Uzma are not
    liable at all</strong>: s. 28(2) says in terms that a partner shall not be personally liable
    for the wrongful act or omission of any other partner. This is the exact case the Act was
    written for.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(c) — s. 30: the fraudulent credit</div>
    Raising ₹40,00,000 on invoices for services never intended to be rendered is an act carried
    out <strong>with intent to defraud creditors</strong>. Under s. 30(1) the liability of
    <strong>Rukmini</strong>, as a partner who acted with that intent, is <strong>unlimited</strong>
    for all or any of the debts of the LLP. The proviso makes the <strong>LLP liable to the same
    extent</strong> <em>unless the LLP establishes</em> that the act was without its knowledge or
    authority — the burden is on the LLP, and on these facts it should discharge it. Rukmini also
    faces s. 30(2) punishment of imprisonment up to five years and a fine of ₹50,000 to
    ₹5,00,000, and s. 30(3) compensation to those who lost by the conduct. Sanjay, Tarun and Uzma,
    having acted with no such intent, keep the s. 28(1) protection.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(d) — s. 29: the "senior partner" on the proposal</div>
    The father-in-law was <strong>represented to be a partner</strong> and
    <strong>knowingly permitted</strong> the representation to stand. The lender <strong>gave
    credit on the faith of it</strong>. Section 29(1) therefore makes <strong>him personally
    liable</strong> to the lender for the ₹15,00,000, and it is irrelevant that he never knew the
    representation had reached this particular lender. The <strong>proviso</strong> adds that the
    LLP is liable too, to the extent of the credit it received or the financial benefit it derived.
    Uzma is not liable merely as his relative; if she authored the description, her own act is what
    would expose her.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">The pattern to carry into the exam</div>
    Four claims, four different answers, one question each time: <strong>whose act was it, and what
    kind of act?</strong> Contract by an agent → the LLP (ss. 26, 27). Own wrongful act → that
    partner plus the LLP (ss. 27(2), 28(2)). Fraud → unlimited, for the guilty (s. 30). A
    representation of partnership → the person held out (s. 29). Never start from who is richest.
  </div>
</Callout>

## 14. Contribution — sections 32 and 33

An LLP has no share capital. What the partners put in is called **contribution**, and the Act
takes a deliberately wide view of what may count.

**Section 32 — form of contribution.**

- **32(1).** A contribution of a partner **may consist of tangible, movable or immovable or
  intangible property or other benefit to the LLP**, **including money, promissory notes, other
  agreements to contribute cash or property, and contracts for services performed or to be
  performed**.
- **32(2).** The **monetary value of the contribution of each partner shall be accounted for and
  disclosed in the accounts** of the LLP in the prescribed manner.

Read the words "contracts for services performed or to be performed". A partner may contribute
**his own future work**, valued in money, and that is a real contribution recorded in the
accounts. No company can accept services as consideration for shares in the same way, and this is
one of the genuine advantages of the form for professional practices.

**Section 33 — obligation to contribute.**

- **33(1).** The obligation of a partner to contribute money, other property, other benefit or to
  perform services **shall be as per the LLP agreement**.
- **33(2).** A **creditor of the LLP** which extends credit, **or otherwise acts in reliance**, on
  an obligation described in that agreement, **without notice of any compromise between partners**,
  **may enforce the original obligation** against that partner.

Section 33(2) is the anti-avoidance rule and it is heavily examined. Once a creditor has lent on
the faith of a stated contribution obligation, the partners cannot quietly reduce or waive that
obligation among themselves and defeat him. He enforces the **original** obligation, unless he had
**notice** of the compromise before he acted.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Contribution is not share capital, and three differences follow. There is <strong>no statutory
    minimum</strong> contribution for an LLP. There are <strong>no capital-maintenance rules</strong>
    of the kind the Companies Act imposes. And the contribution figure matters mainly as a
    <strong>threshold</strong> — ₹25,00,000 for the small-LLP definition in s. 2(1)(ta) and for the
    audit exemption under the rules. Do not write that a partner's liability is "limited to his
    contribution": s. 28(1) protects him from the LLP's obligations altogether, and his real
    exposure is the s. 33 obligation he has actually undertaken.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 15. Accounts, audit, the Statement of Account and Solvency and the annual return — sections 34 to 37

This is the price of the corporate shell, and it is the part of the chapter richest in dates.

**Section 34 — books, the Statement of Account and Solvency, and audit.**

- **34(1).** The LLP shall maintain **proper books of account** as prescribed, relating to its
  affairs **for each year of its existence**, on a **cash basis or accrual basis** and according to
  the **double entry system**, and shall maintain them at its **registered office** for the
  prescribed period.
- **34(2).** Every LLP shall, **within six months from the end of each financial year**, **prepare
  a Statement of Account and Solvency** for that financial year **as at the last day of that
  year**, in the prescribed form, **signed by the designated partners**.
- **34(3).** Every LLP shall **file** the Statement of Account and Solvency with the **Registrar**
  every year, within the prescribed time and in the prescribed form and fee. The rules fix that
  filing at **thirty days from the end of six months of the financial year** — in **Form 8**.
- **34(4).** The accounts of an LLP **shall be audited** in accordance with the prescribed rules;
  the Central Government may by notification **exempt any class of LLPs** from this requirement.
- **34A** (inserted in 2021) empowers the Central Government, **in consultation with the National
  Financial Reporting Authority**, to prescribe **accounting standards and auditing standards** for
  a class or classes of LLP.

**The audit threshold.** Rule 24 of the LLP Rules 2009 exempts an LLP from audit where, in a
financial year, its **turnover does not exceed ₹40,00,000** and its **contribution does not exceed
₹25,00,000**. Put the other way round, and this is the way to apply it:

<div class="formula">Audit is REQUIRED if turnover exceeds ₹40,00,000 OR contribution exceeds ₹25,00,000. Either limb alone triggers it. An LLP may nevertheless opt into audit voluntarily, and if it does, the rules require the decision to be taken before the end of the financial year.</div>

**Section 35 — annual return.** Every LLP shall file an **annual return**, duly authenticated,
with the Registrar **within sixty days of the closure of its financial year**, in the prescribed
form and fee — **Form 11**. For an LLP on the standard 1 April to 31 March year, that is **30 May**.

**Section 36 — inspection.** The **incorporation document**, the **names of partners and changes
made in them**, the **Statement of Account and Solvency** and the **annual return** filed by each
LLP **shall be available for inspection by any person** in the prescribed manner and on payment of
the prescribed fee. This is the "publicity" cost listed in §5.

**Section 37 — penalty for false statement.** If in any return, statement or other document
required by or for the purposes of the Act, a person **makes a statement which is false in any
material particular knowing it to be false**, or **omits any material fact knowing it to be
material**, he is punishable with **imprisonment up to two years** and with a **fine of not less
than ₹1,00,000 and up to ₹5,00,000**.

**Section 38** empowers the **Registrar to obtain information** from any present or former partner
or designated partner, and to summon him.

**Section 69 — additional fee for late filing.** Any document or return required to be filed with
the Registrar which is not filed within the time specified may be filed afterwards **on payment of
an additional fee of ₹100 for every day of delay**, in addition to the ordinary fee. The additional
fee runs per day and, unlike the s. 13(4) penalty, has no cap of its own in the section.

**The compliance calendar for a 31 March year-end**, which is what a numerical will be built on:

| Obligation | Provision | Trigger | Due |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Prepare the Statement of Account and Solvency | s. 34(2) | 6 months from the end of the financial year | **30 September** |
| File the SAS in Form 8 | s. 34(3) with the rules | 30 days from the end of those 6 months | **30 October** |
| File the annual return in Form 11 | s. 35 | 60 days from the closure of the financial year | **30 May** |
| File the LLP agreement or any change, Form 3 | s. 23(2) with the rules | 30 days from incorporation or from the change | — |
| File a change in partners, Form 4 | s. 25(2) | 30 days from the change | — |
| File a change of registered office | s. 13(3) with the rules | 30 days | — |
| Late filing of any of the above | s. 69 | Each day of delay | **₹100 per day** |

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Students collapse the two annual filings into one date. They are different obligations on
    different clocks. The <strong>annual return</strong> (s. 35, Form 11) runs
    <strong>60 days from the CLOSURE of the financial year</strong> — 30 May. The
    <strong>Statement of Account and Solvency</strong> is <em>prepared</em> within
    <strong>six months of the end</strong> of the year (s. 34(2)) — 30 September — and
    <em>filed</em> within a further <strong>thirty days</strong> — 30 October. An option that
    gives the SAS a 60-day deadline, or the annual return a six-month one, is testing exactly this
    confusion.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Vignette 3 — one year of compliance, priced (moderate)">
  <p>
    <strong>Suvarna Analytics LLP</strong> closes its first full financial year on
    <strong>31 March 2027</strong>. Turnover for the year is <strong>₹37,50,000</strong> and the
    total contribution of the partners is <strong>₹28,00,000</strong>. The designated partners
    prepare the Statement of Account and Solvency on 12 September 2027 but file Form 8 only on
    <strong>14 November 2027</strong>. The annual return in Form 11 is filed on
    <strong>26 June 2027</strong>. A partner, Farida, changed her residential address on
    <strong>4 May 2027</strong> and told the LLP on <strong>2 June 2027</strong>. Work out the
    LLP's position.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 1 — is an audit required?</div>
    Turnover ₹37,50,000 does <strong>not</strong> exceed ₹40,00,000, but contribution
    ₹28,00,000 <strong>does</strong> exceed ₹25,00,000. Either limb alone triggers the
    requirement, so the accounts <strong>must be audited</strong>. The same arithmetic also takes
    the LLP outside the <strong>small LLP</strong> definition in s. 2(1)(ta), which needs both
    limbs to be satisfied — so it does not get the halved penalties under s. 76A either.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 2 — the Statement of Account and Solvency</div>
    Preparation under s. 34(2) was due within six months of 31 March 2027, that is by
    <strong>30 September 2027</strong>; done on 12 September, so that limb is met. Filing was due
    thirty days later, by <strong>30 October 2027</strong>. It went in on 14 November 2027, so the
    delay is <strong>15 days</strong>, and the s. 69 additional fee is
    15 × ₹100 = <strong>₹1,500</strong> on top of the ordinary filing fee.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 3 — the annual return</div>
    Section 35 gives sixty days from the closure of the financial year: 60 days from
    31 March 2027 is <strong>30 May 2027</strong>. Filed on 26 June 2027, so the delay is
    <strong>27 days</strong> and the additional fee is 27 × ₹100 = <strong>₹2,700</strong>.
    Note that the two clocks are independent — being late on one does not extend the other.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 4 — Farida's change of address</div>
    Section 25(1) required <strong>Farida</strong> to inform the LLP within <strong>15 days</strong>
    of 4 May 2027, that is by <strong>19 May 2027</strong>. She told it on 2 June, so she has
    contravened s. 25(1) and is liable to a penalty of <strong>₹10,000</strong>. The LLP's own
    clock under s. 25(2)(b) is <strong>30 days from the change</strong> — by 3 June 2027 — and it
    was told only on 2 June, which leaves it a single day. If it misses that, the LLP and every
    designated partner are each liable to a penalty of ₹10,000 under s. 25(4).
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Conclusion</div>
    Audit required; additional fees of <strong>₹1,500 + ₹2,700 = ₹4,200</strong>; a ₹10,000
    penalty on Farida; and a live risk of a further ₹10,000 each on the LLP and its designated
    partners. None of this touches the partners' limited liability under s. 28(1) — statutory
    penalties are a separate track from the LLP's commercial obligations.
  </div>
</Callout>

## 16. A partner's transferable interest — section 42

**Section 42** splits what a partner owns into two halves and makes only one of them transferable.

- **42(1).** The **rights of a partner to a share of the profits and losses** of the LLP and **to
  receive distributions** in accordance with the LLP agreement **are transferable**, either
  **wholly or in part**.
- **42(2).** The transfer **does not by itself cause the disassociation of the partner** or a
  **dissolution and winding up** of the LLP. The transferor remains a partner.
- **42(3).** The transfer **does not, by itself, entitle the transferee or assignee** to
  **participate in the management or conduct of the activities of the LLP**, or to **access
  information concerning the transactions of the LLP**.

So the **economic interest** travels and the **management interest** does not. The assignee gets
money; the assignor keeps the vote, the seat and the liabilities. If the assignee is to become a
partner, that is a separate step under s. 22 read with the LLP agreement — and under the First
Schedule default it needs the **consent of all** the existing partners.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Section 42 is the LLP analogue of s. 29 of the Partnership Act 1932, and the two say almost the
    same thing: a transferee of a share takes the profits and nothing else. Two differences worth a
    line in an answer. First, s. 42(2) says expressly that the transfer causes
    <strong>no disassociation and no dissolution</strong>. Second, an LLP has
    <strong>perpetual succession</strong> under s. 3(2), so a transfer, a death or a retirement
    leaves the entity untouched, whereas in a firm those events go to the firm's very existence.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 17. Conversion into an LLP — sections 55 to 58 and the Second, Third and Fourth Schedules

The Act provides three doors in, and one section governing what happens once you are through.

| Converting entity | Enabling section | Schedule that sets the conditions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A **firm** (partnership under the 1932 Act) | **s. 55** | **Second Schedule** |
| A **private company** | **s. 56** | **Third Schedule** |
| An **unlisted public company** | **s. 57** | **Fourth Schedule** |

Note the two exclusions the examiner likes: a **listed public company cannot convert**, and there
is no provision for converting a **sole proprietorship**, an **LLP into a company** under this
Act, or a **co-operative society**.

**The conditions, in outline.**

- **Second Schedule — firm to LLP.** The application is made by **all the partners of the firm**,
  and the **partners of the LLP must comprise all the partners of the firm and no one else**. So a
  partner who does not want to come across must retire from the firm first; and no outsider may be
  slipped in as part of the conversion.
- **Third and Fourth Schedules — company to LLP.** Two conditions, both of which must be
  satisfied: **there is no security interest in the assets of the company subsisting or in force**
  at the time of the application, and the **partners of the LLP comprise all the shareholders of
  the company and no one else**. A company with a live charge on its assets must have it satisfied
  or released before it can convert.

**Section 58 — registration and effect of conversion.**

- **58(1).** On being satisfied that the Schedule has been complied with, the Registrar
  **registers the documents and issues a certificate of registration** stating that the LLP is,
  **on and from the date specified in the certificate**, registered under this Act.
- **58(2).** The LLP shall, **within fifteen days of the date of registration**, **inform the
  concerned Registrar of Firms or Registrar of Companies** about the conversion and the particulars
  of the LLP, in the prescribed form and manner.
- **58(3).** On and from the date of registration specified in the certificate:
  - **(a)** there is an **LLP by the name specified** in the certificate;
  - **(b)** **all tangible and intangible property, assets, interests, rights, privileges,
    liabilities and obligations** relating to the firm or company, **and the whole of its
    undertaking, are transferred to and vest in the LLP without further assurance, act or deed**;
    and
  - **(c)** the **firm or company is deemed to be dissolved** and **removed from the records** of
    the Registrar of Firms or the Registrar of Companies.
- **58(4)** carries over the machinery: **pending proceedings** may be continued, completed and
  enforced by or against the LLP; every **contract of employment** continues; existing
  **agreements, deeds, contracts, schemes, bonds and instruments** stay in force as if the LLP were
  a party or as if they had been made in its favour.
- **58(5).** Every **approval, permit or licence** issued under any other Act is, **subject to the
  provisions of that other Act**, transferred to the LLP. So the general vesting in s. 58(3)(b)
  does not override a sectoral statute that says a licence is personal.

**The statement on correspondence.** Paragraph 17 of the Second Schedule requires a converted LLP
to ensure that, for **twelve months commencing not later than fourteen days after the date of
registration**, **every official correspondence** of the LLP bears a statement that it was
converted from a firm into an LLP on that date, together with the **name and registration number
of the firm** from which it was converted. Failure attracts a fine of **not less than ₹10,000 and
up to ₹1,00,000**, with a further fine of **not less than ₹50 and up to ₹500 for every day** the
default continues. The Third and Fourth Schedules carry the same requirement for a converting
company.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Two things conversion does <strong>not</strong> do. It does <strong>not</strong> wipe out past
    liability: the partners of the converted firm remain liable for obligations incurred
    <em>before</em> conversion, and the Second Schedule preserves that expressly, so the LLP shell
    is prospective only. And it does <strong>not</strong> carry a licence across in defiance of the
    statute that granted it — s. 58(5) makes that transfer <em>subject to the provisions of the
    other Act</em>. A third slip: the "no security interest" condition applies to a
    <strong>company</strong> converting under the Third or Fourth Schedule, not to a firm under the
    Second.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Vignette 4 — a firm converts, and finds out what it bought (moderate-hard)">
  <p>
    <strong>Halewood Traders</strong> is a registered firm with four partners: Girish, Hema, Imran
    and Jaspal. Jaspal wants out. The firm has a running cash-credit facility secured on its stock,
    a five-year godown lease, an FSSAI licence, and a pending suit brought against it in 2024 by a
    supplier for ₹11,00,000. The partners apply to convert into
    <strong>Halewood Traders LLP</strong> and the Registrar issues a certificate of registration
    dated <strong>20 March 2026</strong>. Advise on (i) Jaspal, (ii) the security, (iii) the lease,
    licence and suit, (iv) the ₹11,00,000 claim, and (v) what the LLP must do next.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(i) Jaspal and the composition condition</div>
    The Second Schedule requires the application to be made by <strong>all the partners of the
    firm</strong> and requires the <strong>partners of the LLP to comprise all the partners of the
    firm and no one else</strong>. Jaspal cannot simply be left out of the LLP while the
    conversion goes through. He must <strong>retire from the firm first</strong>, after which the
    remaining three convert; alternatively he comes across as a partner and resigns afterwards
    under s. 24(1) on thirty days' notice.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(ii) the cash-credit security</div>
    The "no security interest subsisting" condition belongs to the <strong>Third and Fourth
    Schedules</strong>, which govern a converting <strong>company</strong>. Halewood is a
    <strong>firm</strong> converting under the <strong>Second Schedule</strong>, so a live charge on
    its stock is not a bar. The charge itself survives, because s. 58(3)(b) vests the assets in the
    LLP together with the liabilities and obligations relating to them.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(iii) the lease, the licence and the pending suit</div>
    The <strong>lease</strong> vests in the LLP under s. 58(3)(b) without any further assurance,
    act or deed — no fresh deed of assignment is needed. The <strong>pending suit</strong> may be
    continued against the LLP under s. 58(4). The <strong>FSSAI licence</strong> is different:
    s. 58(5) transfers approvals, permits and licences <strong>subject to the provisions of the
    other Act</strong>, so the LLP must do whatever the food-safety statute requires for a change
    of the licence-holder. Treating it as automatic is the standard error.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(iv) the ₹11,00,000 supplier claim</div>
    The obligation was incurred <strong>before</strong> conversion. It vests in the LLP under
    s. 58(3)(b), and the LLP must meet it out of its property (s. 27(4)). But conversion is
    <strong>prospective protection only</strong>: the Second Schedule preserves the
    <strong>personal liability of the partners of the erstwhile firm for obligations incurred
    before conversion</strong>, so Girish, Hema, Imran and Jaspal remain personally exposed on this
    2024 claim. Their s. 28(1) protection covers what the LLP does from 20 March 2026 onwards.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(v) what the LLP must do next</div>
    Two deadlines run from <strong>20 March 2026</strong>. Within <strong>fifteen days</strong>,
    that is by <strong>4 April 2026</strong>, the LLP must inform the <strong>Registrar of
    Firms</strong> of the conversion under s. 58(2). And under paragraph 17 of the Second Schedule
    every official correspondence must carry the conversion statement and the firm's former name
    and registration number, beginning <strong>not later than fourteen days after</strong>
    registration — by <strong>3 April 2026</strong> — and continuing for
    <strong>twelve months</strong>, that is until <strong>19 March 2027</strong>.
  </div>
</Callout>

## 18. Winding up and dissolution, foreign LLPs and compounding — sections 63 to 65, 59 and 39

**Section 63 — the two routes.** The winding up of an LLP may be **either voluntary or by the
Tribunal**, and an LLP so wound up may be **dissolved**.

**Section 64 — when the Tribunal may wind up an LLP.** Five circumstances:

- **(a)** if the **LLP decides** that it be wound up by the Tribunal;
- **(b)** if, for a period of **more than six months**, the **number of partners is reduced below
  two**;
- **(c)** if the LLP has acted **against the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India,
  the security of the State or public order**;
- **(d)** if the LLP has **made a default in filing** with the Registrar the **Statement of Account
  and Solvency or the annual return for any five consecutive financial years**;
- **(e)** if the **Tribunal is of the opinion that it is just and equitable** that the LLP be wound
  up.

The ground of **inability to pay debts** was **omitted** from s. 64 by the LLP (Amendment) Act
2021, because insolvency of an LLP is dealt with under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code 2016.
An option offering "unable to pay its debts" as a s. 64 ground is testing that amendment.

**Section 65 — rules.** The Central Government may make rules for the provisions in relation to
the **winding up and dissolution** of LLPs; the detailed machinery — liquidators, the statement of
solvency in a voluntary winding up, the order of application of assets — sits in those rules
rather than in the Act, and the Foundation syllabus treats it at outline depth only.

**Section 75 — striking off.** Where the Registrar has reasonable cause to believe that an LLP is
**not carrying on any business or operation**, its name may be **struck off** the register in the
prescribed manner. The rules use a period of **two years or more** of inactivity, and an LLP may
also apply to be struck off on that footing.

**Section 59 — foreign LLPs.** The Central Government may make rules for the **establishment of a
place of business by foreign LLPs within India** and for carrying on their business there, by
applying or incorporating, with appropriate modifications, the provisions of the **Companies Act
2013**, or such regulatory mechanism as may be prescribed. Read with s. 2(1)(m), a **foreign LLP**
is one **formed, incorporated or registered outside India which establishes a place of business
within India**; the rules require it to file particulars with the Registrar within **thirty days**
of establishing that place of business.

**Section 39 — compounding of offences.** An offence under the Act which is **punishable with fine
only** may be **compounded** by the **Regional Director** (or an officer not below that rank
authorised by the Central Government), by collecting from the person reasonably suspected of
having committed it a sum **not lower than the minimum fine and not exceeding the maximum fine**
provided for that offence. Two limits are examinable: compounding is **not available for an
offence punishable with imprisonment**, whether alone or with fine; and a **like offence cannot be
compounded again** within the period the section prescribes after an earlier compounding.

**Section 74 — general penalty.** Any person guilty of an offence under the Act for which **no
punishment is expressly provided** is liable to a **fine of not less than ₹5,000 and up to
₹5,00,000**, with a further fine **up to ₹50 for every day** after the first during which the
default continues.

**Section 76A — half penalties for a small LLP.** Where a penalty is payable for non-compliance by
a **small LLP** or a **start-up LLP**, or by its partner or designated partner, the penalty is
**one-half** of the penalty specified in the provision, subject to a maximum of **₹1,00,000** for
the LLP and **₹50,000** for every partner or designated partner or other person.

  <p>
    Three numbers from this section are the ones options are built from: <strong>six
    months</strong> below two partners (s. 64(b)) — and notice this is the same six months that
    triggers the sole partner's personal liability under s. 6(2), so one fact produces two
    consequences; <strong>five consecutive financial years</strong> of default in filing the SAS or
    the annual return (s. 64(d)); and <strong>two years</strong> of inactivity before the Registrar
    strikes an LLP off (s. 75 with the rules).
  </p>

<Callout kind="example" label="Vignette 5 — an LLP down to one partner (hard)">
  <p>
    <strong>Kesari Logistics LLP</strong> has two partners, Lalita and Manohar, both designated
    partners, sharing under the First Schedule defaults. Manohar dies on <strong>9 May
    2026</strong>. Lalita continues the business alone, knowing the position, and does not admit
    anybody. Between <strong>10 November 2026 and 31 March 2027</strong> the LLP incurs fresh
    trade debts of <strong>₹26,00,000</strong>; debts of ₹9,00,000 incurred between May and
    October 2026 also remain unpaid. Manohar's contribution actually paid in was
    <strong>₹8,00,000</strong>; accumulated profits at 9 May 2026 were ₹15,00,000 and accumulated
    losses ₹3,00,000. Advise on (i) the LLP's existence, (ii) Lalita's exposure, (iii) what
    Manohar's estate may claim, and (iv) the LLP's filings.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(i) does the LLP still exist?</div>
    Yes. An LLP is a body corporate with <strong>perpetual succession</strong> (s. 3(2)) and
    <strong>any change in the partners does not affect its existence, rights or liabilities</strong>
    (s. 3(3)). Manohar's death is a cessation under s. 24(2)(a), not a dissolution. The number
    being below two is a <strong>ground on which the Tribunal may wind the LLP up</strong> after
    six months (s. 64(b)) — a discretion someone must invoke, not an automatic end.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(ii) Lalita's personal exposure under s. 6(2)</div>
    Section 6(2) needs four things: the number reduced below two, the LLP carrying on business
    <strong>for more than six months</strong> while it is so reduced, Lalita being the
    <strong>only partner</strong> during that time, and her <strong>knowledge</strong> of it. Six
    months from 9 May 2026 runs to <strong>9 November 2026</strong>. So Lalita is personally
    liable for the obligations incurred <strong>after</strong> that date — the
    <strong>₹26,00,000</strong> incurred from 10 November 2026 onwards. The <strong>₹9,00,000</strong>
    incurred inside the first six months stays where s. 27(3) puts it: solely the obligation of the
    LLP. Note that her liability is <strong>additional</strong> — the LLP remains liable for the
    whole ₹35,00,000.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(iii) Manohar's estate under s. 24(5)</div>
    Unless the LLP agreement provides otherwise, the person entitled to the share of a deceased
    partner receives (a) <strong>the capital contribution actually made</strong> — ₹8,00,000 —
    and (b) <strong>his share in the accumulated profits after deducting accumulated losses,
    determined as at the date of cessation</strong>. Net accumulated profits are
    ₹15,00,000 − ₹3,00,000 = ₹12,00,000, shared <strong>equally</strong> between two partners under
    First Schedule clause 1, giving Manohar ₹6,00,000. The estate therefore claims
    ₹8,00,000 + ₹6,00,000 = <strong>₹14,00,000</strong>. By s. 24(6) the estate has
    <strong>no right to interfere in the management</strong>, so it cannot direct Lalita to admit a
    partner or to stop trading.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(iv) the filings</div>
    Section 25(2)(a) required the LLP to file notice of Manohar's cessation with the Registrar
    <strong>within thirty days</strong> of 9 May 2026, that is by <strong>8 June 2026</strong>;
    default costs ₹10,000 on the LLP and on every designated partner. Separately, the LLP is now
    below the s. 7(1) minimum of <strong>two designated partners</strong>: the s. 9 proviso deems
    every partner to be a designated partner, which on these facts still leaves one, so the s. 10(1)
    penalty of ₹10,000 plus ₹100 for each further day is running against the LLP and against
    Lalita, capped at ₹1,00,000 and ₹50,000 respectively.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">The lesson</div>
    A single fact — one partner dies — sets four different clocks running: six months to personal
    liability (s. 6(2)) and to the Tribunal's winding-up ground (s. 64(b)), thirty days to file the
    cessation (s. 25(2)(a)), thirty days to appoint a designated partner (s. 9), and an immediate
    s. 24(5) entitlement in the estate. Answer these questions by listing the clocks, not by
    arguing about fairness.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

- **"An LLP is a partnership with limited liability."** It is a **body corporate** (s. 3) and the
  **Partnership Act 1932 does not apply to it** (s. 4). Start there.
- **Importing the Partnership Act defaults.** No six per cent interest on advances, no s. 30 minor,
  no s. 69 registration bar, no implied authority under s. 19. The **First Schedule** is the only
  default set, and it says nothing about interest.
- **"Profits are shared in proportion to contribution."** First Schedule clause 1 shares capital,
  profits and losses **equally**, whatever each partner put in.
- **"A partner may draw a salary."** Clause 5 gives **no remuneration** unless the LLP agreement
  provides for it. Calling someone "managing partner" does not pay him.
- **"A majority of contribution carries a decision."** Clause 7 is a **majority in number**, one
  vote each — and admitting a partner (cl. 6) and changing the nature of the business (cl. 7
  proviso) need **unanimity**.
- **Confusing a partner with a designated partner.** A **body corporate may be a partner** (s. 5)
  but a **designated partner must be an individual** (s. 7(1)), and a body corporate partner
  supplies a **nominee**.
- **Using 182 days for residence.** The 2021 amendment made it **120 days** in the financial year.
- **"An LLP must have a common seal."** Section 14(c) says **if it decides to have one**.
- **"An LLP has a maximum of 50 partners."** There is **no maximum** for an LLP; 50 is the general
  partnership ceiling under s. 464 of the Companies Act 2013 with the rules.
- **"The LLP escapes because the partner had no authority."** Section 27(1) needs **both** limbs:
  no actual authority **and** the outsider knowing it, or not believing him to be a partner.
- **"A partner is never personally liable."** Section 28(2) makes him liable for **his own**
  wrongful act or omission; s. 30 makes it **unlimited** for fraud; s. 6(2) catches a sole
  partner after six months; s. 29 catches holding out; s. 8(b) puts the LLP's penalties on a
  designated partner.
- **"A partner is liable for his co-partner's negligence."** He is not — s. 28(2) says so in
  terms. Reversing this pair is the most expensive error in the chapter.
- **Forgetting the s. 30 proviso.** The LLP shares the guilty partner's **unlimited** liability
  **unless the LLP proves** the act was without its knowledge or authority. The burden is on the
  LLP.
- **Mixing up the annual filings.** Annual return — **60 days from the closure** of the financial
  year (s. 35). Statement of Account and Solvency — **prepared within six months** of the end
  (s. 34(2)), **filed thirty days later** under the rules.
- **Reading the audit test as cumulative.** Audit is required if turnover exceeds **₹40,00,000**
  **or** contribution exceeds **₹25,00,000**. The **small LLP** definition, by contrast, needs
  **both** limbs to be satisfied.
- **Forgetting the cap on the s. 13(4) penalty.** ₹500 a day, but **no more than ₹50,000** for the
  LLP and for every partner.
- **Thinking a change of registered office is effective when resolved.** Section 13(3) — it takes
  effect **only upon filing**.
- **"A cessation notice must go to the Registrar by the partner."** Section 24(1) requires
  **thirty days' notice in writing to the other partners**; the Registrar filing is the LLP's job
  under s. 25(2)(a), with s. 25(6) as the former partner's fallback.
- **Ignoring s. 24(3).** A former partner is still treated as a partner by anyone dealing with the
  LLP until that person has **notice**, or **notice reaches the Registrar**.
- **Treating an assignee under s. 42 as a partner.** He gets **profits and distributions only** —
  no management, no information, and no partnership without a fresh admission.
- **"A listed company can convert into an LLP."** Only a **firm** (s. 55), a **private company**
  (s. 56) and an **unlisted public company** (s. 57) may convert.
- **Applying the "no security interest" bar to a converting firm.** It is a **Third and Fourth
  Schedule** condition, that is, a **company** condition.
- **"Conversion wipes the slate clean."** Pre-conversion obligations remain, and the partners of
  the erstwhile firm remain personally liable for them.
- **"Inability to pay debts is a ground for winding up under s. 64."** That ground was **omitted**
  in 2021; insolvency goes to the IBC.
- **Compounding an imprisonment offence.** Section 39 reaches only offences **punishable with fine
  only**.

## One-page revision summary

**The frame.** LLP Act 2008 (Act 6 of 2009), assent 7 January 2009, main provisions in force
31 March 2009, amended by the LLP (Amendment) Act 2021. **s. 3** — body corporate, separate legal
entity, perpetual succession, a change of partners does not affect it. **s. 4** — the Partnership
Act 1932 does **not** apply.

**Definitions (s. 2(1)).** LLP = a partnership formed and **registered** under the Act, (n).
LLP agreement = a **written** agreement fixing mutual rights and duties, (o). Partner = a person
who becomes one in accordance with the LLP agreement, (q). Designated partner = one designated
under s. 7, (j). Business includes **every trade, profession, service and occupation**, (d). Body
corporate includes an LLP and a foreign LLP but **not** a corporation sole or a co-operative
society, (e). Financial year 1 April to 31 March, with the **post-30 September** extension, (l).
**Small LLP** = contribution up to **₹25,00,000** **and** turnover up to **₹40,00,000**, (ta).

**Incorporation.** **s. 11** — two or more persons for a **lawful business with a view to
profit** subscribe the incorporation document; filed with the Registrar of the State of the
registered office; with a **statement** by an advocate / CS / CA / CMA engaged in the formation
**and** a subscriber. **s. 12** — the Registrar registers and certifies **within fourteen days**;
the certificate is **conclusive evidence**. **s. 14** — sue and be sued, own property, common seal
**if it decides to have one**. In practice: DSC → **RUN-LLP** or **FiLLiP** → certificate with
LLPIN → **Form 3** (agreement, 30 days) → **Form 4** (partners).

**Name and office.** **s. 15** — the name must end in "limited liability partnership" or "LLP";
not undesirable, not identical or too nearly resembling. **s. 16** — reservation for **three
months**. **s. 17** — Central Government direction to change, complied with in **three months**,
else a new name is allotted. **s. 19** — voluntary change. **s. 21** — invoices, correspondence and
publications carry the name, registered-office address, registration number and a statement of
**limited liability**. **s. 13** — registered office; change **effective only on filing**; penalty
**₹500 a day, maximum ₹50,000** for the LLP and every partner.

**Partners.** **s. 5** — any **individual or body corporate**; an individual is disqualified if of
**unsound mind** (finding in force), an **undischarged insolvent**, or an **applicant for
adjudication pending**. No minor. **s. 6(1)** — minimum **two**, no maximum. **s. 6(2)** — below
two for **more than six months**, and the **knowing sole partner** is **personally liable for
obligations incurred in that period**. **s. 22** — the subscribers are partners on incorporation;
others join under the LLP agreement.

**Designated partners.** **s. 7(1)** — at least **two individuals**, at least one **resident in
India** (**120 days** in the financial year); where partners are bodies corporate, **two
nominees**. **s. 7(3)** prior **consent**; **s. 7(4)** file particulars in **30 days**; **s. 7(6)**
**DPIN**, with ss. 153 to 159 of the Companies Act 2013 applying. **s. 8** — responsible for all
compliance acts **and liable to all penalties imposed on the LLP**. **s. 9** — fill a vacancy in
**30 days**; if none or only one, **every partner is deemed** a designated partner. **s. 10** —
penalty **₹10,000** (s. 7(1)) or **₹5,000** (ss. 7(4), 7(5), 9), plus **₹100 a day**, capped at
₹1,00,000 / ₹50,000 and ₹50,000 / ₹25,000.

**The agreement.** **s. 23** — the LLP agreement governs; filed with the Registrar with every
change; a **pre-incorporation** agreement binds the LLP only if **ratified by all the partners
after incorporation**; the **First Schedule** applies **to any matter on which the agreement is
silent**. First Schedule: **equal** share of capital, profits and losses; LLP indemnifies a partner
for the ordinary conduct and for preservation; **partner indemnifies the LLP for his fraud**; every
partner may **manage**; **no remuneration**; **unanimous** consent to admit; **majority in number,
one vote each**, but **unanimous** to change the nature of the business; **minutes within 30
days**; **true accounts and full information**; **account for competing profits**; **account for
benefits** from the LLP's property, name or connection; **no expulsion** without an express power;
**arbitration** under the 1996 Act.

**Cessation and changes.** **s. 24(1)** — by agreement or **30 days' written notice to the other
partners**. **s. 24(2)** — death, dissolution, unsoundness of mind, insolvency. **s. 24(3)** —
still a partner to anyone dealing with the LLP until that person has **notice** or notice reaches
the **Registrar**. **s. 24(4)** — cessation does not discharge past obligations. **s. 24(5)** —
entitlement = **contribution actually made** + **share of accumulated profits net of accumulated
losses at the date of cessation**. **s. 24(6)** — no interference in management. **s. 25** —
partner tells the LLP of a name or address change in **15 days**; the LLP tells the Registrar of an
admission, cessation or change in **30 days**; a former partner may file himself, with a **15-day**
confirmation window; penalty **₹10,000** on either side.

**Liability.** **s. 26** — every partner is the agent of the **LLP, but not of other partners**.
**s. 27(1)** — the LLP is not bound only where the partner had **no authority** **and** the
outsider **knew** it or did not believe him to be a partner. **s. 27(2)** — the LLP is liable for a
partner's wrongful act or omission in the course of business or with its authority. **s. 27(3)** —
an obligation of the LLP is **solely the LLP's**; **27(4)** met out of the LLP's property.
**s. 28(1)** — no personal liability **solely by reason of being a partner**; **s. 28(2)** — but
personal liability for **his own** wrongful act, and **never** for another partner's. **s. 29** —
**holding out**: a person represented as a partner is liable to a creditor who gave credit on the
faith of it, and the LLP is liable to the extent of the credit received; the estate of a deceased
partner is not liable merely because the name continues. **s. 30** — acts with **intent to defraud**
make liability **unlimited** for the LLP and the guilty partners, the LLP escaping only if it
proves absence of knowledge or authority; imprisonment up to **five years** and fine **₹50,000 to
₹5,00,000**; compensation under 30(3). **s. 31** — whistle blowing: penalties may be **reduced or
waived**, and no discrimination against the informant.

**Contribution.** **s. 32** — money, tangible or intangible property, promissory notes, agreements
to contribute, and **contracts for services performed or to be performed**; the monetary value is
**disclosed in the accounts**. **s. 33** — the obligation is as per the LLP agreement, and a
creditor who acted in reliance **without notice of a compromise** may enforce the **original**
obligation.

**Accounts and filings.** **s. 34** — proper books at the registered office, cash or accrual,
double entry; **Statement of Account and Solvency prepared within six months** of the year end and
**signed by the designated partners**; **filed** within a further **30 days** (Form 8); audit as
prescribed. **Audit required if turnover exceeds ₹40,00,000 OR contribution exceeds ₹25,00,000.**
**s. 35** — **annual return within 60 days of the closure** of the financial year (Form 11).
**s. 36** — incorporation document, partner particulars, SAS and annual return are **open to public
inspection**. **s. 37** — false statement: imprisonment up to **two years** and fine **₹1,00,000 to
₹5,00,000**. **s. 69** — late filing carries an additional fee of **₹100 for every day of delay**.

**Transferable interest.** **s. 42** — the right to a **share of profits and losses and to receive
distributions** is transferable wholly or in part; the transfer causes **no disassociation and no
dissolution**; and it gives the transferee **no management rights and no access to information**.

**Conversion.** **s. 55** firm (**Second Schedule**), **s. 56** private company (**Third
Schedule**), **s. 57** unlisted public company (**Fourth Schedule**). Company conditions: **no
subsisting security interest** and **all shareholders and no one else** become partners. Firm
condition: **all the partners of the firm and no one else**. **s. 58** — certificate of
registration; inform the Registrar of Firms or of Companies within **15 days**; on and from the
date specified, the **whole undertaking vests** in the LLP without further assurance and the firm
or company is **deemed dissolved and removed** from the records; proceedings, employment contracts
and agreements continue; licences transfer **subject to the other Act**. Conversion statement on
correspondence for **twelve months**, starting **not later than fourteen days** after registration.

**Winding up and the rest.** **s. 63** — voluntary or by the **Tribunal**. **s. 64** — the LLP so
decides; **below two partners for more than six months**; acts against the sovereignty and
integrity of India, the security of the State or public order; default in filing the SAS or annual
return for **five consecutive financial years**; **just and equitable**. Inability to pay debts was
**omitted** in 2021. **s. 65** — rules. **s. 75** — striking off after inactivity (the rules use
**two years**). **s. 59** — foreign LLPs, with Companies Act 2013 provisions applied by rules.
**s. 39** — compounding by the **Regional Director**, offences **punishable with fine only**, a sum
between the minimum and the maximum fine. **s. 74** — general penalty **₹5,000 to ₹5,00,000** plus
up to **₹50 a day**. **s. 76A** — **half penalties** for a **small LLP** or start-up LLP, capped at
₹1,00,000 for the LLP and ₹50,000 for every partner.
