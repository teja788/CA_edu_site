A cloth merchant in Erode accepts a bill for ₹ 4,80,000. The bill travels: he hands it to his
supplier, who signs the back and passes it to a transporter, who signs and passes it to a bank.
By the time it comes home to be paid, four names sit on the paper and none of them was in the
room when the deal was struck. If the very first transaction turns out to have been a fraud, who
loses — the merchant who was cheated, or the bank that took the paper for full value and knew
nothing?

The Contract Act would say the fraud travels with the contract and the bank gets nothing. The
Negotiable Instruments Act says the opposite. **A person who takes a negotiable instrument for
value, in good faith and before maturity gets a better title than the person who gave it to him.**
That single sentence is the reason this Act exists. Everything in this chapter — the presumptions,
the endorsements, the crossings, the notice of dishonour — is machinery built around it.

The Act came into force on **1 March 1882**. It deals with three instruments by name — the
**promissory note**, the **bill of exchange** and the **cheque** — and it extends to the whole of
India. Where the Act is silent, the Indian Contract Act 1872 fills the gap, because every
negotiable instrument is at bottom a contract to pay money.

## 1. What "negotiable" means, and the five characteristics

The Act nowhere defines a "negotiable instrument" by its qualities. **Section 13(1)** says only
that a negotiable instrument **means a promissory note, bill of exchange or cheque payable either
to order or to bearer**. The working definition comes from the case law, and it has two halves:

- **Negotiable** — freely transferable by delivery, or by endorsement and delivery, so that the
  transferee can sue on it **in his own name**.
- **Instrument** — a written document creating a right in favour of some person.

Put together: **a written document, transferable by delivery or by endorsement and delivery, which
gives the transferee a right to sue on it in his own name and, if he takes it in good faith and
for value, a title free from the defects of the transferor's title.**

**The five characteristics.** These are examined as a list, and every one of them is really a
consequence of the last.

1. **Freely transferable.** No stamped transfer deed, no notice to the debtor, no consent from
   anybody. A bearer instrument passes by **delivery alone**; an order instrument passes by
   **endorsement and delivery**.
2. **The holder's title is free from defects.** A **holder in due course** takes the instrument
   clean, even if the person who gave it to him had a defective title. This is the feature that
   separates negotiability from mere transferability.
3. **The holder can sue in his own name.** He need not join the original payee, and he need not
   prove that the debtor ever agreed to deal with him.
4. **The instrument is presumed to have been made for consideration.** Section 118(a) says so, and
   the burden of proving otherwise lies on the person who denies it.
5. **It is payable to order or to bearer.** Section 13(1) requires it. A document payable to a
   named person only, with the words "or order" struck out and transfer expressly forbidden, is
   not negotiable at all.

**Transferability is not negotiability.** Almost any chose in action can be *transferred*; only a
negotiable instrument can be *negotiated*. The difference is set out in the table, and it is worth
a 4-mark question on its own.

| | **Ordinary transfer (assignment)** | **Negotiation** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How it is done | Written assignment, often stamped, plus notice to the debtor | Delivery (bearer) or endorsement and delivery (order) |
| Title the transferee gets | Only what the transferor had, defects and all | A **holder in due course** gets a title **free from defects** |
| Notice to the debtor | Necessary to bind him | Not necessary |
| Consideration | Must be proved by the person suing | **Presumed** under s. 118(a) |
| Who may sue | Assignee, subject to every equity available against the assignor | Holder, in his **own name**, free of prior equities |

**Negotiable by statute and negotiable by custom.** The Act names three instruments. Commercial
usage recognises others, and the courts have treated them as negotiable because merchants do:
**hundis**, **share warrants payable to bearer**, **bearer debentures**, **dividend warrants**,
**circular notes** and **railway receipts** in some usages. Instruments that are **not**
negotiable, however much they look the part: a **money order**, a **postal order**, a **fixed
deposit receipt**, a **share certificate**, and a **letter of credit**.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Two exclusions inside s. 4 itself that examiners like. A <strong>currency note is not a
    promissory note</strong> — the section says so in terms, which is why the ₹ 500 note in your
    pocket is money and not paper governed by this Act. And an instrument is not a promissory note
    if the promise is to deliver <strong>goods</strong> or to do anything other than pay a certain
    sum of <strong>money</strong>.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. The presumptions the law makes — sections 118 and 119

The presumptions are why a holder can sue on a piece of paper without proving the story behind it.
**Section 118** says that until the contrary is proved, the following shall be presumed:

- **118(a) — of consideration.** That every negotiable instrument was **made or drawn for
  consideration**, and that every such instrument, when it has been accepted, endorsed, negotiated
  or transferred, was accepted, endorsed, negotiated or transferred **for consideration**.
- **118(b) — as to date.** That every negotiable instrument bearing a date was **made or drawn on
  such date**.
- **118(c) — as to time of acceptance.** That every accepted bill of exchange was accepted
  **within a reasonable time after its date and before its maturity**.
- **118(d) — as to time of transfer.** That every transfer of a negotiable instrument was made
  **before its maturity**.
- **118(e) — as to the order of endorsements.** That the endorsements appearing on the instrument
  were made **in the order in which they appear** on it.
- **118(f) — as to stamp.** That a lost promissory note, bill or cheque was **duly stamped**.
- **118(g) — that the holder is a holder in due course.** That the holder of a negotiable
  instrument is a **holder in due course**.

**Section 119 — presumption on proof of protest.** In a suit upon an instrument which has been
**dishonoured**, the Court shall, on proof of the **protest**, presume the fact of dishonour,
unless and until that fact is disproved.

**The one presumption that flips.** The proviso to s. 118(g) is the most heavily examined line in
the whole group. Where the instrument has been **obtained from its lawful owner, or from any
person in lawful custody thereof, by means of an offence or fraud, or for an unlawful
consideration**, the burden of proving that the holder is a holder in due course **lies upon
him**. So the moment the defendant proves fraud, theft or an unlawful consideration at the origin
of the instrument, the holder must climb out of the hole himself: he must prove that he gave
value, in good faith, before maturity.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Students write that the presumptions are <em>conclusive</em>. Every one of them opens with
    "until the contrary is proved" — they are <strong>rebuttable</strong>. They decide who carries
    the burden of proof at the start of the trial, not who wins. And the proviso to s. 118(g)
    moves that burden back onto the holder once fraud, an offence or an unlawful consideration is
    shown at the origin.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 139 does the same job for a cheque case.** In a prosecution under s. 138 the Court shall
**presume, unless the contrary is proved, that the holder received the cheque for the discharge,
in whole or in part, of a debt or other liability**. A drawer who says "that cheque was a blank
security, I owed nothing" must prove it; the complainant does not have to prove the debt first.

## 3. Promissory note, bill of exchange and cheque — sections 4, 5 and 6

**Section 4 — promissory note.** A promissory note is an instrument **in writing** (not being a
bank note or a currency note) containing an **unconditional undertaking**, signed by the maker, to
**pay a certain sum of money** only to, or to the order of, a certain person, or to the bearer of
the instrument.

Six essentials, and a one-mark question is usually made by deleting one of them:

1. **In writing**, and **signed by the maker**.
2. An **undertaking to pay** — an acknowledgement of debt alone ("I owe Ram ₹ 20,000") is not a
   note, because there is no promise to pay.
3. The undertaking must be **unconditional**. "I promise to pay ₹ 50,000 when my crop is sold" is
   not a note. But a **stated future date is not a condition**, because a date is certain to
   arrive, and a promise to pay on the death of a named person is good for the same reason.
4. The **sum must be certain**, and it must be **money only**. "₹ 50,000 and 200 metres of cloth"
   fails. A sum with interest at a stated rate, or by stated instalments with a default clause,
   is still certain.
5. The **parties must be certain** — the maker signed, and the payee is named or is the bearer.
6. It must be **duly stamped** under the Indian Stamp Act.

A promissory note has **two parties**: the **maker** (the debtor, who promises) and the **payee**
(the creditor). Because the debtor has already signed, **a note is never accepted** — there is
nobody left to accept it.

**Section 5 — bill of exchange.** A bill of exchange is an instrument in writing containing an
**unconditional order**, signed by the maker, **directing a certain person to pay a certain sum of
money** only to, or to the order of, a certain person, or to the bearer of the instrument.

The essentials are the same six, with one word changed — **order** instead of undertaking — and
one more party. A bill has **three parties**: the **drawer** (the creditor, who gives the order),
the **drawee** (the debtor, who is ordered to pay, and who becomes the **acceptor** when he signs
across the face), and the **payee** (whoever is to receive the money, very often the drawer
himself).

<div class="formula">A note is a PROMISE made by the debtor — 2 parties, no acceptance. A bill is an ORDER given by the creditor — 3 parties, and it must be accepted before the drawee is liable on it.</div>

**Section 6 — cheque.** A cheque is a **bill of exchange drawn on a specified banker and not
expressed to be payable otherwise than on demand**, and it **includes the electronic image of a
truncated cheque and a cheque in the electronic form**. So every cheque is a bill of exchange, but
a bill is a cheque only if both extra conditions hold: it is drawn on a **banker**, and it is
payable **on demand**.

The two electronic limbs come from the 2002 amendment and matter in one-mark questions:

- A **cheque in the electronic form** is a cheque drawn in electronic form using a secure system
  with a digital signature.
- A **truncated cheque** is a cheque which is **truncated during the course of a clearing cycle**,
  either by the clearing house or by the paying or receiving bank, the physical movement of the
  cheque being **stopped and replaced by an electronic image** for further processing.

**Validity of a cheque.** A cheque is by banking practice treated as **stale after three months**
from the date it bears, and a banker will return it as "cheque outdated" or "stale". That period
is not in the Act; it comes from Reserve Bank of India directions, and it matters for s. 138,
where the cheque must be presented **within its period of validity** before the section can be
used at all.

**The three instruments compared.** This table is a standing 5- to 6-mark question.

| | **Promissory note** | **Bill of exchange** | **Cheque** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nature | Unconditional **promise** | Unconditional **order** | An order — a species of bill |
| Who writes it | The **debtor** (maker) | The **creditor** (drawer) | The **account holder** (drawer) |
| Parties | **Two** — maker, payee | **Three** — drawer, drawee, payee | **Three** — drawer, drawee bank, payee |
| Drawee | None | Any person | **Only a specified banker** |
| Acceptance | **Never** required | **Required** for a time bill | Not required |
| Payable | On demand or after a period | On demand or after a period | **Only on demand** |
| Days of grace | Three, if a time note | Three, if a time bill | **None** |
| Can it be crossed? | No | No | **Yes**, ss. 123 to 131A |
| Notice of dishonour | Necessary | Necessary | Not necessary as against the drawer, who is the person primarily in default |
| Stamp | Required | Required | **Not** required |
| Maker's position | Maker is **primarily** liable | Acceptor is **primarily** liable; drawer is liable in default | Drawer is **primarily** liable |

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Two reversals to guard against. <strong>A cheque needs no acceptance and gets no days of
    grace</strong>, because it is payable on demand — students who have just learned the
    three-days-of-grace rule apply it to cheques and lose the mark. And on a bill,
    <strong>the drawee is not liable to anybody until he accepts</strong>; before acceptance the
    holder's remedy on dishonour is against the <strong>drawer</strong>, not the drawee, however
    clearly the drawee owed the drawer money.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. The parties to an instrument

**On a promissory note.**

- **Maker** — the person who promises to pay. Primarily liable.
- **Payee** — the person to whom, or to whose order, payment is to be made.

**On a bill of exchange or a cheque.**

- **Drawer** — the person who makes the bill or draws the cheque.
- **Drawee** — the person directed to pay. On a cheque the drawee is always the **banker**.
- **Acceptor** — the drawee **after he has signed his assent** on the bill and delivered it, or
  given notice of the acceptance, to the holder (s. 7). Until then he is only the drawee. Once he
  accepts, he becomes the party **primarily liable**.
- **Payee** — the person named as the recipient. If the payee's name is fictitious or the
  instrument is payable to bearer, the bearer is the payee.
- **Drawee in case of need** — where the name of any person is given in the bill, **in addition to
  the drawee**, to be resorted to in case of need, that person is the drawee in case of need
  (s. 7). The bill is **not** treated as dishonoured until it has been **presented to him also**
  and he too has refused (s. 115 principle). A "case of need" name is written on a bill precisely
  so that a foreign holder has somebody local to turn to.
- **Acceptor for honour** — where a bill has been **noted or protested** for non-acceptance or for
  better security, **any person who is not already liable on it** may, with the consent of the
  holder, accept it **supra protest** for the honour of any party liable on it (s. 108). He
  becomes bound to pay if the original drawee does not, and on paying he acquires the rights of a
  holder against the party for whose honour he accepted, and against all parties prior to that
  party (s. 111 principle).
- **Endorser and endorsee** — the person who signs the back to transfer the instrument is the
  **endorser**; the person to whom it is so transferred is the **endorsee**.
- **Holder** and **holder in due course** — §5 below.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The <strong>drawee in case of need</strong> is named by the DRAWER when the bill is drawn and
    appears on the face of it. The <strong>acceptor for honour</strong> appears only
    <strong>after</strong> a noting or protest and is a volunteer who was not liable before. The
    two are constantly swapped in options: the first is a fall-back name printed in advance, the
    second is a rescuer who steps in afterwards.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Payment for honour — s. 113.** Distinct again from acceptance for honour. **After a bill has
been protested for non-payment**, any person may pay it **supra protest** for the honour of any
party liable on it, and on doing so he is entitled to all the rights of the holder against the
party for whose honour he paid. He must **declare before a notary public** the party for whose
honour he pays, and the declaration must be recorded.

## 5. Holder, holder in due course and payment in due course — sections 8, 9 and 10

**Section 8 — holder.** The holder of a promissory note, bill of exchange or cheque means any
person **entitled in his own name to the possession thereof and to receive or recover the amount
due thereon from the parties thereto**. Where the note, bill or cheque is **lost or destroyed**,
its holder is the person **so entitled at the time of such loss or destruction**.

Two limbs, and both must be satisfied:

- **Entitlement in his own name** — a servant, an agent or a bank collecting for a customer holds
  the paper but is not the "holder", because the right is not his.
- **Entitlement to receive or recover the amount** — a person who has stolen a bearer cheque has
  possession but no entitlement, so he is not a holder at all.

**Section 9 — holder in due course.** A holder in due course means **any person who, for
consideration, became the possessor of a promissory note, bill of exchange or cheque if payable to
bearer, or the payee or endorsee thereof if payable to order, before the amount mentioned in it
became payable, and without having sufficient cause to believe that any defect existed in the
title of the person from whom he derived his title**.

Four conditions, all of them necessary:

1. **He must be a holder** — entitled in his own name.
2. **For consideration.** A donee, a person who took the instrument as a gift, is a holder but
   never a holder in due course.
3. **Before maturity.** He must have become the possessor, payee or endorsee **before the amount
   became payable**. A person who takes an overdue instrument takes it subject to every defect.
4. **In good faith, without sufficient cause to believe in any defect** in the title of his
   transferor. The test is not merely honesty; it asks whether the circumstances were such that a
   reasonable person would have been put on enquiry.

| | **Holder — s. 8** | **Holder in due course — s. 9** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Consideration | Not necessary | **Essential** |
| Timing | May acquire before or after maturity | **Must acquire before maturity** |
| Good faith | Not part of the definition | **Essential** — no sufficient cause to believe in a defect |
| Title | No better than his transferor's | **Free from the defects** in the transferor's title |
| Privileges | Only the ordinary rights of a transferee | The privileges in ss. 20, 36, 42, 43, 53, 58, 120, 121 and 122 |
| Every one is | Not necessarily a holder in due course | Always also a holder |

**Section 10 — payment in due course.** Payment in due course means payment **in accordance with
the apparent tenor of the instrument, in good faith and without negligence, to any person in
possession thereof under circumstances which do not afford a reasonable ground for believing that
he is not entitled to receive payment of the amount therein mentioned**.

Read it as four tests, because a paying banker's whole protection depends on satisfying all of
them:

1. **According to the apparent tenor** — on or after the due date, for the amount and in the
   manner the instrument shows on its face. Payment of a **time** bill before maturity is not
   payment in due course.
2. **In good faith and without negligence.** Negligence sinks the payment even where there is no
   dishonesty; paying a cheque with an obviously altered amount is negligent.
3. **To the person in possession** of the instrument — payment to somebody who cannot produce the
   paper is at the payer's own risk.
4. **In circumstances raising no reasonable ground for believing he is not entitled** to receive
   payment.

Why it matters: only a payment in due course **discharges the instrument** under s. 82(c), and
only a payment in due course lets the paying banker claim the protections of ss. 85, 89 and 128.

## 6. The privileges of a holder in due course

Nine privileges, and they are the reason people take commercial paper at all. Learn the section
numbers with them — the examiner asks for the section as often as for the rule.

1. **Section 20 — an inchoate stamped instrument.** Where a person signs and delivers a stamped
   paper, wholly or partly blank, to another so that it may be made into a negotiable instrument,
   he gives the holder authority to complete it for **any amount not exceeding the amount covered
   by the stamp**. As between the immediate parties the signer is liable only for the amount he
   authorised, but **a holder in due course may recover the whole amount so filled in**, so long
   as it is within the stamp.
2. **Section 36 — every prior party is liable.** Every prior party to a negotiable instrument is
   **liable thereon to a holder in due course until the instrument is duly satisfied**. He may sue
   any of them, all of them, or one of them, in any order.
3. **Section 42 — a bill drawn in a fictitious name.** An acceptor of a bill drawn in a fictitious
   name and payable to the drawer's order is **not, by reason that such name is fictitious,
   relieved from liability to a holder in due course**, provided the holder can show that the
   first endorsement and the drawer's signature are **in the same handwriting**.
4. **Section 43 — an instrument made without consideration.** A negotiable instrument made, drawn,
   accepted, endorsed or transferred **without consideration** creates **no obligation of payment
   between the parties to the transaction**. But if any such party has transferred the instrument
   **to a holder for consideration**, that holder, **and every subsequent holder deriving title
   from him**, may recover the amount due on it from any prior party. So want of consideration is
   a good defence between the original parties and **no defence at all** against a holder in due
   course.
5. **Section 46 with s. 47 — no defence of conditional delivery.** Where an instrument was
   delivered **conditionally or for a special purpose only**, that fact is a good defence between
   the immediate parties, but it cannot be set up **against a holder in due course**.
6. **Section 53 — the holder derives a good title.** A holder of a negotiable instrument **who
   derives title from a holder in due course** has the rights thereon of that holder in due
   course. This is the "clean hands wash the whole chain" rule: once a holder in due course has
   held the paper, every later holder — even one who knew of the original defect — takes the
   cleansed title, unless he was himself a party to the fraud.
7. **Section 58 — the instrument obtained by unlawful means or for an unlawful consideration.**
   Where an instrument has been **lost, or obtained by an offence or fraud or for an unlawful
   consideration**, no possessor or endorsee who claims through the person who found or so
   obtained it is entitled to receive the amount — **unless he is, or claims through, a holder in
   due course**.
8. **Sections 120 and 121 — estoppels.** No **maker** of a note and no **drawer** of a bill or
   cheque, and no **acceptor** of a bill for the honour of the drawer, shall in a suit thereon by
   a holder in due course be permitted to deny the **validity of the instrument as originally
   made or drawn** (s. 120). And no **maker** of a note and no **acceptor** of a bill payable to
   order shall, in such a suit, be permitted to deny the **payee's capacity, at the date of the
   note or bill, to endorse the same** (s. 121).
9. **Section 122 — estoppel against denying the signature of a prior party.** No **endorser** of a
   negotiable instrument shall, in a suit thereon by a subsequent holder, be permitted to deny the
   **signature or capacity to contract of any prior party** to the instrument.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Section 53 is the privilege students under-use. It means the drawer's fraud defence is
    <strong>destroyed for good</strong> the first time the paper passes through clean hands. If A
    obtains a bill by fraud, endorses it to B who takes it for value in good faith before
    maturity, and B endorses it to C who <em>knows all about the fraud</em>, C still recovers —
    because he derives title from B, a holder in due course. The only person s. 53 will not help
    is a party to the original fraud who buys the paper back.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Vignette 1 — an inchoate cheque, a fraud, and the chain that cleans it (moderate-hard)">
  <p>
    Kavitha runs a printing press at Coimbatore. On <strong>3 February 2026</strong> she signs a
    blank cheque on her current account, leaving the amount and the payee blank, and hands it to
    her office manager Selvan with instructions to fill it in for the paper supplier's bill,
    "which will not exceed <strong>₹ 1,20,000</strong>". Selvan fills the cheque in for
    <strong>₹ 6,50,000</strong>, makes it payable to himself, and endorses it to Anand, who buys
    it for <strong>₹ 6,20,000</strong> in the ordinary course of business, knowing nothing of
    Kavitha or of Selvan's instructions. Anand endorses it to Bharani, who has heard the office
    gossip and knows exactly what Selvan did. Bharani presents the cheque. Advise Kavitha.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 1 — what kind of instrument is this?</div>
    A cheque signed and delivered blank as to amount and payee, so that it may be made into a
    negotiable instrument, is an <strong>inchoate instrument under s. 20</strong>. Kavitha's
    signature gives the person in possession <em>prima facie</em> authority to complete it, and
    gives any person who later becomes the holder authority to complete it <strong>for any amount
    not exceeding the amount covered by the stamp</strong> — and a cheque requires no stamp, so no
    ceiling operates from that quarter.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 2 — Kavitha against Selvan</div>
    Section 20 protects only strangers. <strong>As between the immediate parties</strong> the
    signer is liable only for the amount he intended to be paid. Selvan was authorised up to
    <strong>₹ 1,20,000</strong> and no further; against him Kavitha is liable for nothing beyond
    the supplier's genuine bill, and he has committed a fraud besides.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 3 — Kavitha against Anand</div>
    Anand gave consideration (₹ 6,20,000 for a ₹ 6,50,000 cheque), took the cheque before the
    amount became payable, and had no cause to suspect a defect. He is a <strong>holder in due
    course</strong> under s. 9. Section 20 lets him <strong>recover the whole ₹ 6,50,000</strong>,
    because the limit Kavitha placed on Selvan was private and does not appear on the paper.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 4 — Kavitha against Bharani, who knew</div>
    Bharani cannot be a holder in due course himself; he had every cause to believe a defect
    existed. But <strong>s. 53</strong> gives a holder who derives title from a holder in due
    course the rights of that holder in due course. He derives title from Anand, so
    <strong>he too recovers ₹ 6,50,000</strong>. That is the counter-intuitive result being
    tested.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Conclusion</div>
    Kavitha must pay <strong>₹ 6,50,000</strong> on the cheque and is left to her personal remedy
    against Selvan for the <strong>₹ 5,30,000</strong> excess, plus whatever the criminal law
    gives her. The moral is the one s. 20 exists to teach: a signature on a blank instrument is a
    signature on every amount the paper can carry.
  </div>
</Callout>

## 7. Classification: inland, foreign, ambiguous, inchoate and fictitious

**Section 11 — inland instrument.** A promissory note, bill of exchange or cheque **drawn or made
in India and made payable in, or drawn upon any person resident in, India** is an inland
instrument. Two limbs joined by "or", so an instrument drawn in India upon a person resident in
India is inland even though it is made payable abroad.

**Section 12 — foreign instrument.** Any instrument **which is not an inland instrument** is a
foreign instrument. Practical consequence: a foreign bill, when dishonoured, **must be protested**
if the law of the place where it was drawn so requires (s. 104), whereas protest of an inland
instrument is **optional**.

**Section 13(2) — payable to order and payable to bearer.** A negotiable instrument is payable to
order which is expressed to be so payable, or which is expressed to be payable to a particular
person **and does not contain words prohibiting transfer or indicating an intention that it shall
not be transferable**. It is payable to bearer where it is expressed to be so payable, or where
the **only or last endorsement is an endorsement in blank**.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    "Pay Ravi" with no other words is <strong>payable to order</strong>, not restricted — the
    absence of "or order" does not stop transfer, because s. 13(2) asks only whether words
    <em>prohibiting</em> transfer are present. Write "Pay Ravi only", or strike out "or order",
    and the instrument stops being negotiable. This one line decides a whole family of options.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 17 — ambiguous instrument.** Where an instrument may be construed **either as a
promissory note or as a bill of exchange**, the **holder may at his election** treat it as either,
and the instrument shall thenceforward be treated accordingly. The standing examples are a bill
drawn on a **fictitious person**, and a bill in which the **drawer and the drawee are the same
person** — the holder may treat such a bill as a promissory note made by the drawer.

**Section 18 — amount stated differently in figures and in words.** If the amount undertaken or
ordered to be paid is stated differently in figures and in words, **the amount stated in words is
the amount undertaken or ordered to be paid**. Words prevail. A banker in practice returns such a
cheque marked "amount in words and figures differs", but the legal rule if the instrument is sued
upon is the words.

**Section 20 — inchoate stamped instrument.** Where one person **signs and delivers** to another a
paper **stamped in accordance with the law relating to negotiable instruments** then in force in
India, and either **wholly blank or having written thereon an incomplete negotiable instrument**,
he thereby gives *prima facie* authority to the holder thereof to **make or complete it into a
negotiable instrument for any amount specified therein and not exceeding the amount covered by the
stamp**. The person so signing is liable upon such instrument, in the capacity in which he signed,
to any **holder in due course** for such amount — provided that **no person other than a holder in
due course** shall recover from him **anything in excess of the amount intended by him to be paid
thereunder**.

Four conditions for s. 20 to operate: the paper must be **signed**, it must be **delivered**, it
must be **stamped**, and the amount filled in must be **within the stamp**. Miss any one and the
signer is not bound to a stranger either.

**Fictitious instruments — s. 42.** A bill is drawn in a fictitious name where the **drawer is
fictitious**, or where the **payee is a fictitious or non-existing person**. The acceptor of such
a bill, payable to the drawer's order, is **not relieved from liability to a holder in due
course** merely because the name is fictitious, provided the holder shows that the **first
endorsement and the drawer's signature are in the same handwriting**. The reason is plain: the
acceptor accepted a bill knowing to whose order it was payable, and he cannot escape by proving
that the person he dealt with had invented the name.

**The five in a line each.** *Inland* — made and payable, or drawn on a resident, in India.
*Foreign* — anything else; may require protest. *Ambiguous* — capable of being read as a note or a
bill, the holder elects. *Inchoate* — signed, stamped and delivered blank or incomplete.
*Fictitious* — drawn in an invented name, but a holder in due course is still protected.

## 8. Negotiation, delivery and endorsement — sections 14 to 16 and 46 to 60

**Section 14 — negotiation.** When a promissory note, bill of exchange or cheque is **transferred
to any person so as to constitute that person the holder thereof**, the instrument is said to be
negotiated. A transfer that does not make the transferee a holder — handing a cheque to a clerk to
bank it, for instance — is not a negotiation at all.

**Section 46 — delivery is essential.** The making, acceptance or endorsement of a promissory
note, bill of exchange or cheque is **completed by delivery, actual or constructive**. A signature
that never leaves the signer's drawer creates no liability. And:

- As between **immediate parties**, delivery may be shown to have been **conditional or for a
  special purpose only**, and not for the purpose of transferring the property in the instrument.
- A note, bill or cheque payable to **bearer** is negotiable by the **delivery thereof**.
- A note, bill or cheque payable to **order** is negotiable by the **holder by endorsement and
  delivery thereof**.

**Section 47 — negotiation by delivery.** Subject to s. 58, a promissory note, bill of exchange or
cheque **payable to bearer is negotiable by delivery thereof**. The exception sits in the section
itself: it is not negotiated by the delivery **unless such delivery has been made for the purpose
of negotiation** — a cheque handed to a friend for safe custody is not negotiated to him.

**Section 48 — negotiation by endorsement.** Subject to s. 58, a promissory note, bill of exchange
or cheque **payable to order is negotiable by the holder by endorsement and delivery thereof**.

**Section 15 — endorsement.** When the maker or holder of a negotiable instrument **signs the
same, otherwise than as such maker, for the purpose of negotiation, on the back or face thereof or
on a slip of paper annexed thereto**, or so signs for the same purpose a stamped paper intended to
be completed as a negotiable instrument, he is said to endorse the same, and is called the
**endorser**. The slip of paper annexed to an instrument whose back is full is called an
**allonge**.

**Section 16 — endorsement in blank and in full.** If the endorser **signs his name only**, the
endorsement is said to be **in blank**; if he adds a **direction to pay the amount to, or to the
order of, a specified person**, the endorsement is said to be **in full**, and the person so
specified is the **endorsee** of the instrument.

**Section 49 — converting a blank endorsement into a full one.** The holder of a negotiable
instrument endorsed in blank may, **without signing his own name**, by writing above the
endorser's signature a **direction to pay to any other person as endorsee**, convert the
endorsement in blank into an endorsement in full. He does not thereby incur the responsibility of
an endorser — he has not signed, so he cannot be sued as one.

**Section 50 — the effect of an endorsement.** The endorsement of a negotiable instrument followed
by delivery **transfers to the endorsee the property therein with the right of further
negotiation**. But the endorsement may, by express words, **restrict or exclude** that right, or
may merely **constitute the endorsee an agent** to endorse the instrument or to receive its
contents for the endorser or for some other specified person.

**Section 51 — who may negotiate.** Every **sole maker, drawer, payee or endorsee**, or all of
several joint makers, drawers, payees or endorsees, of a negotiable instrument may, **if the
negotiability of the instrument has not been restricted or excluded**, endorse and negotiate it.
The proviso is the trap: the **drawer or an endorser cannot endorse or negotiate unless he is in
lawful possession of, or is the holder of, the instrument**; and the **payee or endorsee cannot
endorse unless he is the holder**.

**Section 52 — an endorser excluding or limiting his own liability.** The endorser may, by express
words in the endorsement, **exclude his own liability** on the instrument (a *sans recours*
endorsement), or **make it conditional** on the happening of a specified event. Where he so
excludes his liability and afterwards **becomes the holder** of the instrument, **all intermediate
endorsers are liable to him**.

**Section 55 — a blank endorsement followed by a full one.** If a negotiable instrument, after
having been endorsed in blank, is endorsed in full, the amount of it **cannot be claimed from the
endorser in full except by the person to whom it has been endorsed in full, or by one who derives
title through such person**.

**Section 56 — endorsement for part of the amount.** **No writing on a negotiable instrument is
valid for the purpose of negotiation if such writing purports to transfer only a part of the
amount appearing to be due on the instrument.** But where such amount has been **partly paid**, a
note to that effect may be endorsed on the instrument, which may then be **negotiated for the
residue**.

**Section 60 — negotiation before and after maturity.** A negotiable instrument may be negotiated
**until payment or satisfaction thereof by the maker, drawee or acceptor at or after maturity**,
but **not after such payment or satisfaction**. An instrument may therefore still be negotiated
after it is overdue — the transferee simply cannot be a holder in due course.

**Sections 57 and 59 — two smaller rules.** Under **s. 57**, the legal representative of a
deceased person **cannot negotiate by delivery only** a note, bill or cheque payable to order and
endorsed by the deceased but not delivered. Under **s. 59**, the holder of a negotiable instrument
who has acquired it **after dishonour, whether by non-acceptance or non-payment, with notice
thereof, or after maturity**, has only, as against the other parties, the **rights thereon of his
transferor** — he steps into the transferor's shoes, defects and all.

**Negotiation back.** Where an instrument is negotiated back to a **prior party**, that party
cannot enforce it against the **intermediate parties** to whom he was previously liable; the law
declines to allow a circle of actions. The one exception is s. 52: an endorser who endorsed
**sans recours** and later becomes the holder **may** sue all the intermediate endorsers, because
he was never liable to them.

## 9. The kinds of endorsement

Six kinds. Each is defined by what the endorser writes above his signature, and each changes what
the endorsee can do next.

1. **Blank or general endorsement — s. 16(1).** The endorser **signs his name only**. The
   instrument becomes **payable to bearer** (s. 54) and passes thereafter by **delivery alone**,
   even if it started life payable to order. This is why a blank-endorsed cheque is as dangerous
   to lose as cash.
2. **Full or special endorsement — s. 16(1).** The endorser adds a **direction to pay to, or to
   the order of, a specified person**. The instrument then remains payable to order and needs that
   person's endorsement to pass on.
3. **Restrictive endorsement — s. 50.** The endorsement **restricts or excludes the right of
   further negotiation**, or makes the endorsee a **mere agent**. "Pay Kiran only", "Pay Kiran for
   my account", "Pay Kiran for collection". The endorsee may receive the money but cannot
   negotiate the instrument further.
4. **Endorsement *sans recours* (without recourse) — s. 52.** The endorser **excludes his own
   liability**: "Pay Devi or order, sans recours". If the instrument is dishonoured, Devi cannot
   look to this endorser, though she keeps her rights against every other party.
5. **Conditional or qualified endorsement — s. 52.** The endorser makes his **own liability**, or
   the endorsee's right to receive payment, **depend on a specified event**: "Pay Devi on her
   marriage", "Pay Devi on delivery of the goods". The condition binds only the endorser and the
   endorsee; **the paying party may ignore it** and pay the endorsee, because a condition written
   on the back cannot make the instrument itself conditional.
6. **Partial endorsement — s. 56.** An endorsement purporting to transfer **only part of the
   amount** due. It is **invalid for the purpose of negotiation**. The only way to negotiate part
   of an instrument is the route s. 56 itself allows: where part has actually been **paid**, note
   that fact on the instrument and negotiate the **residue**.

Two more labels the study material uses: a *sans frais* endorsement (the endorsee is not to incur
any expense on the endorser's account on dishonour), and a **facultative** endorsement, where the
endorser **waives some right** to which he is entitled — most often "notice of dishonour waived",
which means he remains liable even if he is never given notice.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The two that get swapped every year. A <strong>restrictive</strong> endorsement kills the
    <em>endorsee's</em> right to negotiate further; a <strong>sans recours</strong> endorsement
    kills the <em>endorser's</em> own liability and leaves negotiability untouched. And a
    <strong>partial</strong> endorsement is not merely ineffective against strangers — s. 56 makes
    it <strong>invalid for negotiation altogether</strong>, so nothing passes.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. Crossing of cheques — sections 123 to 131A

A crossing is two parallel transverse lines drawn across the face of a cheque. It is a
**direction to the paying banker not to pay the cheque over the counter, but only to a banker**.
Crossing is possible **only on a cheque** — never on a bill or a note — and it is the cheapest
safety device the Act provides, because it forces the money through an account and so leaves a
trail.

**Section 123 — general crossing.** Where a cheque bears across its face an addition of the words
"**and company**" or any abbreviation thereof, between two parallel transverse lines, or of **two
parallel transverse lines simply**, either with or without the words "**not negotiable**", that
addition shall be deemed a crossing, and the cheque shall be deemed to be **crossed generally**.

**Section 124 — special crossing.** Where a cheque bears across its face an addition of the **name
of a banker**, either with or without the words "not negotiable", that addition shall be deemed a
crossing, and the cheque shall be deemed to be **crossed specially**, and to be crossed to that
banker. The two parallel lines are **not essential** to a special crossing; the banker's name is.

**Section 126 — the effect of a crossing.** Where a cheque is crossed **generally**, the banker on
whom it is drawn shall **not pay it otherwise than to a banker**. Where it is crossed
**specially**, he shall not pay it otherwise than **to the banker to whom it is crossed, or his
agent for collection**.

**Section 127 — a cheque crossed specially more than once.** Where a cheque is crossed specially
to **more than one banker**, except when crossed to an agent for the purpose of collection, the
banker on whom it is drawn shall **refuse payment**.

**Section 125 — who may cross, and when.** A cheque may be crossed after issue as well as before:

- Where a cheque is **uncrossed**, the **holder** may cross it generally or specially.
- Where a cheque is **crossed generally**, the holder may cross it **specially**.
- Where a cheque is crossed **generally or specially**, the holder may **add the words "not
  negotiable"**.
- Where a cheque is crossed **specially**, the **banker to whom it is crossed** may again cross it
  specially to **another banker, his agent, for collection**.

So a crossing may always be made **more restrictive** and never less. Opening a crossing — turning
a crossed cheque back into an open one — can be done only by the **drawer**, by writing "crossing
cancelled" and signing in full; the Act does not provide for it, and banks treat it with great
suspicion.

**Section 128 — payment in due course of a crossed cheque.** Where the banker on whom a crossed
cheque is drawn has paid the same **in due course**, the banker paying the cheque, **and (in case
such cheque has come to the hands of the payee) the drawer thereof**, shall respectively be
entitled to the same rights, and be placed in the same position, as if the amount of the cheque
had been paid to and received by the true owner thereof.

**Section 129 — payment of a crossed cheque out of due course.** Any banker paying a cheque
crossed generally otherwise than to a banker, or crossed specially otherwise than to the banker to
whom it is crossed or his agent for collection, is **liable to the true owner** for any loss he
may sustain owing to the cheque having been so paid.

**Section 130 — the "not negotiable" crossing.** A person taking a cheque crossed generally or
specially, bearing in either case the words "**not negotiable**", **shall not have, and shall not
be capable of giving, a better title to the cheque than that which the person from whom he took it
had**.

Read that sentence twice, because it is the single most misunderstood line in the chapter. "Not
negotiable" does **not** stop the cheque from being transferred. The cheque remains
**transferable**; what it loses is **negotiability** — the power to give a transferee a better
title than the transferor had. Every subsequent holder takes it subject to every defect, exactly
as an assignee of an ordinary debt would.

**Section 131 — protection of the collecting banker, and "account payee".** A banker who has, **in
good faith and without negligence, received payment for a customer of a cheque crossed generally
or specially to himself**, shall not, in case the title to the cheque proves defective, incur any
liability to the true owner by reason only of having received such payment. The Explanation makes
clear that a banker who **credits his customer's account with the amount of the cheque before
receiving payment** does not cease to receive payment for the customer merely because of that
credit. **Section 131A** applies the crossing provisions, so far as they can apply, to
**drafts** drawn by one office of a bank upon another.

An **"account payee" or "A/c payee only" crossing** is **not defined in the Act at all**. It is a
creature of banking practice, and it operates as a **direction to the collecting banker** to
credit the proceeds only to the account of the named payee. A collecting banker who ignores it and
collects for somebody else will almost always be held **negligent**, and so loses the s. 131
protection — which is why, in practice, an account-payee crossing has become the strongest of the
lot even though no section creates it.

| Crossing | What appears on the cheque | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **General**, s. 123 | Two parallel transverse lines, with or without "and company" | Pay only **to a banker** |
| **Special**, s. 124 | The **name of a banker**, lines optional | Pay only to **that banker** or his collecting agent |
| **Not negotiable**, s. 130 | The words added to a general or special crossing | Transferee gets **no better title** than his transferor |
| **Account payee** | Words added by practice, not by the Act | Direction to the **collecting** banker; proceeds only to the named payee's account |
| **Double special**, s. 127 | Two bankers' names | Banker must **refuse payment**, unless the second is an agent for collection |

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Three crossings, three different targets, and mixing them up costs marks every year.
    A <strong>general or special crossing binds the PAYING banker</strong> (ss. 126, 129). A
    <strong>"not negotiable" crossing binds the TRANSFEREE's title</strong> (s. 130) and nobody
    else. An <strong>"account payee" crossing binds the COLLECTING banker</strong> in practice
    (through s. 131 negligence), and is nowhere in the Act. And none of them makes the cheque
    non-transferable — only "Pay X only", written on the face, does that.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 11. Maturity, days of grace and the due date — sections 21 to 25

**Section 21 — "after sight".** In a **promissory note**, "after sight" means **after presentment
for sight**. In a **bill of exchange**, it means **after acceptance, or noting for non-acceptance,
or protest for non-acceptance**. The words "at sight" and "on presentment" mean **on demand**.

**Section 22 — maturity and the three days of grace.** The maturity of a promissory note or bill
of exchange is **the date at which it falls due**. Every promissory note or bill of exchange which
is **not expressed to be payable on demand, at sight or on presentment** is at maturity on the
**third day after the day on which it is expressed to be payable**.

So the three days of grace are not a banker's courtesy. They are **part of the legal due date**,
and they are added to every **time** instrument. An instrument payable **on demand, at sight or on
presentment gets no grace at all**, which is why a **cheque never gets grace**.

**Section 23 — months.** In calculating the date at which an instrument made payable a stated
number of **months after date, after sight, or after a certain event** is at maturity, the period
stated **terminates on the day of the month which corresponds with the day on which the instrument
is dated, or presented for acceptance or sight, or noted or protested for non-acceptance, or on
which the event happens**. If the month in which the period would terminate **has no
corresponding day**, the period terminates on the **last day of such month**.

Never convert a month into thirty days. Two months after 31 December is 28 February (or the 29th
in a leap year), not 1 March.

**Section 24 — days.** In calculating the date at which an instrument made payable a stated number
of **days after date, after sight or after a certain event** is at maturity, the **day of the
date, or of presentment for acceptance or sight, or of protest for non-acceptance, or on which the
event happens, shall be excluded**. Count actual calendar days; the trigger day is day zero.

**Section 25 — when the day of maturity is a public holiday.** When the day on which a promissory
note or bill of exchange is at maturity is a **public holiday**, the instrument shall be deemed to
be due on the **next preceding business day**. The Explanation says that "public holiday"
**includes Sundays** and any other day declared by the Central Government, by notification in the
Official Gazette, to be a public holiday.

<div class="formula">Due date = (day the instrument is expressed to be payable, found by s. 23 for months or s. 24 for days) + 3 days of grace under s. 22 → then test that date against s. 25 and move BACK to the preceding business day if it is a public holiday.</div>

**The order matters.** Grace is added **before** the holiday test, never after. You test the date
the grace produced, and if it is a holiday you step back one business day — which may itself have
to step back again if the preceding day is also a holiday.

**The emergency-holiday practice.** Section 25 covers holidays that were known when the instrument
was drawn. Where a holiday is declared **suddenly and could not have been planned around**, the
settled commercial practice — and the treatment the study material applies — is that payment falls
due on the **next succeeding business day**. Known holiday: pay **early**. Emergency holiday: pay
**late**. Unless a question says otherwise, treat Saturdays as ordinary business days; only Sundays
and notified holidays move a due date.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>Day-counting convention used throughout this chapter.</strong> A period expressed in
    days is counted <strong>from and excluding</strong> the trigger date, so day 1 is the day
    <em>after</em> the date, the sight or the event — that is exactly what s. 24 says, and it is
    the convention the sibling chapters use for every statutory period. A period expressed in
    months ends on the <strong>corresponding date</strong> in the later month (s. 23), and where
    there is no corresponding date, on the <strong>last day</strong> of that month. The same two
    rules are applied by analogy to the statutory periods in s. 138 in §17 below.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Vignette 2 — four bills, four due dates (moderate)">
  <p>
    Ashwini Traders holds four instruments. Find the due date of each. Assume that
    <strong>2 October</strong> is a notified public holiday and that no other holiday falls in any
    of the periods.
  </p>
  <p>
    (a) A bill dated <strong>30 November 2026</strong>, payable <strong>three months after
    date</strong>. (b) A bill dated <strong>3 August 2026</strong>, payable <strong>100 days after
    date</strong>. (c) A bill dated <strong>5 January 2026</strong>, payable <strong>two months
    after sight</strong>, accepted on <strong>10 January 2026</strong>. (d) A bill dated
    <strong>29 June 2026</strong>, payable <strong>three months after date</strong>.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(a) — s. 23, then s. 22</div>

    2027</strong>. Add three days of grace: <strong>3 March 2027</strong>. It is a Wednesday and
    no holiday, so that is the due date. Note that the fact that 28 February 2027 happens to be a
    Sunday is irrelevant — s. 25 tests the <em>day of maturity</em>, not the day the instrument is
    expressed to be payable.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(b) — s. 24, then s. 22</div>
    Count 100 days from 3 August 2026, <strong>excluding</strong> 3 August: 28 days remain in
    August (to the 31st), 30 in September (day 58), 31 in October (day 89), leaving 11 days in
    November — day 100 is <strong>11 November 2026</strong>. Add three days of grace:
    <strong>14 November 2026</strong>. A Saturday, which is an ordinary business day, so the date
    stands.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(c) — s. 21 with s. 23</div>
    "After sight" on a <em>bill</em> runs from <strong>acceptance</strong> (s. 21), so the clock
    starts on 10 January 2026 and not on the date of the bill. Two months from 10 January is
    <strong>10 March 2026</strong>; add three days of grace: <strong>13 March 2026</strong>. The
    distractor every paper plants here is 8 March, computed from the bill's own date.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(d) — s. 23, s. 22, then s. 25</div>
    Three months after 29 June 2026 is <strong>29 September 2026</strong>; add three days of
    grace and you land on <strong>2 October 2026</strong>, which is a notified public holiday.
    Section 25 moves the maturity <strong>back</strong> to the <strong>next preceding business
    day, 1 October 2026</strong>, a Thursday. Moving forward to 3 October is the single commonest
    error in the chapter.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">The pattern to carry into the exam</div>
    Three steps, always in this order: find the day the instrument is <em>expressed</em> to be
    payable (s. 23 for months, s. 24 for days, s. 21 to decide what the clock starts from), add
    <strong>three days of grace</strong> (s. 22), then test <strong>that</strong> date against
    s. 25 and step <strong>back</strong> if it is a public holiday.
  </div>
</Callout>

## 12. Presentment, payment and interest — sections 61 to 81

**Section 61 — presentment for acceptance.** A bill of exchange **payable after sight** must, if no
time or place is specified in it for presentment, be presented to the drawee thereof **for
acceptance**, if he can, after reasonable search, be found, by a person entitled to demand
acceptance, **within a reasonable time after it is drawn, and in business hours on a business
day**. In default of such presentment, no party thereto is liable thereon to the person making
such default. Where a bill is directed to the drawee **at a particular place**, it must be
presented at that place.

**Section 62 — presentment of a promissory note for sight.** A promissory note **payable at a
certain period after sight** must be presented to the maker thereof for sight, by a person
entitled to demand payment, within a reasonable time after it is made and in business hours on a
business day. In default, no party thereto is liable to the person making such default.

**Section 63 — the drawee's time for deliberation.** The holder must, **if so required by the
drawee**, allow the drawee **forty-eight hours, exclusive of public holidays**, to consider
whether he will accept.

**Sections 64 to 66 — presentment for payment.** Notes, bills and cheques must be presented for
payment **to the maker, acceptor or drawee** by or on behalf of the holder, and in default of such
presentment the other parties are not liable to the holder. Presentment must be made **during the
usual hours of business**, and, if at a banker's, **within banking hours** (s. 65). An instrument
payable **after date or after sight** must be presented for payment **at maturity** (s. 66).

**Section 76 — when presentment for payment is unnecessary.** No presentment is necessary, and the
instrument is dishonoured at the due date, in five situations: where the maker, drawee or acceptor
**intentionally prevents** presentment, or, the instrument being payable at his place of business,
**closes it** on a business day during the usual business hours; where he **cannot after due
search be found**; where there is a **part payment or a promise to pay** made by the party liable
after the instrument is due; where the presentment is **expressly or impliedly waived** by the
party entitled to require it; and where the drawer **could not have suffered damage** from want of
presentment.

**Section 78 — to whom payment should be made.** Payment of the amount due on a note, bill or
cheque must, in order to discharge the maker or acceptor, be made **to the holder of the
instrument**. Payment to anybody else, however plausible, does not discharge the instrument — the
holder can still sue.

**Section 79 — interest when a rate is specified.** When interest at a **specified rate** is
expressly made payable on a promissory note or bill of exchange, interest shall be calculated **at
the rate specified**, on the amount of the principal money due thereon, **from the date of the
instrument until tender or realisation** of such amount, or until such date after the institution
of a suit to recover such amount as the Court directs.

**Section 80 — interest when no rate is specified.** When **no rate of interest is specified** in
the instrument, interest on the amount due thereon shall, notwithstanding any agreement relating
to interest between any parties to the instrument, be calculated at the rate of **eighteen per cent
per annum**, from the date at which the same **ought to have been paid by the party charged**,
until tender or realisation of the amount due thereon, or until such date after the institution of
a suit to recover such amount as the Court directs.

<div class="formula">s. 79 — rate specified: run interest at THAT rate from the DATE OF THE INSTRUMENT. s. 80 — no rate specified: run interest at 18% p.a. from the DATE THE MONEY WAS DUE. Two different rates and two different starting dates; options are built by crossing them over.</div>

**Section 81 — delivery of the instrument on payment.** Any person liable to pay, and called upon
by the holder to pay, the amount due on the instrument is, **before payment, entitled to have it
shown**, and is entitled, **on payment, to have it delivered up to him**, or, if the instrument is
lost or cannot be produced, to be **indemnified against any further claim** thereon against him.
Where the cheque is an **electronic image of a truncated cheque**, the paying banker is entitled to
demand any further information regarding the truncated cheque, and on suspicion of fraud may
demand the **presentment of the truncated cheque itself** for verification.

**Section 56 in money terms — a partly paid instrument.** Where part of the amount due has
actually been paid, a note of that fact may be endorsed on the instrument, which may then be
negotiated **for the residue** — that is, for the face amount minus the part paid, and for nothing
else. An endorsement purporting to transfer a part while the whole is still outstanding transfers
**nothing at all**.

<Callout kind="example" label="Vignette 3 — two notes, two interest calculations (moderate)">
  <p>
    Sunanda holds two promissory notes made by Girish. Note P is for <strong>₹ 6,00,000</strong>,
    dated <strong>1 June 2026</strong>, payable <strong>twelve months after date</strong>, and
    bears on its face the words "with interest at <strong>12 per cent per annum</strong>". Girish
    pays it on <strong>1 September 2027</strong>, which is <strong>fifteen months</strong> from
    the date of the note. Note Q is for <strong>₹ 4,50,000</strong>, says nothing about interest,
    and fell due on <strong>10 April 2026</strong>; Girish pays it exactly <strong>eight
    months</strong> later. Compute the interest on each.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Note P — s. 79 applies</div>
    A rate is specified, so s. 79 governs: interest runs <strong>at the specified rate</strong>,
    and it runs <strong>from the date of the instrument</strong> until realisation — not from the
    due date. Period = 1 June 2026 to 1 September 2027 = <strong>15 months</strong>.
    <br />₹ 6,00,000 × 12% × 15/12 = <strong>₹ 90,000</strong>.
    <br />The distractor here is ₹ 18,000, which runs 12% for only the three months
    <em>after</em> maturity, and ₹ 72,000, which runs it for the twelve-month tenor alone.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Note Q — s. 80 applies</div>
    No rate is specified, so s. 80 supplies <strong>18 per cent per annum</strong>, and it runs
    <strong>from the date at which the money ought to have been paid</strong> — the due date, not
    the date of the note.
    <br />₹ 4,50,000 × 18% × 8/12 = <strong>₹ 54,000</strong>.
    <br />Note the two switches between the notes: the <em>rate</em> changes because one
    instrument specifies one and the other does not, and the <em>starting date</em> changes for
    the same reason. Crossing them over gives every wrong option in a question of this kind.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Conclusion</div>
    Girish owes <strong>₹ 6,90,000</strong> on Note P and <strong>₹ 5,04,000</strong> on Note Q.
    Section 80 adds that its rate applies "notwithstanding any agreement relating to interest
    between any parties to the instrument" — a side agreement for a lower rate does not displace
    the statutory 18 per cent once the instrument itself is silent.
  </div>
</Callout>

## 13. Dishonour and notice of dishonour — sections 91 to 98

**Section 91 — dishonour by non-acceptance.** A bill of exchange is said to be dishonoured by
non-acceptance in four situations:

1. When the **drawee, or one of several drawees not being partners, makes default in acceptance**
   upon being duly required to accept the bill;
2. Where presentment is **excused** and the bill is not accepted;
3. Where the drawee is **incompetent to contract**; or
4. Where the acceptance is **qualified** and the holder is entitled to, and does, treat it as a
   dishonour. (Where the drawee is a **fictitious person**, or cannot after reasonable search be
   found, the bill may likewise be treated as dishonoured.)

**Section 92 — dishonour by non-payment.** A promissory note, bill of exchange or cheque is said to
be dishonoured by non-payment when the **maker of the note, acceptor of the bill or drawee of the
cheque makes default in payment** upon being duly required to pay the same. Section 76 adds that
where presentment for payment is unnecessary, the instrument is dishonoured **at the due date**.

**Section 93 — by and to whom notice should be given.** When a promissory note, bill of exchange or
cheque is dishonoured by non-acceptance or non-payment, **the holder thereof, or some party
thereto who remains liable thereon, must give notice** that the instrument has been so dishonoured
**to all other parties whom the holder seeks to make severally liable thereon**, and to some one of
several parties whom he seeks to make jointly liable thereon.

**Section 94 — the mode and the time.** Notice of dishonour may be given to a **duly authorised
agent** of the person to whom it is required to be given, or, where he has died, to his legal
representative, or, where he has been declared an insolvent, to his assignee. It may be **oral or
written**; it may, if written, be sent by post; and it may be in any form, but it **must inform
the party to whom it is given, either in express terms or by reasonable intendment, that the
instrument has been dishonoured and in what way, and that he will be held liable thereon**. It must
be given **within a reasonable time after the dishonour, at the place of business or (if he has no
place of business) at the residence** of the party for whom it is intended.

The Act's own working rule on time: where the party giving and the party receiving the notice
carry on business or live in **different places**, the notice is given in due time if it is
**despatched by the post on the day next after the day of dishonour**. Where they are in the
**same place**, it must be despatched in time to reach its destination **on the day next after the
day of dishonour**.

**Section 95 — party receiving must transmit.** Any party receiving notice of dishonour must, in
order to render any prior party liable to himself, give notice of dishonour to such party **within
a reasonable time**, unless such party otherwise receives due notice as provided by s. 93.

**Section 96 — agent for presentment.** When the instrument is deposited with an agent for
presentment, the agent is entitled to the **same time to give notice** to his principal as if he
were the holder, and the principal is entitled to a **further like period** to give notice to the
parties liable.

**Section 97 — party to whom notice is given as an agent.** When the party to whom notice of
dishonour is despatched is **dead**, but the party despatching the notice is ignorant of his death,
the notice is **sufficient**.

**Section 98 — when notice of dishonour is unnecessary.** Eight situations, and this is the list
the examiner asks for:

1. When it is **dispensed with by the party entitled thereto**;
2. In order to charge the **drawer**, when he has **countermanded payment** (stopped the cheque);
3. When the party charged **could not suffer damage** for want of notice;
4. When the party entitled to notice **cannot after due search be found**, or the party bound to
   give notice is, for any other reason, **unable without any fault of his own** to give it;
5. In order to charge the **drawers**, when the acceptor is also a drawer;
6. When the promissory note is **not negotiable**;
7. When the party entitled to notice, knowing the facts, **promises unconditionally to pay** the
   amount due on the instrument;
8. When the instrument was made or accepted for the **accommodation** of the party entitled to
   notice.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Notice of dishonour is <strong>not</strong> a demand for payment and it is <strong>not</strong>
    the same as the notice under s. 138. Notice of dishonour under s. 93 preserves the holder's
    <em>civil</em> right against the drawer and the endorsers, may be
    <strong>oral</strong>, and must go out within a reasonable time — roughly, by the next day.
    The s. 138 notice is a <strong>written demand</strong> that is a precondition to a
    <strong>criminal</strong> complaint, must be given within <strong>30 days</strong> of the
    information of dishonour, and must demand payment. A holder who gives one and forgets the
    other loses half his remedy.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 14. Noting and protest — sections 99 to 104A

Notice of dishonour tells the other parties what has happened. **Noting and protest create the
evidence** that it happened. They matter because a holder who sues years later will otherwise have
to prove presentment and refusal by oral testimony.

**Section 99 — noting.** When a promissory note or bill of exchange has been dishonoured by
non-acceptance or non-payment, the holder may **cause such dishonour to be noted by a notary
public upon the instrument, or upon a paper attached thereto, or partly upon each**. The note must
be made **within a reasonable time after dishonour**, and must specify:

- the **date of dishonour**;
- the **reason, if any, assigned** for the dishonour;
- if the instrument has not been expressly dishonoured, the **reason why the holder treats it as
  dishonoured**; and
- the **notary's charges**.

**Section 100 — protest.** When a promissory note or bill of exchange has been dishonoured by
non-acceptance or non-payment, the holder may, **within a reasonable time**, cause such dishonour
to be **noted and certified by a notary public**. Such certificate is called a **protest**. The
section adds **protest for better security**: when the acceptor of a bill has **become insolvent,
or his credit has been publicly impeached, before the maturity of the bill**, the holder may,
within a reasonable time, cause a notary public to demand better security of the acceptor, and on
its being refused may, within a reasonable time, cause such facts to be noted and certified —
which is called **protest for better security**.

**Section 101 — the contents of a protest.** A protest must contain the **instrument itself, or a
literal transcript** of it and of everything written or printed thereon; the **name of the person
for whom and against whom** it is protested; a statement that **payment or acceptance, or better
security, has been demanded** by the notary from the person concerned, together with his answer or
the fact that he could not be found; the **place and time of dishonour** or of refusal; the
**notary's subscription**; and, in the case of an acceptance or a payment for honour, the name of
the person for whose honour, and the manner in which, it was made.

**Section 102 — notice of protest.** When a promissory note or bill of exchange is required by law
to be protested, **notice of protest must be given instead of notice of dishonour**, in the same
manner and subject to the same conditions.

**Section 103 — protest of an inland bill or note.** When a promissory note or bill of exchange is
required to be protested, the protest may be made at any time **after the dishonour**, and the
holder may protest an **inland** bill or note **at his option**.

**Section 104 — protest of a foreign bill.** All **foreign** bills of exchange must be protested
for dishonour when such protest is **required by the law of the place where they are drawn**.

**Section 104A — when noting is equivalent to protest.** For the purposes of the Act, where a bill
or note is required to be protested within a specified time or before some further proceeding is
taken, it is **sufficient that the bill has been noted for protest before the expiration of the
specified time or the taking of the proceeding**; and the **formal protest may be extended
thereafter at any time during the notary's business hours**. So the noting is what has to be done
in time; the formal protest can be written up later and it will **date back to the noting**.

| | **Noting — s. 99** | **Protest — s. 100** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | A **minute** by a notary on the instrument or on an attached paper | A **formal certificate** issued by the notary |
| Form | Brief: date, reason, notary's charges | Full contents prescribed by s. 101 |
| Purpose | To record the dishonour at once, cheaply | To furnish **formal evidence** of dishonour |
| Effect in evidence | Preliminary; usually extended into a protest | Section 119 lets the Court **presume the dishonour** on proof of the protest |
| Compulsory? | Never compulsory | Compulsory for a **foreign bill** if the law of the place of drawing requires it (s. 104); optional for inland instruments (s. 103) |
| Timing | Within a **reasonable time** after dishonour | May be extended later; the **noting** must be in time (s. 104A) |

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Noting and protest are <strong>optional for an inland instrument</strong> and
    <strong>compulsory for a foreign bill</strong> where the law of the place of drawing says so.
    The practical reward for doing it anyway is s. 119: on proof of the protest the Court
    <strong>presumes the fact of dishonour</strong>, so the holder does not have to prove
    presentment and refusal all over again. The same procedure supports
    <strong>noting charges</strong> in the accounting entries — see Foundation Paper 1,
    <a href="/foundation/accounting/bills-of-exchange-and-promissory-notes/">Ch 6 §9</a>, where
    those charges are debited to the party in default.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 105 — "reasonable time".** In determining what is a reasonable time for presentment for
acceptance or payment, for giving notice of dishonour and for noting, regard shall be had to the
**nature of the instrument and the usual course of dealing** with respect to similar instruments,
and, in calculating such time, **public holidays shall be excluded**.

## 15. Liability of the parties — sections 30 to 32 and 35 to 45

**Who is liable, and in what order.** The Act sorts every signature into one of two boxes.
**Section 37** says the **maker of a note or cheque, the drawer of a bill until acceptance, and
the acceptor** are, in the absence of a contract to the contrary, liable as **principal debtors**,
and every other party is liable as a **surety** for them. **Section 38** adds that as between the
parties so liable as sureties, **each prior party is a principal in respect of each subsequent
party**. So liability runs backwards along the chain, and the last endorsee may pursue anybody
before him.

**Section 30 — liability of the drawer.** The drawer of a bill of exchange or cheque is bound, in
case of **dishonour by the drawee or acceptor**, to **compensate the holder**, provided due notice
of dishonour has been given to, or received by, the drawer. Two conditions in one sentence: there
must be a dishonour, and there must be **due notice** — unless notice is excused under s. 98.

**Section 31 — liability of the drawee of a cheque.** The drawee of a cheque **having sufficient
funds of the drawer in his hands, properly applicable to the payment of such cheque**, must pay
the cheque when duly required to do so, and in default **must compensate the drawer** for any loss
or damage caused by such default.

Read the beneficiary carefully. Section 31 makes the bank answerable **to its customer, the
drawer** — for the injury to his credit — and **not to the payee**. The payee has no contract with
the bank and cannot sue it on the cheque; his remedy is against the drawer under s. 30 and, if the
facts fit, under s. 138.

**Section 32 — liability of the maker of a note and the acceptor of a bill.** In the absence of a
contract to the contrary, the **maker of a promissory note** and the **acceptor before maturity of
a bill of exchange** are bound to pay the amount **at maturity according to the apparent tenor** of
the note or the acceptance; and the **acceptor of a bill at or after maturity** is bound to pay the
amount **to the holder on demand**. In default, the maker or acceptor must compensate any party to
the instrument for the loss or damage caused.

**Section 35 — liability of the endorser.** In the absence of a contract to the contrary, whoever
**endorses and delivers** a negotiable instrument **before maturity**, without expressly excluding
or making conditional his own liability, is bound thereby to **every subsequent holder**, in case
of dishonour by the drawee, acceptor or maker, to compensate that holder for the loss or damage
caused — **provided due notice of dishonour has been given to, or received by, that endorser**.

**Section 36 — liability of prior parties to a holder in due course.** Every prior party to a
negotiable instrument is **liable thereon to a holder in due course until the instrument is duly
satisfied**.

**Sections 26 to 29 — who can be a party.** Every person **capable of contracting** may bind
himself and be bound by a negotiable instrument. A **minor** may **draw, endorse, deliver and
negotiate** an instrument so as to bind **all parties except himself** — his own signature is void
against him but the paper stays alive for everybody else. An **agent** who signs must expressly
state that he signs for his principal, or he is **personally liable** (s. 28); and a **legal
representative** who signs is personally liable unless he expressly limits his liability to the
extent of the assets received (s. 29).

**Section 41 — an acceptor is bound though the endorsement is forged.** An acceptor of a bill of
exchange **already endorsed** is **not relieved from liability** by reason that such endorsement
is forged, if he knew or had reason to believe the endorsement to be forged when he accepted the
bill.

**Sections 43 to 45 — consideration.** Section 43 (want of consideration) is set out in §6. Where
the failure is **partial**, the Act splits it:

- **Section 44 — partial absence or failure of money consideration.** When the consideration for
  which a person signed a note, bill or cheque **consisted of money, and was originally absent in
  part or has subsequently failed in part**, the sum which a holder standing in immediate relation
  with such signer is entitled to receive from him is **proportionally reduced**. The reduction
  operates only **between immediate parties**; a holder in due course recovers the whole.
- **Section 45 — partial failure of consideration not consisting of money.** Where the
  consideration was **not money** and has failed in part, and the failure **can be quantified in
  money**, the same proportional reduction applies as between immediate parties.

**Section 45A — the holder's right to a duplicate of a lost bill.** Where a bill of exchange has
been **lost before it is overdue**, the person who was the holder of it may apply to the drawer to
give him **another bill of the same tenor**, giving security to the drawer, if required, to
indemnify him against all persons whatever in case the lost bill is found again. If the drawer
refuses, **he may be compelled** to do so.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Three liability traps. (i) <strong>The drawee of a bill owes nobody anything until he
    accepts</strong> — before acceptance the holder's claim on dishonour lies against the
    <strong>drawer</strong> under s. 30. (ii) <strong>Section 31 protects the drawer, not the
    payee</strong> — a bank that wrongly returns a good cheque compensates its customer for the
    damage to his credit, and the payee has no action against the bank at all. (iii) A
    <strong>minor's</strong> signature binds every other party but not the minor: the instrument
    is not void, it simply has one signature that cannot be enforced.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 16. Discharge from liability — sections 82 to 90

Two different things are being discharged in this part, and the difference decides several
questions. **Discharge of a PARTY** frees that one person; everybody else stays liable and the
instrument lives on. **Discharge of the INSTRUMENT** kills the paper, so no party remains liable
on it at all. An instrument is discharged only by **payment in due course** by the party primarily
liable, by the primary debtor becoming the **holder in his own right at or after maturity**, by an
**express renunciation** by the holder, or by **cancellation**.

**Section 82 — how a party is discharged.** The maker, acceptor or endorser of a negotiable
instrument is discharged from liability thereon:

- **82(a) — by cancellation.** To a holder thereof who **cancels such acceptor's or endorser's name
  with intent to discharge him**, and to all parties claiming under such holder.
- **82(b) — by release.** To a holder thereof who **otherwise discharges** such maker, acceptor or
  endorser, and to all parties deriving title under such holder **after notice of such discharge**.
- **82(c) — by payment.** To **all parties** thereto, if the instrument is **payable to bearer or
  has been endorsed in blank**, and such maker, acceptor or endorser makes **payment in due
  course** of the amount due thereon.

**Section 83 — by allowing the drawee more than 48 hours to accept.** If the holder of a bill of
exchange allows the drawee **more than forty-eight hours, exclusive of public holidays**, to
consider whether he will accept the same, **all previous parties not consenting** to such
allowance are thereby **discharged from liability** to such holder.

**Section 84 — by a cheque not being presented in reasonable time.** Where a cheque is not
presented for payment **within a reasonable time of its issue**, and the drawer **suffers actual
damage** through the delay — because the bank fails in the meantime, for instance — he is
**discharged to the extent of such damage**. That is, discharged from liability to the extent to
which the drawer is a **creditor of the bank to a larger amount than he would have been** if the
cheque had been paid. The holder of the cheque, as against any other party, stands in the same
position as if payment had been made and he had received notice of dishonour.

**Section 86 — by a qualified acceptance.** If the holder of a bill of exchange **acquiesces in a
qualified acceptance**, or in one limited to part of the sum mentioned in the bill, or limited in
time or place, or by two or more drawees but not by all, **all previous parties whose consent is
not obtained** to such acceptance are **discharged** as against the holder and those claiming
under him, unless on notice given by the holder they assent to it.

**Section 87 — the effect of a material alteration.** **Any material alteration of a negotiable
instrument renders the same void as against anyone who is a party thereto at the time of making
such alteration and does not consent thereto**, unless it was made in order to **carry out the
common intention of the original parties**. And any such alteration, **if made by an endorsee**,
**discharges his endorser** from all liability to him in respect of the consideration thereof.

What counts as **material** — the list is worth memorising, because a one-mark question is made by
picking one item off it:

- the **date** of the instrument;
- the **sum payable**;
- the **time of payment**;
- the **place of payment**, or the addition of a place where none was stated;
- the **rate of interest**;
- the **addition of a new party**, or the tearing off of a signature;
- **crossing a cheque**, or altering a crossing, otherwise than as s. 125 permits.

What is **not** material: **filling in the blanks** of an inchoate instrument under s. 20;
converting a **blank endorsement into a full one** under s. 49; **crossing an uncrossed cheque**,
or making a general crossing special, in the ways s. 125 expressly allows; and **any alteration
made before the instrument is issued**, with the consent of the parties.

**Section 88 — an acceptor or endorser is bound notwithstanding a previous alteration.** An
acceptor or endorser of a negotiable instrument is bound by his acceptance or endorsement
**notwithstanding any previous alteration** of the instrument. He is taken to have accepted or
endorsed the paper as he found it.

**Section 89 — payment where the alteration is not apparent.** Where a promissory note, bill of
exchange or cheque has been **materially altered but does not appear to have been so altered**, or
where a cheque is presented for payment which **does not at the time of presentation appear to be
crossed or to have had a crossing obliterated**, payment thereof by a person or banker liable to
pay, and paying the same **according to the apparent tenor thereof at the time of payment and
otherwise in due course**, shall **discharge** that person or banker. The banker is protected
because he could not see the alteration; if the alteration is visible, he pays at his peril.

**Section 90 — extinguishment of rights of action on a bill in the acceptor's hands.** If a bill of
exchange which has been **negotiated is, at or after maturity, held by the acceptor in his own
right**, all rights of action thereon are **extinguished**. The instrument itself is dead, because
the person primarily liable and the person entitled to be paid are now the same person.

**The paying banker's protections, gathered.** Foundation questions cross-refer them constantly:

| Section | Protects | Condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **s. 85(1)** | Paying banker, order cheque | Endorsement **regular on its face** and payment **in due course** |
| **s. 85(2)** | Paying banker, bearer cheque | Payment **in due course**; a cheque originally bearer stays bearer whatever endorsements it carries |
| **s. 85A** | Paying bank on its own **draft** | Payment in due course |
| **s. 89** | Paying banker or party liable | Alteration or obliterated crossing **not apparent**, payment according to the apparent tenor and otherwise in due course |
| **s. 128** | Paying banker, **crossed** cheque | Payment **in due course** — and the drawer too is protected once the cheque has reached the payee |
| **s. 131** | **Collecting** banker | Received payment **for a customer**, in **good faith and without negligence** |

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    "Once bearer, always bearer." Section 85(2) means a cheque <strong>originally drawn payable to
    bearer</strong> remains payable to bearer, and the paying banker is discharged by paying it in
    due course, <strong>however many endorsements — genuine or forged — appear on the
    back</strong>. Students apply this to an order cheque that has merely been endorsed in blank,
    which is a different animal: there the banker must still check that the endorsements are
    regular under s. 85(1).
  </p>
</Callout>

## 17. Dishonour of a cheque for insufficiency of funds — section 138

Sections 138 to 142 were inserted in 1988 to give a bounced cheque teeth. Everything else in this
Act is civil; this small group is **criminal**, and it exists to make people treat a cheque as
good as cash.

**What the section does, in one sentence.** Where a person draws a cheque on his own account to
discharge a **legally enforceable debt or other liability**, and the bank returns it unpaid
because the **funds in the account are insufficient**, or because the amount exceeds the
**arrangement** agreed with the bank, the drawer is **deemed to have committed an offence**
punishable with imprisonment up to **two years**, or with a fine which may extend to **twice the
amount of the cheque**, or with both.

**The ingredients the prosecution must show.** Options in a Foundation paper are written by
deleting one of these, so learn them as a checklist:

1. The cheque was drawn by the accused **on his own account** with a banker.
2. It was drawn **for the discharge, in whole or in part, of a legally enforceable debt or other
   liability**. A cheque given as a **gift**, or for a **time-barred debt**, or as a pure security
   for a liability that never crystallised, falls outside the section.
3. The cheque was **presented to the bank within its period of validity** — in practice, within
   **three months** of the date it bears.
4. It was **returned unpaid** for **insufficiency of funds** or because the amount exceeds the
   arrangement made with the bank.
5. The three procedural conditions in the provisos, below, were all satisfied.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The words "or other liability" do a lot of work. The section is not confined to a loan: rent,
    a price, a contractual damages figure and a partly-performed obligation all qualify, so long
    as the liability is <strong>legally enforceable on the date of the cheque</strong>. And
    "insufficiency of funds" has been read to cover the everyday return reasons that amount to the
    same thing — <em>account closed</em>, <em>payment stopped</em> where funds were short anyway,
    and <em>exceeds arrangement</em>. It does not cover a return for a
    <strong>technical defect</strong> such as a signature mismatch or a date error, which is a
    different matter altogether.
  </p>
</Callout>

**The three provisos — the clock the holder must keep.** Section 138 does not bite unless all
three are met, and each one is a deadline:

- **Proviso (a) — presentment.** The cheque must be presented to the bank **within six months of
  the date on which it is drawn, or within the period of its validity, whichever is earlier**.
  Since Reserve Bank of India directions cut the validity of a cheque to **three months**, the
  operative limit today is **three months from the date the cheque bears**, and that is the figure
  to use in a problem.
- **Proviso (b) — the demand notice.** The payee or holder in due course must make a **demand for
  payment of the cheque amount** by giving **notice in writing** to the drawer **within thirty
  days** of receiving information from the bank that the cheque has been returned unpaid.
- **Proviso (c) — the drawer's chance to pay.** The drawer must **fail to make the payment within
  fifteen days** of receiving that notice. Only when those fifteen days pass without payment is
  the offence complete.

<div class="formula">Cheque presented inside 3 months → bank returns it → payee has 30 DAYS from receipt of the dishonour information to send a written demand → drawer has 15 DAYS from receipt of that notice to pay → the offence is complete on the expiry of the 15th day → complaint within ONE MONTH of that date, s. 142(b).</div>

Read the three deadlines together and the design is obvious: the law gives a careless drawer two
chances to put it right and only then treats him as an offender. A payee who lets any one of the
three periods slip loses the criminal remedy entirely — though his **civil** suit for the money
survives, because s. 138 adds a remedy and takes none away.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The three periods measure from three different events, and swapping them is the standard trap.
    <strong>Three months runs from the DATE ON THE CHEQUE</strong>, not from delivery.
    <strong>Thirty days runs from the payee RECEIVING the bank's information</strong> of dishonour,
    not from the date of the return memo and not from the date of presentment.
    <strong>Fifteen days runs from the drawer RECEIVING the notice</strong>, not from the date the
    payee posted it. Every one of the three is a receipt-based or instrument-based date, never a
    despatch date.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Re-presentment.** A cheque may be presented again within its validity period, and a fresh
dishonour restarts the clock — but the payee gets **only one cause of action**, and once he has
issued a demand notice and let the fifteen days run out, that is the cause of action he must sue
on.

## 18. The section 138 procedure and sections 139 to 142

**Section 139 — the presumption in favour of the holder.** The Court **shall presume, unless the
contrary is proved**, that the holder of the cheque received it **for the discharge, in whole or
in part, of a debt or other liability**. So the complainant does not begin by proving the debt.
The presumption is **rebuttable**, and the burden on the drawer is the civil standard — he must
make his version probable, not prove it beyond doubt.

**Section 140 — a defence that is shut out.** It is **not a defence** that the drawer had **no
reason to believe, when he issued the cheque, that it might be dishonoured** on presentment. Good
faith about the state of the account is irrelevant; on that point the offence is one of strict
liability.

**Section 141 — offences by companies.** Where the offence is committed by a **company**, both the
company and **every person who, at the time the offence was committed, was in charge of and
responsible to the company for the conduct of its business** are deemed guilty. Two safety valves:

- Such a person is **not liable** if he proves the offence was committed **without his knowledge**,
  or that he **exercised all due diligence** to prevent it.
- Where the offence is proved to have been committed **with the consent or connivance of, or is
  attributable to any neglect on the part of**, any director, manager, secretary or other officer,
  that officer is **also** deemed guilty.

"Company" here includes a **firm or other association of individuals**, and "director" in relation
to a firm means a **partner**.

**Section 142 — how a case is started.** Three rules and a saving:

- **142(1)(a)** — no Court takes cognizance except upon a **complaint in writing** made by the
  **payee or the holder in due course**.
- **142(1)(b)** — the complaint must be made **within one month of the date on which the cause of
  action arises** under proviso (c) to s. 138. The **proviso** lets a Court take cognizance after
  that month if the complainant **satisfies the Court that he had sufficient cause** for not
  making it in time.
- **142(1)(c)** — no Court inferior to that of a **Metropolitan Magistrate or a Judicial
  Magistrate of the first class** shall try the offence.
- **142(2)** fixes **territorial jurisdiction** by where the cheque was banked: where the cheque is
  delivered for collection through an account, the Court within whose local jurisdiction the
  **payee's bank branch** is situated; and where it is presented for payment over the counter, the
  Court where the **drawee bank branch on which it was drawn** is situated.

**Two 2018 additions, noted for completeness.** **Section 143A** lets the trial Court direct the
drawer to pay the complainant **interim compensation not exceeding twenty per cent of the cheque
amount** while the case is pending. **Section 148** lets an Appellate Court, where a convicted
drawer appeals, direct him to deposit a **minimum of twenty per cent** of the fine or compensation
awarded by the trial Court. Both are recent, and their detail is beyond ordinary Foundation depth;
know that they exist and what the twenty per cent figure attaches to.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Two more points worth a line in an answer. <strong>Section 147</strong> makes every offence
    under this Act <strong>compoundable</strong>, so the parties may settle and the case ends —
    which is how the great majority of cheque cases actually finish. And s. 138 is
    <strong>in addition to</strong>, never in substitution for, the ordinary civil suit for the
    money: a payee who misses the thirty-day notice window has lost the prosecution, not the debt.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 19. Application vignettes

Three vignettes are already worked into the sections above — an inchoate cheque in §6, four
due-date computations in §11, and two interest calculations in §12. Two more follow: a complete
s. 138 timeline, and a mixed problem on crossings, endorsements and the paying banker.

<Callout kind="example" label="Vignette 4 — a complete section 138 timeline (hard)">
  <p>
    Padmini Fabrics supplies cloth worth <strong>₹ 7,80,000</strong> to Ravikiran Garments.
    Ravikiran gives Padmini a cheque for that sum, drawn on his own current account with Vindhya
    Bank and <strong>dated 8 June 2026</strong>. Padmini banks it on <strong>27 July 2026</strong>.
    The cheque is returned marked "funds insufficient", and Padmini's bank hands her the return
    memo on <strong>29 July 2026</strong>. She posts a written demand for the cheque amount, and it
    is <strong>received by Ravikiran on 3 August 2026</strong>. Ravikiran pays nothing. Work out
    every date, and say what Padmini must do and by when.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 1 — proviso (a): was the cheque presented in time?</div>
    A cheque is valid for <strong>three months</strong> from the date it bears, so this one could
    be presented up to <strong>8 September 2026</strong>. Presentment on 27 July 2026 is well
    inside that. Proviso (a) is satisfied. Had she banked it on, say, 20 September, s. 138 would
    have been unavailable however clear the dishonour.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 2 — proviso (b): the thirty-day demand notice</div>
    The thirty days run from the day Padmini <strong>received the information</strong> of dishonour,
    which is <strong>29 July 2026</strong>, and that day is excluded. Thirty days from and
    excluding 29 July ends on <strong>28 August 2026</strong>. Her written demand must be given
    inside that window. It was, so proviso (b) is satisfied. Note the two dates that do
    <em>not</em> start this clock: the date of presentment (27 July) and the date the bank wrote
    the memo.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 3 — proviso (c): the drawer's fifteen days</div>
    Ravikiran <strong>received</strong> the notice on <strong>3 August 2026</strong>, and that day
    is excluded. He therefore has until the end of <strong>18 August 2026</strong> to pay the
    cheque amount. He does not. The offence is complete on the expiry of the fifteenth day, so the
    <strong>cause of action arises on 19 August 2026</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 4 — s. 142(1)(b): the complaint</div>
    The complaint must be in <strong>writing</strong>, made by the <strong>payee</strong>, within
    <strong>one month of the date the cause of action arose</strong>. One month from and excluding
    19 August 2026 ends on <strong>19 September 2026</strong>. Padmini must file with a
    Metropolitan Magistrate or a Judicial Magistrate of the first class (s. 142(1)(c)), in the
    territory of her own collecting branch (s. 142(2)). If she is late she is not automatically
    out: the proviso to s. 142(1)(b) lets the Court condone the delay on sufficient cause.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 5 — exposure, and the civil claim</div>
    On conviction Ravikiran faces imprisonment up to <strong>two years</strong>, or a fine up to
    <strong>twice the cheque amount — ₹ 15,60,000</strong>, or both, and the Court may order
    interim compensation of up to twenty per cent of ₹ 7,80,000 under s. 143A while the case runs.
    Independently of all this, Padmini keeps her <strong>civil suit</strong> for ₹ 7,80,000 with
    interest, and s. 139 presumes the cheque was given for a debt, so Ravikiran must displace that
    presumption before he can argue there was nothing owing.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Vignette 5 — a not-negotiable crossing, a forged endorsement, and three innocent parties (hard)">
  <p>
    Neelima draws a cheque for <strong>₹ 2,40,000</strong> on Sahyadri Bank, payable to
    "<strong>Ashirwad Traders or order</strong>", and crosses it with two parallel lines and the
    words "<strong>not negotiable</strong>". Before it reaches Ashirwad, a clerk steals the cheque,
    forges Ashirwad's endorsement and passes it to Chetan, a stationer, in payment of a genuine
    bill. Chetan takes it in good faith and for full value, knowing nothing. Chetan pays it into
    his own bank, which collects it in good faith and without negligence, and Sahyadri Bank pays
    it in due course. Ashirwad discovers the theft and claims the ₹ 2,40,000. Who bears the loss?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 1 — what did the forged endorsement pass?</div>
    Nothing. A <strong>forged signature is a nullity</strong>: the clerk had neither possession
    with entitlement nor any title, so he was not a holder at all and could pass nothing on. Had
    the cheque been an ordinary crossed cheque without the added words, Chetan might have argued
    he was a holder in due course — but on an <em>order</em> cheque he could not, because the
    chain of endorsements is broken by the forgery.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 2 — the effect of "not negotiable", s. 130</div>
    The added words settle it beyond argument. A person taking a cheque crossed "not negotiable"
    <strong>cannot have, and cannot give, a better title than the person from whom he took it
    had</strong>. The clerk had no title, so <strong>Chetan has none</strong>, however honest he
    is. The words do not stop the cheque being transferred; they strip the transfer of its power
    to cure a defect.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 3 — the paying banker, Sahyadri</div>
    <strong>Protected.</strong> It paid a crossed cheque to a banker, so s. 126 was observed and
    <strong>s. 128</strong> puts it in the same position as if the amount had been paid to the
    true owner. On the endorsement itself, <strong>s. 85(1)</strong> protects a paying banker who
    pays an order cheque in due course where the endorsement is <strong>regular on its face</strong>
    — a skilful forgery is regular on its face, and the banker is not required to detect it.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 4 — the collecting banker, Chetan's bank</div>
    <strong>Protected by s. 131</strong>, because it received payment <strong>for a customer</strong>,
    in <strong>good faith and without negligence</strong>. Negligence is the whole battleground in
    practice: had the cheque carried an <strong>"account payee"</strong> crossing naming Ashirwad
    and the bank had still collected it for Chetan, that alone would ordinarily be negligence, the
    s. 131 shield would fall away, and the bank would answer to the true owner.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Conclusion — the loss lands on Chetan</div>
    Ashirwad remains the <strong>true owner</strong> and recovers <strong>₹ 2,40,000</strong> from
    Chetan, who received money that was never his to receive. Chetan's only remedy is a personal
    action against the clerk. The lesson the section is built to teach: an
    <strong>ordinary crossing protects the drawer's money by routing it through a bank; the words
    "not negotiable" protect the true OWNER by refusing to launder a bad title</strong>, and they
    do so at the cost of the innocent transferee.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

- **Giving a cheque three days of grace.** A cheque is payable **on demand**, so s. 22 gives it
  **no grace at all**. Grace belongs to time notes and time bills only.
- **Adding grace after the holiday test.** The order is fixed: find the day the instrument is
  *expressed* to be payable, **add three days**, and only then test that date under s. 25.
- **Moving a holiday maturity forward.** Section 25 moves it **back** to the **next preceding**
  business day. Forward movement is the emergency-holiday practice, not the section.
- **Converting months into thirty-day blocks.** Section 23 uses the **corresponding day** of the
  later month, and the **last day of the month** where there is no corresponding day.
- **Counting the trigger day.** Section 24 **excludes** the day of the date, of the sight, or of
  the event. Day 1 is the day after.
- **Running "after sight" from the date of the bill.** On a **bill** it runs from
  **acceptance** (s. 21); on a **promissory note**, from **presentment for sight**.
- **Treating "not negotiable" as "not transferable".** Section 130 leaves the cheque fully
  transferable and only stops a transferee getting a **better title** than his transferor had.
  Only words such as "Pay X only" on the face destroy transferability.
- **Thinking "account payee" is in the Act.** It is not. It is banking practice, and it binds the
  **collecting** banker through the negligence limb of s. 131.
- **Aiming the crossings at the wrong banker.** General and special crossings bind the **paying**
  banker (ss. 126, 129); "not negotiable" binds the **transferee's title** (s. 130); "account
  payee" binds the **collecting** banker in practice.
- **Assuming a holder is a holder in due course.** Section 9 needs **consideration**, acquisition
  **before maturity**, and **no sufficient cause to believe in a defect**. A donee and a person
  who takes an overdue instrument both fail.
- **Forgetting s. 53.** A holder who takes from a holder in due course gets that holder's clean
  title **even if he himself knew of the original fraud**.
- **Reading the presumptions as conclusive.** Every limb of s. 118 opens with "until the contrary
  is proved", and the **proviso to s. 118(g)** shifts the burden back onto the holder once fraud,
  an offence or an unlawful consideration at the origin is shown.
- **Suing the drawee of a bill before acceptance.** He owes nobody anything until he signs. The
  holder's claim on dishonour lies against the **drawer** under s. 30.
- **Suing the bank as payee of a dishonoured cheque.** Section 31 makes the bank answerable to its
  **customer, the drawer**, for the damage to his credit — never to the payee.
- **Confusing a restrictive endorsement with a *sans recours* endorsement.** The first kills the
  **endorsee's** right to negotiate further (s. 50); the second kills the **endorser's own**
  liability (s. 52) and leaves negotiability untouched.
- **Treating a partial endorsement as partly effective.** Section 56 makes it **invalid for
  negotiation altogether**. The only lawful route is to note an actual **part payment** and
  negotiate the **residue**.
- **Mixing up notice of dishonour with the s. 138 notice.** Notice of dishonour (s. 93) may be
  **oral**, goes out within a reasonable time, and preserves a **civil** claim against the drawer
  and endorsers. The s. 138 notice must be **written**, must **demand payment**, must go within
  **thirty days of receiving the information of dishonour**, and unlocks a **criminal** complaint.
- **Starting the s. 138 clocks from the wrong events.** Three months from the **date on the
  cheque**; thirty days from the payee **receiving** the bank's information; fifteen days from the
  drawer **receiving** the notice. Never from a date of despatch.
- **Filing the s. 138 complaint from the wrong date.** The one month in s. 142(1)(b) runs from the
  date the **cause of action arises** — the day after the drawer's fifteen days expire — not from
  the dishonour and not from the notice.
- **Assuming a s. 138 case dies with a late complaint.** The proviso to s. 142(1)(b) lets the
  Court condone the delay on sufficient cause, and s. 147 makes the offence **compoundable**.
- **Calling every alteration material.** Filling the blanks of an inchoate instrument (s. 20),
  converting a blank endorsement into a full one (s. 49) and crossing a cheque in the ways s. 125
  allows are **not** material alterations.
- **Forgetting that a minor's instrument still works.** A minor may draw, endorse, deliver and
  negotiate so as to bind **every party except himself**.
- **Reading s. 79 and s. 80 as one rule.** Rate specified: **that rate, from the date of the
  instrument**. No rate specified: **18% p.a., from the date the money fell due**.
- **Confusing the drawee in case of need with the acceptor for honour.** The first is named by the
  drawer **on the face of the bill in advance**; the second is a volunteer who steps in **after a
  noting or protest**.
- **Thinking noting and protest are compulsory.** They are **optional for inland** instruments
  (s. 103) and required for a **foreign** bill only where the law of the place of drawing says so
  (s. 104). The reward for doing them anyway is the s. 119 presumption of dishonour.

## One-page revision summary

**The gate.** s. 13(1) — a negotiable instrument means a **promissory note, bill of exchange or
cheque payable to order or to bearer**. Five characteristics: freely transferable; a holder in due
course takes free of defects; the holder sues in his own name; consideration is presumed; payable
to order or bearer. **Transferability is not negotiability** — only negotiation can give a better
title than the transferor had.

**Presumptions.** s. 118 — consideration (a), date (b), time of acceptance (c), time of transfer
(d), order of endorsements (e), stamp on a lost instrument (f), holder in due course (g); all
**rebuttable**. The **proviso to 118(g)** shifts the burden to the holder where the instrument was
obtained by an offence, fraud or an unlawful consideration. s. 119 — proof of **protest** raises a
presumption of **dishonour**. s. 139 — in a s. 138 case, the cheque is presumed to have been given
for a debt or other liability.

**The three instruments.** s. 4 note — unconditional **promise**, written by the debtor, **two**
parties, never accepted. s. 5 bill — unconditional **order**, written by the creditor, **three**
parties, accepted by the drawee. s. 6 cheque — a bill **drawn on a banker** and payable **on
demand**, including a cheque in electronic form and a truncated cheque; **no acceptance, no grace,
no stamp**, and it can be **crossed**. Cheque validity by RBI practice: **three months**.

**Parties.** Maker, drawer, drawee, **acceptor** (s. 7), payee, endorser, endorsee. **Drawee in
case of need** — named on the face by the drawer in advance; the bill is not dishonoured until he
too refuses. **Acceptor for honour** (s. 108) — a volunteer who accepts *supra protest* after a
noting or protest. **Payment for honour** (s. 113) — payment *supra protest* after protest for
non-payment, on a declaration before a notary.

**Holder and holder in due course.** s. 8 — entitled in his **own name** to possession and to
recover the amount. s. 9 — **for consideration**, **before maturity**, **without sufficient cause
to believe in any defect**. s. 10 payment in due course — **apparent tenor**, **good faith and
without negligence**, to the **person in possession**, no reasonable ground for believing him not
entitled.

**Privileges of a holder in due course.** s. 20 inchoate instrument (recover the whole amount
within the stamp); s. 36 every prior party liable until satisfaction; s. 42 fictitious drawer;
s. 43 want of consideration no defence; ss. 46–47 conditional delivery no defence; **s. 53 a
holder deriving title from a holder in due course takes the clean title**; s. 58 instrument
obtained by fraud or unlawful means; ss. 120–121 estoppels against the maker, drawer and acceptor;
s. 122 estoppel against an endorser denying a prior signature.

**Classification.** s. 11 inland — drawn or made in India and payable in, or drawn on a resident
of, India. s. 12 foreign — everything else. s. 13(2) order and bearer. s. 17 **ambiguous** — the
holder elects to treat it as a note or a bill. s. 18 — **words prevail over figures**. s. 20
**inchoate** — signed, stamped, delivered blank or incomplete. s. 42 **fictitious** name.

**Negotiation.** s. 14 transfer so as to make the transferee the **holder**. s. 46 completed by
**delivery**. s. 47 bearer instrument by **delivery**; s. 48 order instrument by **endorsement and
delivery**. s. 15 endorsement; s. 16 **blank** (signature only) and **full** (direction to pay a
named person). s. 49 a holder may convert a blank endorsement into a full one **without signing**.
s. 50 effect of endorsement, and restrictive endorsements. s. 51 who may negotiate. s. 52 *sans
recours* and conditional endorsements. s. 55 a blank endorsement followed by a full one. **s. 56
a partial endorsement is invalid for negotiation**; only the residue after an actual part payment
may be negotiated. s. 59 a person taking after dishonour or maturity gets only his transferor's
rights. s. 60 negotiable until payment or satisfaction at or after maturity.

**Kinds of endorsement.** Blank; full; **restrictive** (kills the endorsee's right to negotiate);
***sans recours*** (kills the endorser's own liability); **conditional**; **partial** (invalid).
Also *sans frais* and **facultative** (a waiver, most often of notice of dishonour).

**Crossing.** s. 123 general — two parallel lines, pay only **to a banker**. s. 124 special — the
**name of a banker**, lines optional. s. 125 who may cross and when; a crossing may only be made
**more** restrictive. s. 126 effect. s. 127 double special crossing — **refuse payment** unless
the second is a collecting agent. s. 128 paying banker protected on payment in due course of a
crossed cheque. s. 129 payment out of due course — liable to the **true owner**. **s. 130 "not
negotiable" — no better title than the transferor had.** s. 131 **collecting banker** protected
where he receives payment for a customer in good faith and without negligence; s. 131A extends the
crossing rules to bank drafts. **"Account payee" is banking practice, not a section.**

**Maturity.** s. 21 "after sight" — from **acceptance** on a bill, from **presentment for sight**
on a note; "at sight" and "on presentment" mean on demand. **s. 22 — three days of grace**, on
every instrument not payable on demand, at sight or on presentment. s. 23 **months** —
corresponding day, else the last day of the month. s. 24 **days** — exclude the trigger day.
**s. 25 — a maturity falling on a public holiday moves BACK to the next preceding business day**;
Sundays are public holidays. Emergency holidays, by practice, move payment **forward**.

**Presentment, payment and interest.** s. 61 presentment for acceptance of an after-sight bill;
s. 62 presentment of an after-sight note for sight; s. 63 the drawee's **48 hours** to consider;
ss. 64–66 presentment for payment, business and banking hours, at maturity; s. 76 when presentment
is unnecessary; s. 78 payment must be made **to the holder**. **s. 79 — rate specified: that rate,
from the DATE OF THE INSTRUMENT. s. 80 — no rate specified: 18% p.a., from the DATE THE MONEY FELL
DUE.** s. 81 the payer may see the instrument before paying and have it delivered up on payment,
or an indemnity if it is lost.

**Dishonour and notice.** s. 91 dishonour by **non-acceptance**; s. 92 by **non-payment**. s. 93
who gives notice and to whom; s. 94 the mode, the contents and the timing (despatch by the next
day after dishonour); s. 95 a party receiving notice must transmit it; s. 96 agents; s. 97 death
of the addressee. **s. 98 — eight cases where notice is unnecessary**: waiver; the drawer has
countermanded payment; no damage could be suffered; the party cannot be found or the giver is
unable without fault; the acceptor is also a drawer; the note is not negotiable; an unconditional
promise to pay; and accommodation.

**Noting and protest.** s. 99 **noting** — a notary's minute of the date, the reason and his
charges. s. 100 **protest** — a formal certificate, and **protest for better security** where the
acceptor becomes insolvent or his credit is publicly impeached before maturity. s. 101 contents.
s. 102 notice of protest replaces notice of dishonour where protest is required. s. 103 inland
instruments — protest is **optional**. s. 104 **foreign bills must be protested** where the law of
the place of drawing requires it. s. 104A noting in time is enough; the protest may be extended
later. s. 105 reasonable time, **excluding public holidays**.

**Liability.** s. 37 maker, drawer until acceptance, and acceptor are **principal debtors**;
everyone else is a **surety**. s. 38 each prior party is a principal to each subsequent party.
s. 30 the **drawer** compensates the holder on dishonour, on due notice. **s. 31 the drawee bank
compensates the DRAWER**, not the payee, for wrongful dishonour where funds were sufficient.
s. 32 the **maker and the acceptor** must pay at maturity according to the apparent tenor. s. 35
the **endorser** is liable to every subsequent holder on due notice. s. 36 prior parties liable to
a holder in due course. ss. 26–29 capacity: a **minor** binds every party but himself; an agent or
legal representative who does not limit his liability is personally liable. s. 41 an acceptor is
bound though a prior endorsement is forged. ss. 44–45 **partial** failure of consideration reduces
the claim **proportionally between immediate parties only**. s. 45A duplicate of a lost bill.

**Discharge.** Discharge of a **party** frees one person; discharge of the **instrument** kills the
paper. s. 82 — **cancellation (a), release (b), payment in due course of a bearer or blank-endorsed
instrument (c)**. s. 83 allowing the drawee more than **48 hours** discharges non-consenting prior
parties. s. 84 a cheque not presented in a reasonable time discharges the drawer **to the extent
of the actual damage**. s. 86 acquiescing in a **qualified acceptance** discharges non-consenting
prior parties. **s. 87 a material alteration makes the instrument void against every party who did
not consent**, unless it carries out the common intention. Material: date, sum, time of payment,
place of payment, rate of interest, a new party, crossing otherwise than under s. 125. Not
material: filling an inchoate instrument (s. 20), a blank endorsement made full (s. 49), a
permitted crossing (s. 125). s. 88 acceptor and endorser bound notwithstanding a previous
alteration. s. 89 the payer is discharged where the alteration or obliterated crossing was **not
apparent**. s. 90 rights extinguished where the **acceptor holds the bill in his own right at or
after maturity**. Paying-banker protections: **ss. 85(1), 85(2), 85A, 89, 128**; collecting-banker
protection: **s. 131**.

**Section 138.** A cheque drawn on the drawer's own account for a **legally enforceable debt or
other liability**, returned unpaid for **insufficiency of funds or exceeding the arrangement**,
makes the drawer liable to imprisonment up to **two years**, or a fine up to **twice the cheque
amount**, or both. The three provisos: **(a)** presentment **within the cheque's validity**;
**(b)** a **written demand within 30 days** of the payee receiving the information of dishonour;
**(c)** the drawer's **failure to pay within 15 days** of receiving that notice. **s. 139**
