A cloth wholesaler in Surat agrees over the phone to supply 400 metres of shirting to a shop in
Nagpur. No paper, no stamp — a handshake and a WhatsApp message. The goods go; the money does
not. Six months later a judge is deciding, from a statute written in 1872, a rule notified in
2014 and a Supreme Court judgment from 1996, whether a contract even existed.

This chapter is about where those rules come from, who made them, and which room in the building
decides the fight. It is the shortest chapter in Paper 2 and the one students skim — do not.
Everything after it (contract, sale of goods, partnership, LLP, companies, negotiable
instruments) is you reading sections and applying them. If you cannot tell a proviso from an
explanation, or a rule from a circular, you will bleed marks in *every* later chapter.

## 1. What law is, and why business needs it

**Law** is a body of rules that the State recognises and **enforces**. Both words matter. *Rules*
— so you know in advance what you may do. *Enforced* — so behind them stands the coercive power
of the State: a decree, a fine, an attachment of property, a jail term. A moral rule ("be honest
with customers") becomes a *legal* rule only when the State will back it with force.

Business needs it for four reasons: **certainty** (the Surat wholesaler ships to a stranger 800
km away because he knows what a court will do if he is not paid); **enforcement of bargains**, so
a promise becomes a claim; **protection of the weaker side** — consumers, minority shareholders,
depositors; and **rules of the game**, so every competitor has the same permitted moves.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A one-mark favourite: <strong>law is enforceable, morality is not</strong>. Many rules are
    both, some are legal but not moral, and most moral duties carry no legal sanction. Never
    write that law and morality are the same thing.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. The sources of Indian law

Indian law comes from six places. An MCQ almost always asks which item is *not* a source, or
which one prevails.

1. **The Constitution of India** — the supreme source; everything else must survive it.
2. **Legislation (statutes)** — Acts of Parliament and of State Legislatures.
3. **Subordinate or delegated legislation** — rules, regulations, orders, bye-laws and
   notifications made by the executive under power a statute gives it.
4. **Judicial precedent** — the law declared by courts, above all the Supreme Court.
5. **Customs and usage** — long, uniform, reasonable practice recognised by the courts.
6. **Personal laws** — marriage, succession and family law, differing by community.

**Order of strength** when two conflict:

<div class="formula">Constitution &gt; Statute &gt; Delegated legislation &gt; Executive circular</div>

Precedent sits *alongside* this ladder rather than on it: a judgment **interprets** the
Constitution or a statute; it never overrides the statute it is interpreting. Parliament can undo
a judicial interpretation by amending the Act — and often does.

## 3. The Constitution — the source above the sources

In force since 26 January 1950, the Constitution is the **supreme law of the land**. It does
three jobs a commerce student should be able to name: it **creates** the institutions
(Parliament, State Legislatures, executive, courts), it **distributes** law-making power between
the Union and the States, and it **limits** all of them through the Fundamental Rights in Part
III.

The limiting job is the sharpest. Under **Article 13**, a law inconsistent with the Fundamental
Rights is **void to the extent of the inconsistency** — so an Act, however properly passed, can
be struck down by the Supreme Court or a High Court. That power is **judicial review**, and it is
why the Constitution sits at the top of the ladder rather than merely at the beginning of it.

**Article 19(1)(g)** — the right to carry on any occupation, trade or business — is the provision
a businessperson invokes most. It is subject to "reasonable restrictions … in the interests of
the general public", which is exactly why licensing and regulation are constitutional.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Students write that a court "can strike down any law it dislikes". It cannot. A statute falls
    only on constitutional grounds — want of legislative competence, or breach of a
    constitutional provision — never because the judge thinks the policy unwise.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Legislation — how a Bill becomes an Act

A **Bill** is a draft; an **Act** is law. The journey at the Union level:

1. **Introduction** in the Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha — except a **Money Bill**, which can be
   introduced only in the Lok Sabha.
2. **First reading** — introduction and Gazette publication; no debate on merits.
3. **Second reading** — the real stage: general discussion, often a reference to a Select or
   Standing Committee, then clause-by-clause consideration and amendments.
4. **Third reading** — voted on as a whole.
5. **The other House** repeats the process; deadlock on an ordinary Bill can go to a **joint
   sitting**.
6. **Assent of the President** (Article 111). He may assent, withhold assent, or return an
   ordinary Bill once for reconsideration; if passed again, assent follows.
7. **Publication in the Official Gazette** — it is now an Act with a number and year, e.g.
   *the Companies Act, 2013 (Act 18 of 2013)*.
8. **Commencement.** An Act does **not** automatically operate from the date of assent. It
   commences on the date it states or a date the Government **notifies** — and different sections
   may be notified on different dates. The Companies Act 2013 came into force in tranches.

A State Bill takes the same route through the Legislative Assembly (and Council, where one
exists) with the **Governor's** assent; some Bills are reserved for the President.

**Ordinances.** When Parliament is not in session and immediate action is needed, an **Ordinance**
may be promulgated (Article 123; Article 213 for a Governor). It has the force of an Act but is
temporary — it must be laid before the legislature and ceases six weeks after reassembly unless
approved earlier.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Two exam-sized distinctions. <strong>Bill vs Act</strong>: proposal vs law.
    <strong>Assent vs commencement</strong>: assent makes it an Act, notification brings it into
    force. "An Act operates from the date the President signs it" is a planted wrong option.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Who may make which law — the three Lists

India is federal, so before asking what a law says you sometimes ask *who could make it*.
**Article 246**, with the **Seventh Schedule**, splits subjects into three lists:

| List | Who legislates | Business examples |
|---|---|---|
| **List I — Union List** | Parliament only | Banking, insurance, stock exchanges, currency, foreign trade |
| **List II — State List** | State Legislature only | Trade and commerce within the State, agriculture, money-lending |
| **List III — Concurrent List** | Both | Contracts, transfer of property, bankruptcy and insolvency, trusts |

- **Residuary power** (Article 248): a subject in no list belongs to **Parliament**.
- **Repugnancy** (Article 254): where a Union and a State law on a **Concurrent** subject
  conflict, the **Union law prevails** and the State law is void to the extent of the repugnancy
  — unless that State law was reserved for and received the President's assent.

A law made by a legislature with no competence over the subject is **ultra vires the
Constitution** and void, however sensible its contents.

## 6. Delegated legislation — rules, regulations, notifications, circulars

Parliament cannot write every detail. It sets the policy in the Act and authorises the executive
to fill in the rest. That is **delegated (subordinate) legislation**, and four names must stay
straight:

| Instrument | Made by | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Rules** | Central or State Government, under a rule-making section | Companies (Incorporation) Rules 2014, under s. 469 of the Companies Act 2013 |
| **Regulations** | A statutory regulator, under its own Act | SEBI regulations; RBI directions |
| **Notifications** | Government, in the Official Gazette | Bringing sections into force; prescribing a monetary limit; granting an exemption |
| **Circulars / clarifications** | A department or regulator, administratively | An MCA general circular on how a form is to be filed |

Three limits define the whole topic:

- **It must stay inside the parent Act.** Delegated legislation beyond the power granted is
  **ultra vires** and void — the rule cannot outgrow its parent.
- **It cannot override the Act.** Where a rule and its section conflict, the **section prevails**.
- **A circular is the weakest instrument.** It guides officers and the public but cannot create a
  liability, take away a right, or contradict the statute.

Most parent Acts also require rules to be **laid before Parliament**, which may modify or annul
them — the legislative control that makes delegation acceptable.

### Vignette 1 — a circular that goes too far

Section 12 of a fictional Act says: *"Every dealer shall file a return within thirty days of the
close of the quarter."* The department issues a circular directing that returns filed after the
fifteenth day attract a late fee of ₹5,000. A dealer files on the twenty-second day and is
charged.

Ask three questions in order. **Is there a power?** A late fee is a liability, and a liability can
be created only by the Act or by rules made under an express power in it. **Was the right
instrument used?** No — a circular, the weakest instrument, with no rule-making section invoked.
**Is it consistent with the parent Act?** No — the section gives thirty days, the circular cuts
it to fifteen.

**Conclusion:** the levy is bad; the dealer filed within the section's thirty days. Remember the
shape of that reasoning — *source of power → correct instrument → consistency with the Act.* You
will reuse it all through the paper.

## 7. Judicial precedent — stare decisis

A judgment settles the dispute *and* declares what the law means; the second part outlives the
case. **Stare decisis** — "to stand by things decided" — is the principle that a decided point is
followed later, so like cases are decided alike.

**What binds?** Only the **ratio decidendi**: the legal principle on which the decision rests,
applied to the material facts. The rest is **obiter dicta** — persuasive, not binding.

**Who binds whom** — the most examined idea in this section:

- **Supreme Court:** by **Article 141** the law it declares is **binding on all courts within the
  territory of India** — every High Court, subordinate court and tribunal.
- **The Supreme Court is not bound by itself**; it may reconsider its own decisions, ordinarily
  through a **larger Bench**, and a larger Bench prevails over a smaller one.
- **A High Court** binds all courts and tribunals **within its own territorial jurisdiction**;
  elsewhere its decision is only **persuasive**.
- **A single judge** of a High Court is bound by a Division Bench or Full Bench of that Court.
- **Foreign judgments** (English contract cases, for instance) are persuasive only — useful,
  because much Indian commercial law was drafted from English law, but never binding.

A precedent may be **distinguished** (material facts differ), **overruled** (a higher court or
larger Bench holds it wrong in law), or **reversed** (the same case decided the other way on
appeal — a different thing altogether).

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Three slips that cost marks: saying a High Court decision binds all of India; treating
    <em>obiter dicta</em> as binding; and confusing <strong>overruling</strong> (a later case
    kills the earlier precedent) with <strong>reversal</strong> (the appellate court flips the
    same case).
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. Customs, usage and personal law

A **custom** is a practice followed so long and so uniformly in a place, trade or community that
courts enforce it as law. To be recognised it must be **ancient, continuous and uniform, certain,
reasonable, and not opposed to any statute or to public policy**. Where a statute covers the
field the statute wins — custom fills gaps, it never defeats legislation.

**Usage of trade** is the commercially important cousin: the settled practice of a particular
market, read into a contract where the parties are silent. The Contract Act and the Sale of Goods
Act both make room for it.

**Personal laws** govern marriage, divorce, adoption, guardianship, succession and maintenance
and differ by community. Foundation asks only that you *recognise personal law as a source*; no
substantive personal law is examinable in Paper 2.

## 9. The structure of the Indian judiciary

India has a **single, integrated judiciary** — one pyramid handling both Union and State laws,
both civil and criminal work, with the Supreme Court at the apex. (Contrast the United States,
which runs parallel federal and state systems; that comparison is a standard theory MCQ.)

**Supreme Court of India** — Article 124; the Chief Justice of India and other judges (sanctioned
strength currently 34). Four kinds of jurisdiction:

- **Original** — disputes between the Union and States or between States (Article 131), and writ
  petitions to enforce Fundamental Rights (Article 32).
- **Appellate** — appeals from High Courts in constitutional, civil and criminal matters
  (Articles 132–134).
- **Special leave** — Article 136 permits appeal from *any* judgment or order of *any* court or
  tribunal in India (other than a court martial). Discretionary, never a right.
- **Advisory** — the President may refer a question of law or public importance (Article 143).

**High Courts** — Article 214; one for each State, though some serve more than one State or Union
Territory (25 in all). A High Court hears appeals from the district judiciary and many tribunals,
exercises **writ jurisdiction under Article 226** — *wider* than Article 32, since it runs for
Fundamental Rights "and for any other purpose" — and has **superintendence** over all courts and
tribunals in its territory under Article 227.

**District judiciary.** On the **civil** side the **District Judge**, with Senior Civil Judges and
Civil Judges (Junior Division) below, tiered by the **value** of the suit. On the **criminal**
side the **Sessions Judge**, with the Chief Judicial Magistrate and Judicial Magistrates below,
tiered by the **severity of punishment** they may award.

**Tribunals.** Specialised bodies with judicial and technical members. In company law the
**National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT)** and above it the **National Company Law Appellate
Tribunal (NCLAT)**, constituted under ss. 408 and 410 of the Companies Act 2013. NCLT decides
company-law and insolvency matters; appeal lies to NCLAT and from NCLAT to the **Supreme Court on
a question of law**. Others to know by name: Debts Recovery Tribunal, Securities Appellate
Tribunal (appeals from SEBI), Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, consumer commissions.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Note the exit route. From <strong>NCLAT the appeal goes to the Supreme Court</strong>, not to
    a High Court. An option routing an NCLAT order to the High Court is a standard trap.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. Jurisdiction — three questions before you file

Jurisdiction is a court's authority to hear a case. Three questions, and **all three must be
answered yes**:

1. **Subject-matter** — is this court competent for *this kind* of case? A civil court cannot try
   a murder; a magistrate cannot decree specific performance; a matter the Companies Act assigns
   to the NCLT cannot go to a civil court, whose jurisdiction is expressly barred.
2. **Territorial** — is it competent for *this place*? A civil suit is ordinarily filed where the
   defendant resides or carries on business, or where the cause of action wholly or partly arose.
3. **Pecuniary** — is the **value** of the claim within this court's limit? Civil courts are
   tiered by value, and the limits are fixed by the State concerned, so they differ from State to
   State. A plaint filed above a court's pecuniary competence is returned for presentation to the
   proper court.

A decree passed with no subject-matter jurisdiction is a **nullity** — it can be attacked even in
execution, and the parties' consent cannot supply the missing jurisdiction.

### Vignette 2 — which court hears this dispute

Anand supplies packaging material worth ₹18,00,000 to Bhavana's factory in the same district and
is not paid. In that State the Civil Judge (Junior Division) may try suits valued up to
₹10,00,000; above that and up to ₹1,00,00,000 the Senior Civil Judge is competent; beyond that
the District Judge. Anand also wants Bhavana prosecuted, saying she never meant to pay.

**Subject-matter.** Recovery of the price of goods sold is a **civil** claim; the remedy is money.
So it is a suit, not a complaint.

**Pecuniary.** ₹18,00,000 exceeds the Junior Division's ₹10,00,000 ceiling but is within the
Senior Civil Judge's ₹1,00,00,000 limit. **The Senior Civil Judge is the court of first
instance.**

**Territorial.** The defendant resides and carries on business in the district where delivery was
made, so that district's courts are competent on both limbs.

**The criminal allegation.** Non-payment of a debt is not an offence. Only if Anand can show a
**dishonest intention at the time of the deal** — that Bhavana induced delivery meaning never to
pay — does it become cheating, on a separate complaint before a magistrate. The two proceedings
are independent: winning the suit does not convict, and an acquittal does not wipe out the debt.

## 11. Civil proceedings and criminal proceedings

Civil law asks *what is owed to whom*; criminal law asks *whether a wrong against society was
committed, and how it should be punished*. A comparison question on this table is close to
guaranteed.

| | **Civil** | **Criminal** |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Enforce a right, compensate a loss | Punish an offence against the State |
| Started by | The aggrieved person, by a **plaint** (suit) | The State by prosecution, or a private **complaint** |
| Parties | **Plaintiff** v **Defendant** | **State / prosecution** v **Accused** |
| Procedural law | Code of Civil Procedure 1908 | Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita 2023 |
| Substantive law | Contract Act, Sale of Goods Act, etc. | Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023 and special penal statutes |
| Standard of proof | **Preponderance of probabilities** | **Beyond reasonable doubt** |
| Burden | On the plaintiff, who asserts | On the prosecution; the accused is **presumed innocent** |
| Outcome | **Decree** — damages, injunction, specific performance, possession | **Conviction or acquittal** — fine, imprisonment |
| Compromise | Generally allowed; suits may be settled or withdrawn | Only where the offence is **compoundable** |

**The same facts can produce both.** A dishonoured cheque gives the holder a civil suit for the
money *and* a criminal complaint under s. 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act 1881 — you will
meet that section later in this paper. The two run in parallel and neither decides the other.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The classic mix-up: writing that a plaintiff must prove his case "beyond reasonable doubt".
    That standard belongs to the prosecution in a criminal trial; in a civil suit the plaintiff
    need only tilt the balance of probabilities.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="amendment">
  <p>
    From <strong>1 July 2024</strong> the three criminal codes were replaced — the Bharatiya Nyaya
    Sanhita 2023 for the Indian Penal Code 1860, the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita 2023 for
    the Criminal Procedure Code 1973, and the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam 2023 for the Indian
    Evidence Act 1872. Older books still name the IPC, CrPC and Evidence Act. Use the new names.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 12. Alternative dispute resolution

Courts are slow and public. **ADR** is the family of methods that settle disputes outside a full
trial — faster, private, usually cheaper. Section 89 of the Code of Civil Procedure even lets a
court refer a pending case to one of them.

- **Arbitration.** The parties agree, usually through an **arbitration clause** in the contract,
  to have the dispute decided by an arbitral tribunal instead of a court. The tribunal delivers an
  **award**, which is **binding** and enforceable in the same manner as a decree. Court
  interference is deliberately narrow: an award may be set aside only on the limited grounds in
  **s. 34 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996** — it is not a re-hearing on merits.
  Arbitration is **adjudicatory**: someone decides *for* you.
- **Conciliation.** A neutral **conciliator** (Part III of the same 1996 Act) helps the parties
  settle and **may propose terms of settlement**. Nothing binds unless they agree — but once a
  settlement agreement is signed it has the **same status and effect as an arbitral award**.
- **Mediation.** A **mediator** helps the parties negotiate their own solution and neither decides
  nor proposes terms. Under the **Mediation Act 2023** a **mediated settlement agreement** is
  final, binding and enforceable as if it were a judgment or decree of a court.
- **Negotiation.** The parties talk directly, with no neutral. Cheapest, and non-binding until
  agreement.
- **Lok Adalat.** Under the Legal Services Authorities Act 1987; its **award is deemed a decree of
  a civil court and no appeal lies**, since it rests on consent.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Sort the methods by <strong>who decides</strong>. Negotiation, mediation and conciliation leave
    the decision with the parties (a conciliator may suggest terms, a mediator does not). In
    arbitration the <em>tribunal</em> decides and the award binds. That one distinction answers
    most ADR MCQs.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 13. How to read a bare Act

This section pays for the whole chapter. Every later question in Paper 2 is really "apply this
section to these facts", and sections have a fixed anatomy.

**The parts of an Act:** *short title* (the Indian Contract Act, 1872) · *long title and
preamble*, stating purpose and helping resolve ambiguity · *extent and commencement* · the
*definitions* clause · the substantive **sections**, often grouped in Chapters · **Schedules**,
which are part of the Act.

**Inside a section.** A section may split into **sub-sections** — 25(1), 25(2) — each sub-section
into **clauses** (a), (b), and each clause into **sub-clauses** (i), (ii). Read a citation
outside-in: *s. 2(68)(ii)* is clause (ii) of sub-section (68) of section 2.

Then the four riders that decide most exam questions:

| Device | Opening words | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Proviso** | "Provided that …" | Carves an **exception** out of the provision it is attached to, and only that provision. It narrows; it is read strictly. |
| **Explanation** | "Explanation.—" | **Clarifies or extends** the meaning of the main provision. It can bring something within the section but cannot be read against it. |
| **Exception** | "Exception.—" | States a case expressly taken out of the rule (common in the Contract Act). |
| **Illustration** | "Illustrations" | A worked example given by the legislature. It **helps you understand** the section but **cannot control or cut down** its language. |

**Definitions.** The definitions clause usually opens *"In this Act, unless the context otherwise
requires …"*, so a defined word carries its defined meaning throughout. Watch the verb:
**"means"** = the definition is **exhaustive**; **"includes"** = it is **extensive**, adding to
the ordinary meaning, so the list is illustrative and not closed; **"means and includes"** =
exhaustive but expressly enlarged.

**Two openings that change everything.** A **non-obstante clause** — *"Notwithstanding anything
contained in …"* — makes the provision **override** whatever it names: if s. 41 opens
"Notwithstanding anything contained in section 30", s. 41 wins where the two collide. **"Subject
to …"** does the opposite: it **subordinates** the provision to what follows.

**"Shall" and "may".** *Shall* is ordinarily **mandatory** (a duty); *may* is **enabling or
discretionary** (a power). Courts read them contextually, but at Foundation answer on the ordinary
rule — and never write that "may means shall".

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>Reading order for any section:</strong> main provision → proviso (does the exception
    cover my facts?) → explanation (has a meaning been extended?) → the definition of every
    defined word used → and last, the illustration, as a check on your understanding. In that
    order you cannot miss a limb.
  </p>
</Callout>

### Vignette 3 — reading a section with a proviso and an explanation

Take this fictional provision:

> **14. Registration of dealers.** (1) Every dealer whose turnover in a financial year exceeds
> ₹20,00,000 shall apply for registration within thirty days of exceeding that turnover:
>
> Provided that a dealer dealing exclusively in exempted goods shall not be required to apply
> under this sub-section.
>
> *Explanation.—* For the purposes of this section, "turnover" **includes** the value of goods
> supplied to a branch of the dealer situated outside the State.

Kavya's turnover from taxable goods is ₹17,00,000, plus ₹4,00,000 of goods sent to her own branch
in another State. She says she is below the limit and, in any case, deals partly in exempted
goods.

**Main provision** — the duty bites above ₹20,00,000 of turnover in the year.
**Explanation** — it *extends* "turnover" to cover branch transfers outside the State, so Kavya's
turnover is 17,00,000 + 4,00,000 = **₹21,00,000**; the limit is crossed. That is exactly what an
explanation does: it creates no new duty, it clarifies a word in the existing one.
**Proviso** — it exempts only a dealer dealing **exclusively** in exempted goods. Kavya deals
partly in taxable goods, so the exception does not reach her.

**Conclusion:** Kavya must apply within thirty days of crossing ₹20,00,000. Notice that the
explanation and the proviso pulled in opposite directions, and that the words *includes* and
*exclusively* did the real work.

## 14. The statutes and regulators of this paper

**The statutes you will study in Paper 2** — know the years; a one-mark question loves them.

| Statute | What it governs |
|---|---|
| **Indian Contract Act, 1872** | Formation, performance and breach; indemnity, guarantee, bailment, pledge, agency |
| **Sale of Goods Act, 1930** | Sale of goods; conditions and warranties; transfer of property; unpaid seller |
| **Indian Partnership Act, 1932** | Firms, mutual rights and duties, registration, dissolution |
| **Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008** | LLPs — partnership flexibility with limited liability |
| **Companies Act, 2013** | Incorporation, share capital, management and administration of companies |
| **Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881** | Promissory notes, bills of exchange, cheques; dishonour |

**The regulators.** Foundation asks for names and roles only.

- **MCA — Ministry of Corporate Affairs.** Administers the Companies Act 2013 and the LLP Act
  2008, works through the **Registrars of Companies (RoC)**, and issues rules and general
  circulars.
- **SEBI — Securities and Exchange Board of India.** Statutory regulator under the SEBI Act 1992:
  investor protection, the securities market, stock exchanges, listed companies, intermediaries
  and public issues. Appeals go to the Securities Appellate Tribunal.
- **RBI — Reserve Bank of India.** The central bank, under the RBI Act 1934: currency, monetary
  policy, regulation of banks under the Banking Regulation Act 1949 and of NBFCs, and foreign
  exchange under FEMA 1999.

Also place in a sentence each: **IBBI** (insolvency and bankruptcy), **IRDAI** (insurance),
**CCI** (competition), and **ICAI** — the statutory body under the Chartered Accountants Act 1949
that regulates the profession you are entering.

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

- **Confusing a rule with a circular.** Rules are made under an express statutory power and have
  the force of law; a circular is administrative and can neither override a section nor create a
  liability.
- **"An Act comes into force when the President signs it."** Assent makes it an Act;
  **commencement** is the date the Act states or the Government notifies, section by section.
- **Saying a High Court binds all of India.** Only the **Supreme Court** does (Article 141); a
  High Court binds courts within its own jurisdiction and persuades elsewhere.
- **Treating obiter dicta as binding**, or quoting an **illustration** as the operative text.
  Illustrations explain; they never control the section.
- **Reading a proviso as a free-standing rule.** It carves an exception out of the provision it
  sits under, and is read strictly — words like "exclusively" or "only" decide the answer.
- **Swapping burden and standard of proof.** Civil: plaintiff, preponderance of probabilities.
  Criminal: prosecution, beyond reasonable doubt, accused presumed innocent.
- **Sending a civil money claim to a criminal court**, or an NCLAT appeal to a High Court (it goes
  to the **Supreme Court** on a question of law).
- **Mixing up mediation, conciliation and arbitration.** Only arbitration ends in a binding
  **award decided by the tribunal**; a conciliator may *propose* terms, a mediator only
  facilitates.
- **"Means" versus "includes".** *Means* is exhaustive, *includes* extends the ordinary meaning.
  An option treating an "includes" list as closed is the planted error.
- **Forgetting residuary power.** A subject in none of the three Lists goes to **Parliament**, not
  to the States.

## One-page revision summary

**Sources:** Constitution · legislation · delegated legislation · judicial precedent · custom and
usage · personal law. Strength: **Constitution &gt; statute &gt; rules/regulations &gt; circular.**

**Constitution:** supreme law · Art. 13 — a law inconsistent with Fundamental Rights is void to
that extent · Art. 19(1)(g) freedom of trade, subject to reasonable restrictions · Art. 246 +
Seventh Schedule — **Union / State / Concurrent Lists** · Art. 248 residuary power to
**Parliament** · Art. 254 — on the Concurrent List the **Union law prevails**.

**Bill to Act:** introduction (Money Bill: Lok Sabha only) → three readings → other House →
**President's assent (Art. 111)** → Gazette = **Act** → **commencement** by its own terms or by
notification. **Ordinance** (Art. 123) when Parliament is not in session; lapses six weeks after
reassembly unless approved.

**Delegated legislation:** rules (Government) · regulations (regulator) · notifications (Gazette)
· circulars (administrative, weakest). Must be **intra vires**; the **section prevails** over an
inconsistent rule; rules are usually laid before Parliament.

**Precedent:** **ratio decidendi** binds, **obiter** persuades · **Art. 141** — Supreme Court binds
all courts in India · SC not bound by itself (larger Bench) · High Court binds within its own
territory only · foreign judgments persuasive · distinguish / overrule / reverse.

**Courts:** single integrated judiciary · Supreme Court (Art. 124) — original (Arts. 131, 32),
appellate (Arts. 132–134), **special leave (Art. 136)**, advisory (Art. 143) · High Courts (Art.
214; 25 of them) — **writs under Art. 226, wider than Art. 32** · superintendence Art. 227 ·
District Judge (civil, by value) and Sessions Judge (criminal, by punishment) · **NCLT → NCLAT →
Supreme Court on a question of law**.

**Jurisdiction:** subject-matter · territorial · pecuniary — **all three needed**; a decree without
subject-matter jurisdiction is a nullity, and consent cannot cure it.

**Civil vs criminal:** plaintiff v defendant / State v accused · plaint v complaint · CPC 1908 /
BNSS 2023 · preponderance of probabilities / beyond reasonable doubt · decree (damages,
injunction, specific performance) / conviction (fine, imprisonment) · the same facts can give
both.

**ADR:** negotiation (no neutral) · mediation (facilitates only; **Mediation Act 2023**,
settlement enforceable as a decree) · conciliation (neutral may propose terms; 1996 Act Part III,
settlement has the effect of an award) · **arbitration** (tribunal decides; award **binding**,
enforceable as a decree, set aside only on s. 34 grounds) · Lok Adalat (award deemed a decree,
**no appeal**).

**Reading a bare Act:** section → sub-section (1) → clause (a) → sub-clause (i) · **proviso** =
exception, read strictly · **explanation** = clarifies or extends · **illustration** = example,
never controls · **means** exhaustive, **includes** extensive · **"notwithstanding"** overrides,
**"subject to"** subordinates · **shall** mandatory, **may** discretionary.

**Regulators:** **MCA** (Companies Act, LLP Act, RoC) · **SEBI** (securities market, listed
companies) · **RBI** (currency, monetary policy, banks, NBFCs, FEMA) · plus IBBI, IRDAI, CCI,
ICAI.
