At the chai stall outside your coaching centre, you tell your friend you will buy him a cutting
chai tomorrow. Tomorrow you forget. Nothing happens — no notice, no court, no damages. On the same
morning the stall owner agrees to supply 200 cups a day to the office block next door for three
months at a fixed rate. He stops after a week. Something very different happens.

Both were promises. Only one was a **contract**. The Indian Contract Act, 1872 exists to draw that
line, and then to say what follows once the line has been crossed: who must perform, what excuses
performance, and what the law pays you when the other side walks away.

This is the biggest chapter in Paper 2 — on a normal paper it carries roughly **30 to 40 per cent
of the marks**, and questions from it appear both as one-liners on definitions and as small
scenarios where you must name the section and apply it. The good news is that the Act is short,
old and beautifully organised. Learn its ladder of definitions first, and the rest of the chapter
is that ladder applied to different facts.

## 1. Agreement, contract and the section 2 ladder

Section 2 is a staircase. Every later idea in the Act stands on one of its steps, so learn the
steps in order rather than as a list.

1. **Proposal — s. 2(a).** "When one person signifies to another his willingness to do or to
   abstain from doing anything, with a view to obtaining the assent of that other to such act or
   abstinence, he is said to make a proposal." Note the three parts: a signification (it must be
   communicated), of willingness, *with a view to obtaining assent*. A statement made without
   wanting the other person's yes is not a proposal.
2. **Acceptance and promise — s. 2(b).** "When the person to whom the proposal is made signifies
   his assent thereto, the proposal is said to be accepted. A proposal, when accepted, becomes a
   promise."
3. **Promisor and promisee — s. 2(c).** The maker of the proposal is the promisor; the person
   accepting it is the promisee.
4. **Consideration — s. 2(d).** The price of the promise. Full text and analysis in §7.
5. **Agreement — s. 2(e).** "Every promise and every set of promises, forming the consideration for
   each other, is an agreement." So: **agreement = promise + consideration**, or in the equation
   students memorise, **agreement = offer + acceptance**.
6. **Reciprocal promises — s. 2(f).** Promises which form the consideration, or part of the
   consideration, for each other.
7. **Void agreement — s. 2(g).** "An agreement not enforceable by law is said to be void."
8. **Contract — s. 2(h).** "An agreement enforceable by law is a contract." So
   **contract = agreement + enforceability at law**.
9. **Voidable contract — s. 2(i).** "An agreement which is enforceable by law at the option of one
   or more of the parties thereto, but not at the option of the other or others, is a voidable
   contract."
10. **Contract becoming void — s. 2(j).** "A contract which ceases to be enforceable by law becomes
    void when it ceases to be enforceable."

Salmond's line is the exam summary: **all contracts are agreements, but all agreements are not
contracts**. Draw two circles, the smaller (contracts) inside the larger (agreements). What sits in
the outer ring but not the inner one? Social and domestic arrangements, agreements the parties
never meant to be legally bound by, and agreements the Act itself declares void.

The **intention to create legal relations** is the reason your chai promise fails. The Act does not
say so in words, but it is read into the requirement of an agreement, and *Balfour v Balfour* — a
husband's promise of a monthly allowance to his wife — is the case name every examiner expects. In
domestic and social arrangements the intention is presumed absent; in commercial arrangements it is
presumed present.

## 2. The essentials of a valid contract — section 10

Section 10 is the checklist the whole rest of the chapter fills in: "All agreements are contracts
if they are made by the free consent of parties competent to contract, for a lawful consideration
and with a lawful object, and are not hereby expressly declared to be void."

Unpacked into the list you must be able to write in an exam:

1. **Offer and acceptance** — a lawful offer and a lawful acceptance, producing an agreement.
2. **Intention to create legal relations.**
3. **Free consent** — sections 13 and 14, expanded in §11 to §13.
4. **Capacity of the parties** — section 11, expanded in §10.
5. **Lawful consideration** — sections 2(d), 23 and 25.
6. **Lawful object** — section 23.
7. **Not expressly declared void** — sections 26 to 30.
8. **Certainty of meaning** — section 29.
9. **Possibility of performance** — section 56, first paragraph.
10. **Legal formalities where a law requires them** — the second paragraph of section 10 preserves
    any law requiring a contract to be in writing, attested or registered. So a contract of the kind
    that must be registered is not saved by section 10 merely because the ten other boxes are ticked.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A contract need <strong>not</strong> be in writing, on stamp paper, or witnessed unless some
    other law says so. Oral contracts are fully valid under this Act — the difficulty with them is
    <em>proof</em>, not validity. Any MCQ option that says "an agreement is a contract only if it is
    in writing" is wrong on the face of section 10.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. Classifying contracts — valid, void, voidable, illegal, unenforceable

**By enforceability**

- **Valid** — satisfies everything in section 10. Enforceable by both sides.
- **Void agreement** — s. 2(g). Never had legal effect at all: void *ab initio*. An agreement with a
  minor, an agreement without consideration, a wagering agreement.
- **Void contract** — s. 2(j). It was a good contract and then *ceased* to be enforceable, for
  instance when performance became impossible under section 56. The distinction matters: a void
  agreement was never a contract; a void contract was one and stopped being one.
- **Voidable** — s. 2(i). Enforceable at the option of the aggrieved party only. This is the
  standard consequence of coercion, undue influence, fraud and misrepresentation. Until the
  aggrieved party avoids it, the contract is perfectly good; if he affirms it, it stays good
  forever.
- **Illegal (unlawful) agreement** — its object or consideration is forbidden by law or is otherwise
  unlawful under section 23. Every illegal agreement is void, **but not every void agreement is
  illegal**. The extra sting of illegality is that *collateral transactions* also fail: money lent
  to finance a smuggling venture cannot be recovered, whereas money lent to a person to pay his
  betting losses is recoverable in most of India, because a wager is void but not illegal.
- **Unenforceable** — good in substance but the court cannot help you because of a technical defect:
  a required stamp is missing, the writing or registration is absent, or the limitation period has
  run out. Cure the technical defect (where it can be cured) and the contract becomes enforceable
  again.

**By formation**

- **Express** — s. 9. Made in words, spoken or written.
- **Implied** — s. 9. Inferred from conduct or circumstances. Boarding a bus and sitting down is an
  implied contract to pay the fare.
- **Tacit** — a sub-species of implied, formed by action alone, such as taking cash from an ATM.
- **Quasi-contract** — sections 68 to 72. Not a contract at all: an obligation the law imposes to
  prevent unjust enrichment. See §24.
- **E-contract** — formed by electronic means; valid, and given legal recognition by the Information
  Technology Act 2000.

**By performance**

- **Executed** — both sides have done everything. A cash sale over the counter.
- **Executory** — something remains to be done on both sides.
- **Partly executed, partly executory** — one side has performed, the other has not.
- **Unilateral (one-sided) contract** — one party has performed his part at the moment the contract
  comes into being, leaving an obligation outstanding only on the other. A reward advertisement is
  the standard example: the finder performs, and only the payment is left.
- **Bilateral** — obligations outstanding on both sides when the contract is made; the same thing as
  an executory contract.

## 4. Offer or proposal — its kinds, and what is not an offer

**Legal rules for a valid offer**

1. It must be capable of creating **legal relations**.
2. Its terms must be **certain**, not loose or vague.
3. It must be **communicated** to the offeree — one cannot accept an offer one does not know about.
4. It may be made **subject to conditions**, but the conditions must be brought to the offeree's
   notice; unusual conditions hidden on the back of a ticket do not bind.
5. It **must not contain a term the non-compliance with which amounts to acceptance** — "if I do
   not hear from you by Friday I shall assume you have accepted" imposes nothing on the offeree.
6. It must be distinguished from a mere statement of intention, a quotation of price, or an
   **invitation to offer**.

**Kinds of offer**

- **Express** — by words. **Implied** — by conduct, like a transport company running buses on a
  route.
- **Specific** — made to a definite person, and only that person can accept it (*Boulton v Jones*).
- **General** — made to the world at large; anyone with notice who fulfils the conditions accepts
  it. *Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co* is the case: a company advertised a reward for anyone who
  caught influenza after using its smoke ball as directed, and was held bound to a lady who did.
  A general offer of a **continuing** nature (a missing-person reward) remains open until withdrawn
  and can be accepted by many people; a general offer for a **single** act (a lost-dog reward) is
  exhausted by the first person to perform.
- **Cross offers** — two identical offers cross in the post in ignorance of each other. There is
  **no contract**, because neither is an acceptance of the other. This is a favourite one-liner.
- **Counter offer** — an acceptance with a variation. It **kills the original offer**, which cannot
  afterwards be accepted (*Hyde v Wrench*). Distinguish a genuine counter offer from a mere
  *inquiry* ("would you consider payment in instalments?"), which leaves the original offer alive.
- **Standing, open or continuing offer** — an offer left open over a period, typically a tender to
  supply goods as and when required. Each order placed against it is a separate acceptance forming
  a separate contract, and the offeror may revoke the standing offer for future orders at any time
  (subject to the tender's own terms).

**Invitation to offer — the three classics**

An invitation to offer merely invites the other side to make the offer; the person issuing it keeps
the last word.

| Situation | Who makes the offer | Who accepts |
|---|---|---|
| Goods displayed in a shop window or on a self-service shelf with a price tag | The customer, at the counter | The shopkeeper |
| Auction: the auctioneer's announcement and the display of lots | The bidder, by bidding | The auctioneer, by the fall of the hammer |
| Tenders invited by advertisement | The tenderer, by submitting the tender | The inviter, by accepting a tender |

Also invitations, not offers: a **prospectus** issued by a company, a **catalogue** or price list, a
**menu**, and an advertisement for the sale of goods generally. A bid at an auction may be withdrawn
before the hammer falls, precisely because it is only an offer until then.

## 5. Communication, revocation and lapse of an offer — sections 3 to 6

**Section 3** says communication of proposals, acceptances and revocations is made by any act or
omission of the party by which he intends to communicate, or which has the effect of communicating
it. So conduct communicates as effectively as words.

**Section 4 — when communication is complete.** The section is short and the exam lives inside it:

- The communication of a **proposal** is complete when it comes to the knowledge of the person to
  whom it is made.
- The communication of an **acceptance** is complete — *as against the proposer*, when it is put in
  a course of transmission to him, so as to be out of the power of the acceptor; *as against the
  acceptor*, when it comes to the knowledge of the proposer.
- The communication of a **revocation** is complete — *as against the person who makes it*, when it
  is put into a course of transmission to the person to whom it is made, so as to be out of the
  power of the person who makes it; *as against the person to whom it is made*, when it comes to his
  knowledge.

The illustrations to section 4 are the cleanest statement of the postal rule in Indian law and are
worth reproducing exactly. A proposes, by letter, to sell a house to B at a certain price:

> "(a) The communication of the proposal is complete when B receives the letter.
> (b) B accepts A's proposal by a letter sent by post. The communication of the acceptance is
> complete, as against A, when the letter is posted; as against B, when the letter is received by A."

**Section 5 — revocation, and its two deadlines.** "A proposal may be revoked at any time before the
communication of its acceptance is complete as against the proposer, but not afterwards. An
acceptance may be revoked at any time before the communication of the acceptance is complete as
against the acceptor, but not afterwards."

Translate the two deadlines into dates, because that is how they are examined:

<div class="formula">The offeror may revoke until the moment the acceptance is POSTED. The acceptor may revoke until the moment the acceptance letter REACHES the offeror.</div>

And remember the second half: a revocation only bites when the other side **knows** of it. So a
revocation of the offer must actually *reach the acceptor* before he posts; a revocation of the
acceptance must *reach the offeror* no later than the acceptance itself. A telegram of revocation
that overtakes a posted acceptance letter is effective; one that arrives afterwards is not.

**Telephone, telex and instant communication.** Where the parties speak in real time there is no
transmission gap, so the postal rule has nothing to work on: the contract is complete only when the
acceptance is actually **heard** by the offeror, and it is made at the place where the acceptance is
heard. If the line goes dead in the middle of the words of acceptance, there is no contract, and the
acceptor must repeat himself.

**Section 6 — how an offer comes to an end.** A proposal is revoked:

1. by **notice of revocation** given by the proposer to the other party;
2. by the **lapse of the time** prescribed in the proposal for its acceptance, or, where no time is
   prescribed, by the lapse of a **reasonable time**, without communication of the acceptance;
3. by the **failure of the acceptor to fulfil a condition precedent** to acceptance;
4. by the **death or insanity of the proposer**, *if the fact of his death or insanity comes to the
   knowledge of the acceptor before acceptance*.

Add two more ways from the general law: a **counter offer**, and an offer that is **rejected**
outright. Note carefully the shape of ground (4) — the death of the proposer does not by itself kill
the offer; the acceptor's *knowledge* of it does. An acceptance posted in genuine ignorance of the
proposer's death binds his estate. The death of the *acceptor*, on the other hand, ends the matter,
since only he could have accepted.

  <p>
    Ashok, in Nagpur, posts a letter on <strong>4 March 2026</strong> offering to sell his printing
    press to Bharti in Raipur for ₹6,40,000, adding "this offer is open for twenty-one days from the
    date of this letter". Bharti receives it on <strong>7 March</strong>. On <strong>11 March</strong>
    Bharti posts her acceptance; the letter reaches Ashok on <strong>15 March</strong>. Meanwhile, on
    <strong>9 March</strong> Ashok posted a letter revoking the offer, which reached Bharti on
    <strong>13 March</strong>. Ashok says there is no contract. Advise.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 1 — when did the offer lapse?</div>
    Twenty-one days from 4 March = <strong>25 March</strong> (s. 6(2)). Everything below happened
    inside the window, so lapse of time is not in issue.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 2 — was the acceptance effective?</div>
    Communication of the acceptance is complete as against Ashok when Bharti <em>posts</em> it —
    <strong>11 March</strong> (s. 4). The contract was therefore concluded on 11 March.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 3 — was the revocation in time?</div>
    Ashok could revoke only up to the moment the acceptance was posted (s. 5), and his revocation
    binds Bharti only when it comes to her knowledge (s. 4) — that is,
    <strong>13 March</strong>. 13 March is after 11 March, so the revocation is
    <strong>too late and inoperative</strong>. The date Ashok <em>posted</em> it (9 March) is
    irrelevant: for a revocation, posting completes the communication only as against the sender.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Conclusion</div>
    A valid contract came into existence on <strong>11 March 2026</strong>. Ashok is bound.
    If Bharti had instead wished to change her mind, she could have revoked her acceptance by any
    faster means reaching Ashok on or before <strong>15 March</strong>, the day her letter arrived.
  </div>
</Callout>

## 6. Acceptance — sections 7 and 8

**Section 7 — the two conditions.** To convert a proposal into a promise, the acceptance must:

1. be **absolute and unqualified**; and
2. be **expressed in some usual and reasonable manner**, unless the proposal prescribes the manner
   in which it is to be accepted.

The tail of section 7 is where marks hide. If the proposal prescribes a manner and the acceptance
comes in some other manner, the contract is **not** automatically bad. The proposer may, **within a
reasonable time** after the acceptance is communicated to him, insist that his proposal be accepted
in the prescribed manner; **but if he fails to do so, he accepts the acceptance**. Silence by the
proposer therefore validates a deviant acceptance. (Contrast the English rule, where a prescribed
mode is mandatory.)

**The rules of a valid acceptance, in the order examiners ask them**

- It must be **by the offeree**, or by someone authorised by him — a specific offer cannot be
  accepted by a stranger who learns of it.
- It must be **absolute and unqualified**. A conditional or partial acceptance is a counter offer.
- It must be **communicated** to the offeror. A mental resolve to accept, or an acceptance written
  and left in a drawer, is nothing. *Brogden v Metropolitan Railway* — a signed draft agreement kept
  in a drawer became binding only when the parties acted on it.
- It must be **in the prescribed or a usual and reasonable manner**.
- It must be given **within the time prescribed** or within a reasonable time, and **before the offer
  lapses or is revoked**.
- It **cannot precede the offer**, and it must be made **with knowledge of the offer** — a person who
  finds and returns a lost article without knowing of the reward cannot claim it (*Lalman Shukla v
  Gauri Datt*).
- It must show an **intention to fulfil the promise**.

**Section 8 — acceptance by performing conditions.** "Performance of the conditions of a proposal,
or the acceptance of any consideration for a reciprocal promise which may be offered with a
proposal, is an acceptance of the proposal." This is the section that makes general offers work: the
lady in *Carlill* accepted by using the smoke ball, not by writing a letter. Where acceptance is by
performance, **prior communication is not required** — performance is the communication.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Silence is never acceptance.</strong> The offeror cannot impose a duty to reply. In
    <em>Felthouse v Bindley</em> the buyer wrote "if I hear no more, I consider the horse mine";
    the seller said nothing, and there was no contract. Two riders: (i) the offeree's silence
    <em>plus conduct</em> showing acceptance is a good acceptance under s. 8; and (ii) where the
    parties have an established course of dealing in which silence has always meant yes, silence may
    be read as assent.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. Consideration — section 2(d) and its essentials

Consideration is what makes a promise a bargain instead of a gift. **Section 2(d)**: "When, at the
desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained from doing, or does
or abstains from doing, or promises to do or to abstain from doing, something, such act or
abstinence or promise is called a consideration for the promise."

Pollock's summary — the price for which the promise of the other is bought — is the line to quote.

**The essentials, each of which is an examinable rule**

1. **It must move at the desire of the promisor.** An act done at the desire of a third party, or
   voluntarily, is not consideration. In *Durga Prasad v Baldeo* a man built shops at the Collector's
   order; the shopkeepers' later promise to pay him a commission was unsupported by consideration,
   because his building was not at their desire.
2. **It may move from the promisee or any other person.** These words are the Indian departure from
   English law — **privity of consideration does not apply in India**. In *Chinnaya v Ramayya* an old
   lady gifted land to her daughter on condition that the daughter pay an annuity to the lady's
   sister; the sister could enforce the promise although the consideration moved from the mother, not
   from her.
3. **It may be past, present (executed) or future (executory).** *Past* consideration — something
   already done at the promisor's desire before the promise — is **good consideration in India**, and
   is not in England. Do not confuse past consideration with the section 25(2) exception, which is
   about a *voluntary* past act done without any desire of the promisor.
4. **It need not be adequate.** Explanation 2 to section 25 settles this: an agreement is not void
   merely because the consideration is inadequate, but inadequacy may be taken into account by the
   court in deciding whether consent was freely given. The Act's own illustration is unforgettable —
   "A agrees to sell a horse worth Rs. 1,000 for Rs. 10. A's consent to the agreement was freely
   given. The agreement is a contract notwithstanding the inadequacy of the consideration."
5. **It must be real and not illusory.** A promise to bring a dead relative back to life, or to do
   something physically impossible, is no consideration. Neither is a promise to do what one is
   *already legally bound to do* — a witness cannot charge for giving evidence he is summoned to
   give. A promise to do **more** than the existing duty is good consideration.
6. **It must be something the promisor is not already bound to do**, and **it must not be unlawful**
   — an unlawful consideration takes the agreement into section 23 and makes it void.

**Stranger to consideration versus stranger to contract.** In India a stranger to the
*consideration* may sue; a stranger to the *contract* may not. Keep the two apart — it is the single
most confused pair in Unit 2.

## 8. No consideration, no contract — section 25 and its exceptions

The rule and its exceptions sit in one section. **Section 25**: an agreement made without
consideration is void, **unless** —

1. **Natural love and affection — s. 25(1).** It is expressed **in writing** and **registered** under
   the law for the registration of documents, and is made **on account of natural love and affection
   between parties standing in a near relation to each other**. All four elements are cumulative:
   writing, registration, near relation, and actual love and affection. A registered deed between
   estranged brothers executed only to settle quarrels has failed this test — near relation without
   affection is not enough, and affection without registration is not enough either.
2. **Compensation for past voluntary service — s. 25(2).** A promise to compensate, wholly or in
   part, a person who has **already voluntarily done something for the promisor**, or something which
   **the promisor was legally compellable to do**. The service must have been rendered voluntarily
   (not at the promisor's request — that would be ordinary past consideration) and to the promisor
   himself, and the promisor must have been competent to contract when the service was rendered.
3. **Promise to pay a time-barred debt — s. 25(3).** A promise **in writing and signed** by the
   person to be charged, or by his duly authorised agent, to pay wholly or in part a debt of which
   the creditor might have enforced payment but for the law of limitation. An oral promise will not
   do, and neither will a signature by a stranger.

Beyond section 25 itself, the SM lists these further situations where the absence of consideration
does not matter:

- **Completed gift — Explanation 1 to s. 25.** Nothing in the section affects the validity, as
  between donor and donee, of any gift actually made. A gift once delivered cannot be recalled for
  want of consideration.
- **Agency — s. 185.** "No consideration is necessary to create an agency."
- **Remission — s. 63.** A promisee may remit or reduce performance, or extend time, without any
  consideration for doing so (see §18).
- **Bailment — s. 148.** A gratuitous bailment is valid though the bailee is paid nothing.
- **Charitable subscription acted upon.** Where the promisee, on the faith of the promised
  subscription, incurs a liability, the promise is enforceable to the extent of that liability —
  *Kedar Nath Bhattacharji v Gorie Mahomed*, the Howrah town hall case. A bare promise to subscribe
  with nothing done on its faith remains unenforceable.

## 9. Privity of contract and privity of consideration

**Privity of contract** means only a party to a contract can sue on it. A stranger to the contract
acquires no right under it even if the contract was made for his benefit — *Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co
v Selfridge*. India follows this rule, though the Act nowhere states it.

**The exceptions**, which is what gets asked:

1. **Trust or charge.** Where the contract creates a trust in favour of a third party, or a charge
   on specific property in his favour, the beneficiary may enforce it.
2. **Marriage settlement, partition or other family arrangement.** A provision made for a family
   member in a partition deed or marriage settlement can be enforced by that member although he did
   not sign.
3. **Acknowledgement or estoppel.** Where a party to the contract acknowledges his liability to the
   third person — expressly or by conduct — he is estopped from denying it. Money handed to an agent
   to be paid over to X, which the agent acknowledges to X, is recoverable by X.
4. **Assignment of a contract.** The assignee of the benefit of a contract may sue on it (an
   assignee by operation of law, such as a legal representative or an official assignee, likewise).
   The *burden* of a contract cannot be assigned without the other party's consent.
5. **Covenants running with land.** A person buying land with notice of a covenant affecting it is
   bound by it, though not a party to the original covenant (*Tulk v Moxhay*).
6. **Agency and contracts entered into by an agent** for a principal — the principal may sue and be
   sued.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The trap in every privity question: students write "in India a stranger to a contract can sue,
    because section 2(d) says consideration may move from any other person". That mixes the two
    doctrines. Section 2(d) abolishes privity of <strong>consideration</strong> only. Privity of
    <strong>contract</strong> is alive in India, and the way in is one of the six exceptions above —
    in <em>Chinnaya v Ramayya</em> the sister could sue because the deed created a
    <strong>charge</strong> in her favour, not merely because she had furnished no consideration.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. Capacity to contract — section 11, and the minor

**Section 11**: "Every person is competent to contract who is of the age of majority according to
the law to which he is subject, and who is of sound mind, and is not disqualified from contracting
by any law to which he is subject." Three tests, all of which must be satisfied.

**Age of majority.** Under section 3 of the Indian Majority Act 1875, a person attains majority on
completing **18 years**. (Older textbooks mention 21 years where a guardian of the minor's person or
property had been appointed by a court; that extension was **removed by the Indian Majority
(Amendment) Act 1999**. If your SM edition still prints the 21-year exception, follow the SM in the
exam and note the amendment here.)

**The minor's position — the ten propositions**

1. **An agreement with a minor is void ab initio.** *Mohori Bibee v Dharmodas Ghose* (1903) settled
   this: a minor mortgaged his house to a moneylender, and the Privy Council held the mortgage
   wholly void, refusing the lender's claim for repayment. There is no such thing as a "voidable"
   minor's agreement.
2. **No ratification on attaining majority.** A void agreement cannot be brought to life by later
   approval. If the now-major person wants to be bound he must make a **fresh contract supported by
   fresh consideration**; consideration furnished during minority will not support the new promise.
3. **A minor may be a promisee or beneficiary.** The bar protects him; it does not disable him. A
   minor may enforce a contract that is wholly for his benefit — he can sue on a promissory note
   executed in his favour, or on a fully paid-for delivery due to him.
4. **No estoppel against a minor.** A minor who lies about his age and obtains a loan is still not
   liable on the contract; he is not estopped from pleading minority.
5. **Restitution in narrow circumstances.** Because the agreement is void *ab initio* and not merely
   "discovered to be void", sections 64 and 65 do not fasten liability on the minor. Where, however,
   the minor obtained property or money by **misrepresenting his age**, a court may on equitable
   principles order **restoration of what is still traceable** in his hands. Money already spent
   cannot be followed, and no personal decree for repayment will be made — that would be enforcing
   the void agreement by another route.
6. **Liability for necessaries — section 68.** Where a person incapable of contracting, or anyone he
   is legally bound to support, is supplied by another with **necessaries suited to his condition in
   life**, the supplier is entitled to be reimbursed **from the property of that incapable person**.
   Read the three limits: it is a *quasi-contractual* claim, not a contractual one; only
   **necessaries** count (food, clothing, shelter, basic education, medical aid, and in a decided
   case the cost of defending the minor in litigation — not luxuries, and not goods of which he
   already has an adequate supply); and recovery is **against the estate, never against the minor
   personally**. A minor with no property pays nothing.
7. **A minor may be an agent.** He binds his principal by his acts but incurs no personal liability.
8. **A minor cannot be a partner**, but may, with the consent of all the partners, be **admitted to
   the benefits of partnership** (section 30 of the Indian Partnership Act 1932). His liability is
   limited to his share.
9. **A minor cannot be adjudicated insolvent**, since he is incapable of incurring debts.
10. **A minor is liable in tort**, but not where the tort is in substance a breach of contract — one
    cannot convert a void contract into a good tort claim by changing the label. A **joint contract
    by a minor and an adult** binds the adult alone. A minor's **parent or guardian is not liable**
    for his contracts unless the minor acted as his agent; a guardian may, however, make a valid
    contract *for the minor's benefit* within his authority (not, for instance, a contract to buy
    immovable property for the minor).

**Persons of unsound mind — section 12.** A person is of sound mind for contracting if, **at the
time when he makes the contract**, he is capable of understanding it and of forming a rational
judgment as to its effect upon his interests. Hence: a person usually of unsound mind but
occasionally sane **may** contract during a lucid interval; a person usually sane but occasionally
insane **may not** contract during his insanity. The category covers idiocy, lunacy, delirium from
illness, **drunkenness** and hypnotism. An agreement by a person of unsound mind is **void**, and his
estate remains liable for necessaries under section 68.

**Disqualified persons** — persons whom some other law disables:

- **Alien enemies** — contracts made during a war with an alien enemy are void; contracts made before
  it are suspended or dissolved.
- **Foreign sovereigns, ambassadors and diplomatic staff** — they may contract and sue, but cannot
  be sued in Indian courts without the sanction of the Central Government.
- **Convicts** — incapable of contracting while undergoing a sentence of imprisonment.
- **Insolvents** — an adjudicated insolvent cannot deal with his property until discharged.
- **Corporations and statutory bodies** — capacity is limited by their memorandum and the statute
  creating them; a contract beyond those powers is *ultra vires* and void.

## 11. Free consent: coercion and undue influence — sections 15 and 16

**Section 13** defines consent: two or more persons are said to consent when they **agree upon the
same thing in the same sense** — *consensus ad idem*. **Section 14** says consent is **free** when it
is not caused by (i) coercion, (ii) undue influence, (iii) fraud, (iv) misrepresentation, or
(v) mistake under sections 20, 21 and 22. And it adds the causation test: consent is said to be so
caused where **it would not have been given but for** the existence of that coercion, undue
influence, fraud, misrepresentation or mistake.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Memorise the consequence line for each of the five, because MCQ options are built on the
    difference: coercion, undue influence, fraud and misrepresentation make the contract
    <strong>voidable</strong> at the option of the aggrieved party (ss. 19 and 19A); a
    <strong>bilateral</strong> mistake of fact makes the agreement <strong>void</strong> (s. 20); a
    <strong>unilateral</strong> mistake of fact and a mistake of Indian law leave the contract
    <strong>valid</strong> (ss. 21 and 22).
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 15 — coercion.** Coercion is "the committing, or threatening to commit, any act forbidden
by the Indian Penal Code, or the unlawful detaining, or threatening to detain, any property, to the
prejudice of any person whatever, with the intention of causing any person to enter into an
agreement." Points that get examined:

- Two limbs — an act **forbidden by the penal law**, or **unlawful detention of property**. A threat
  to file a *civil* suit, or to do something one is lawfully entitled to do, is not coercion.
- The act may be directed **against any person whatever**, not only the contracting party — a threat
  against a stranger's child is coercion.
- It may be applied **by any person**, not necessarily the other contracting party.
- The **Explanation** makes it immaterial whether the penal law is or is not in force in the place
  where the coercion is employed.
- The classic Indian case is *Chikham Amiraju v Chikham Seshamma*, where a threat by a husband to
  commit suicide, made to induce his wife and son to release property, was held to be coercion.

<Callout kind="amendment" label="Reading section 15 today">
  <p>
    Section 15 still speaks of the <strong>Indian Penal Code</strong>. The IPC was repealed and
    replaced by the <strong>Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023</strong> with effect from 1 July 2024, and by
    the ordinary rule of construction the reference is now read as a reference to the corresponding
    provision of the new Sanhita. Nothing of substance changes — the test remains "an act forbidden
    by the penal law" — and ICAI examines the substance. If your SM edition prints "Indian Penal
    Code", write that, and you will not be marked down for adding "now the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita
    2023".
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 16 — undue influence.** "(1) A contract is said to be induced by undue influence where the
relations subsisting between the parties are such that one of the parties is in a position to
dominate the will of the other and uses that position to obtain an unfair advantage over the other."

Sub-section (2) deems a person able to dominate the will of another:

- **(a)** where he holds a **real or apparent authority** over the other, or stands in a **fiduciary
  relation** to him; or
- **(b)** where he makes a contract with a person whose **mental capacity is temporarily or
  permanently affected by reason of age, illness, or mental or bodily distress**.

Sub-section (3) shifts the burden: where a person in a position to dominate contracts with the other
and the transaction appears **unconscionable** on its face or on the evidence, **the burden of proving
that the contract was not induced by undue influence lies on the dominant party**.

| Relationship | Undue influence presumed? |
|---|---|
| Parent and child; guardian and ward | Yes |
| Trustee and beneficiary | Yes |
| Doctor and patient; advocate and client | Yes |
| Spiritual guru and disciple | Yes |
| Master and servant | Yes |
| Creditor and debtor in urgent need | Yes, where the debtor's distress is exploited |
| Husband and wife (ordinary case) | No |
| Landlord and tenant | No |
| Creditor and debtor at arm's length | No |

**Coercion compared with undue influence** — the comparison is a standard 4-mark question:

| | Coercion (s. 15) | Undue influence (s. 16) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of pressure | Physical or penal — a threat or an act forbidden by penal law, or detention of property | Moral or mental — domination of the will |
| Relationship needed | None; strangers can coerce | A relationship enabling one party to dominate the other |
| Who may apply it | Any person, against any person | A party to the contract, against the other |
| Burden of proof | On the party alleging coercion | Shifts to the dominant party once domination plus an unconscionable bargain are shown |
| Relief | Contract voidable; benefit received must be restored (s. 64); money paid recoverable (s. 72) | Contract voidable; the court may set it aside **absolutely or upon such terms as it thinks just** (s. 19A) |
| Criminal aspect | May involve an offence under the penal law | No criminal liability |

## 12. Free consent: fraud and misrepresentation — sections 17 to 19

**Section 17 — fraud.** Fraud means and includes any of the following acts committed **by a party to
a contract, or with his connivance, or by his agent**, with intent to deceive another party or his
agent, or to induce him to enter into the contract:

1. the **suggestion, as a fact, of that which is not true**, by one who does not believe it to be
   true;
2. the **active concealment** of a fact by one having knowledge or belief of the fact;
3. a **promise made without any intention of performing it**;
4. **any other act fitted to deceive**;
5. any such act or omission as the **law specially declares to be fraudulent**.

Note the two gateways at the start: the false statement must come from a **party** (or his agent or
with his connivance), and there must be an **intent to deceive or to induce the contract**. A lie by
a complete stranger is not fraud under section 17.

**Silence and fraud — the Explanation.** "Mere silence as to facts likely to affect the willingness
of a person to enter into a contract is not fraud, unless the circumstances of the case are such
that, regard being had to them, it is the duty of the person keeping silence to speak, or unless his
silence is, in itself, equivalent to speech."

So silence becomes fraud in four situations:

1. **Where there is a duty to speak** — contracts *uberrimae fidei* (of utmost good faith):
   insurance, contracts for the sale of immovable property as to defects in title, family
   settlements, contracts of guarantee, allotment of shares on a prospectus, and any fiduciary
   relationship.
2. **Where silence is equivalent to speech** — the buyer says "I shall assume the horse is sound if
   you say nothing", and the seller says nothing.
3. **Half-truths** — a statement true as far as it goes but misleading because of what is left out.
4. **Change of circumstances** — a representation true when made becomes false before the contract is
   concluded, and is not corrected.

**Section 18 — misrepresentation.** Misrepresentation is an **innocent** false statement. It means
and includes:

1. the **positive assertion**, in a manner not warranted by the information of the person making it,
   of that which is not true, **though he believes it to be true**;
2. any **breach of duty** which, without an intent to deceive, gains an advantage to the person
   committing it by misleading another to his prejudice;
3. causing, **however innocently**, a party to an agreement to make a **mistake as to the substance**
   of the thing which is the subject of the agreement.

| | Fraud (s. 17) | Misrepresentation (s. 18) |
|---|---|---|
| State of mind | The maker knows the statement is false, or does not believe it true | The maker honestly believes it is true |
| Intention | To deceive or to induce the contract | No intent to deceive |
| Remedies | Rescind the contract, **or** insist on performance as represented, **and** claim damages | Rescind, or insist on performance as represented — **no damages** |
| "Means of discovering the truth" defence | Not available where the fraud is by active misstatement | Available — see the Exception to s. 19 |

**Section 19 — the consequence.** Where consent to an agreement is caused by coercion, fraud or
misrepresentation, the agreement is a **contract voidable at the option of the party whose consent
was so caused**. That party may instead **affirm** the contract and insist that it be performed, and
that he be put in the position in which he would have been if the representations made had been
true.

Two qualifications you must carry:

- **The Exception.** If consent was caused by misrepresentation, or by silence amounting to fraud
  under section 17, the contract is **not voidable** if the party whose consent was so caused **had
  the means of discovering the truth with ordinary diligence**. This defence does **not** rescue
  active fraud.
- **The Explanation.** A fraud or misrepresentation which **did not cause** the consent does not
  render the contract voidable. The lie must have been relied upon.

**Section 19A** deals with undue influence separately: the contract is voidable at the option of the
party whose consent was so caused, and the court may set the contract aside **absolutely, or upon
such terms and conditions as it thinks just**.

**Loss of the right to rescind.** The aggrieved party loses the option where he **affirms** the
contract after knowing the truth, where **restitution becomes impossible**, where **third-party
rights** have intervened for value and without notice, or after **unreasonable delay**.

## 13. Free consent: mistake — sections 20 to 22

Three short sections, and the whole topic is the pattern of which one applies.

**Section 20 — bilateral mistake of fact makes the agreement void.** "Where both the parties to an
agreement are under a mistake as to a matter of fact essential to the agreement, the agreement is
void." Note the Explanation: an **erroneous opinion as to the value** of the subject matter is
**not** a mistake of fact. Three conditions: the mistake must be **mutual**, of **fact** (not law),
and about a fact **essential** to the agreement.

Categories of essential bilateral mistake:

- **Existence of the subject matter** (*res extincta*) — the cargo has already perished when the sale
  is made (*Couturier v Hastie*).
- **Identity of the subject matter** — one party means the ship *Peerless* sailing in October, the
  other the ship *Peerless* sailing in December.
- **Title** (*res sua*) — the buyer already owns what he is buying (*Cooper v Phibbs*).
- **Quantity** — a mutual misunderstanding about the quantity contracted for.
- **Quality of the subject matter**, but only where the mistake makes the thing **essentially
  different** from what the parties believed. A mistake about a mere attribute is not enough.
- **Price**, where a clerical slip in the offer is shared by both.

**Section 21 — mistake of law.** "A contract is not voidable because it was caused by a mistake as to
any law in force in India; but a mistake as to a law not in force in India has the same effect as a
mistake of fact." Ignorance of Indian law is no excuse; a mistake about **foreign law** is treated as
a mistake of fact and can void the agreement. Note the separate rule in section 72: money paid under
a **mistake** — including a mistake of law — is recoverable, so section 21 governs the contract's
validity, not the recovery of money paid.

**Section 22 — unilateral mistake.** "A contract is not voidable merely because it was caused by one
of the parties to it being under a mistake as to a matter of fact." One-sided carelessness does not
undo a bargain.

But a unilateral mistake makes the agreement **void** in two situations where there was never any
real consent at all:

1. **Mistake as to the identity of the person contracted with**, where identity was material to the
   contract (*Cundy v Lindsay* — a rogue imitating a known firm's name).
2. **Mistake as to the nature of the document signed** (*non est factum*) — where a person is made to
   sign a document fundamentally different from what he was told it was, and was not negligent.

<div class="formula">Bilateral mistake of fact → VOID (s. 20) · Unilateral mistake of fact → VALID (s. 22) · Mistake of Indian law → VALID (s. 21) · Mistake of foreign law → treated as mistake of fact</div>

## 14. Lawful object and lawful consideration — sections 23 and 24

**Section 23.** The consideration or object of an agreement is lawful **unless** —

1. it is **forbidden by law**;
2. it is of such a nature that, **if permitted, it would defeat the provisions of any law**;
3. it is **fraudulent**;
4. it **involves or implies injury to the person or property of another**; or
5. **the court regards it as immoral, or opposed to public policy**.

In each of those cases the consideration or object is unlawful, and **every agreement of which the
object or consideration is unlawful is void**.

"Object" here means **purpose or design**, not "consideration" — an agreement can have perfectly
lawful consideration and still be void because its purpose is unlawful. Money lent, knowing it is to
be used to bribe a public servant, is the classic example.

**Heads of public policy** — the list the SM expects:

- **Trading with an alien enemy.**
- **Stifling a prosecution** — an agreement to drop criminal proceedings for money. (Compounding an
  offence that the law permits to be compounded is lawful.)
- **Maintenance and champerty** — financing another's litigation for a share of the proceeds. In
  India these are not automatically void, but an agreement that is extortionate or made from an
  improper motive is opposed to public policy.
- **Interference with the course of justice** — an agreement to influence a judge or a witness.
- **Marriage brokerage agreements** — payment for procuring a marriage.
- **Traffic in public offices and titles** — payment for securing a government post or an honour.
- **Agreements tending to create monopolies.**
- **Agreements in restraint of parental rights, of personal liberty, or of the duties of a public
  servant.**

**Section 24 — partial illegality.** "If any part of a single consideration for one or more objects,
or any one or any part of any one of several considerations for a single object, is unlawful, the
agreement is void." So a single, indivisible bargain that is part lawful and part unlawful is void
**as a whole**. Where the lawful part is **severable** and supported by its own separate
consideration, the lawful part can stand — this is why draftsmen split agreements into independent
covenants.

## 15. Agreements expressly declared void — sections 26 to 30

These are agreements which fail even though offer, acceptance, consideration, capacity and consent
are all present. The Act simply declares them void.

**Section 26 — restraint of marriage.** Every agreement in restraint of the marriage of any person,
**other than a minor**, is void. Partial restraints count too — a promise not to marry a particular
person, or not to marry for five years, is void. A restraint on the marriage of a **minor** is
outside the section.

**Section 27 — restraint of trade.** "Every agreement by which any one is restrained from exercising
a lawful profession, trade or business of any kind, is to that extent void." India is stricter than
England: **reasonableness is no defence** under section 27 except where the Act or another statute
provides an exception.

The exceptions:

- **Sale of goodwill (the Exception to s. 27).** One who sells the goodwill of a business may agree
  with the buyer to refrain from carrying on a similar business **within specified local limits**, so
  long as the buyer, or anyone deriving title to the goodwill from him, carries on a like business
  there — provided the limits appear **reasonable to the court**, regard being had to the nature of
  the business. Note what the exception does *not* allow: an unlimited restraint as to area, or a
  restraint continuing after the buyer has stopped carrying on the business.
- **Partnership Act exceptions** — section 11(2) (partners may agree that a partner shall not carry
  on any business other than that of the firm while he is a partner), section 36(2) (an outgoing
  partner may agree not to carry on a similar business within specified periods or local limits),
  section 54 (a similar agreement on dissolution) and section 55(3) (on sale of the firm's goodwill).
  All are subject to the restrictions being **reasonable**.
- **Service agreements.** A restraint operating **during** the term of employment — an exclusive
  service covenant, or a covenant not to work for a competitor while employed — is valid
  (*Niranjan Shankar Golikari v Century Spinning*). A restraint operating **after** employment ends
  is hit by section 27 and is void, however short or narrow, save for the protection of trade
  secrets and confidential information.
- **Trade combinations** that regulate business, fix prices or allocate output among members are
  valid so long as they do not restrain a member's trade or tend to create a monopoly.

**Section 28 — restraint of legal proceedings.** An agreement is void to the extent that it:

- **absolutely restricts** a party from enforcing his rights by the usual legal proceedings in the
  ordinary tribunals, or **limits the time** within which he may enforce them; or
- **extinguishes the rights** of a party, or discharges a party from liability, on the expiry of a
  specified period, so as to restrict him from enforcing his rights.

Exceptions: (1) an agreement to refer **future disputes** to arbitration; (2) an agreement to refer
an **existing question** to arbitration; and (3) a contract of **guarantee given to a bank or a
financial institution** may fix a period for enforcement, provided that period is **not less than one
year** from the date the relevant right accrues.

Not hit by section 28: an agreement that merely **chooses one of two competent courts** (an ouster of
jurisdiction clause naming a court that in fact has jurisdiction is valid), and a clause fixing the
**place** of suit among courts that could all otherwise hear it.

**Section 29 — uncertainty.** "Agreements, the meaning of which is not certain, or capable of being
made certain, are void." "A quantity of oil" is void for uncertainty; "one hundred tons of Malabar
coconut oil of the description usually sold by me" is certain enough. Note the second limb — an
agreement whose meaning **can be made certain** (by reference to a market rate, a formula, or a
named arbitrator) is good.

**Section 30 — wagering agreements.** "Agreements by way of wager are void; and no suit shall be
brought for recovering anything alleged to be won on any wager, or entrusted to any person to abide
the result of any game or other uncertain event on which any wager is made."

Essentials of a wager:

1. **An uncertain future event** (or a past event whose result the parties do not know).
2. **Mutual chances of gain and loss** — each party must stand to win on one outcome and lose on the
   other. If only one side can lose, it is not a wager.
3. **No interest other than the stake** — neither party has any interest in the event apart from the
   sum they stand to win or lose. This is what separates insurance from wagering: the insured has an
   **insurable interest** in the subject matter.
4. **Neither party has control** over the event.
5. The **stake must be the sole consideration** for the promise.

**The horse-race carve-out.** The section is expressly not to be deemed to render unlawful a
subscription or contribution, or an agreement to subscribe or contribute, made towards any **plate,
prize or sum of money of the value or amount of five hundred rupees or upwards**, to be awarded to
the winner or winners of any **horse-race**. Below ₹500, the ordinary wagering rule applies.

**Void, but not illegal.** A wagering agreement is void, not unlawful under section 23. Collateral
transactions — a loan to enable a person to pay a betting debt, or a broker's commission on wagering
contracts — are therefore **enforceable in most of India**. In **Gujarat and Maharashtra**, however,
wagers have been declared *illegal* by local legislation, and collateral transactions fail there too.

**Wager compared with contingent contract**: in a wager the uncertain event is the **sole** determining
factor and the parties have **reciprocal** chances of gain and loss, with no other interest; in a
contingent contract the event is **collateral** to a contract the parties have a real interest in
performing. A contract of insurance is a contingent contract; a bet on the same fire is a wager.

## 16. Performance of a contract — who, when and where

**Section 37 — the obligation.** The parties to a contract must either **perform, or offer to
perform**, their respective promises, unless such performance is dispensed with or excused under the
Act or any other law. Promises **bind the representatives of the promisor** on his death, unless a
contrary intention appears from the contract.

**Section 38 — tender (attempted performance).** Where a promisor **offers** to perform and the offer
is not accepted, the promisor is **not responsible for non-performance** and does **not lose his
rights** under the contract. But the tender must be valid, and every essential is examinable:

1. **Unconditional.**
2. Made at a **proper time and place**, and in the usual hours of business.
3. Made in such circumstances that the promisee has a **reasonable opportunity to inspect** and
   satisfy himself that the thing offered is the thing promised.
4. Made to the **promisee or his duly authorised agent**.
5. For the **whole obligation** — a tender of part is no tender.
6. If it is a tender of money, the **exact amount in legal tender** must be actually produced.
7. Where there are several joint promisees, a tender to **any one** of them is sufficient.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The examiner's favourite consequence of a valid tender of goods refused: the promisor
    <strong>keeps his right to sue</strong> for the price or for damages, and is
    <strong>discharged from his own obligation to deliver</strong>. But a refused tender of
    <em>money</em> does not discharge the debt — the debtor still owes it, though he escapes
    liability for interest and costs from that date.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 40 — who must perform.** If it appears from the nature of the case that the parties
intended that the promise should be performed by the **promisor himself**, he must perform it.
Otherwise the promisor **or his representatives may employ a competent person** to perform it.
Contracts involving **personal skill, taste or credit** — to paint a portrait, to sing, to write a
book — must be performed personally and are discharged by the promisor's death.

**Section 41 — accepting performance from a third person.** "When a promisee accepts performance of
the promise from a third person, he cannot afterwards enforce it against the promisor." Note the two
words that do the work: the promisee must have **accepted**. Mere payment by a stranger which the
promisee refuses changes nothing; acceptance is final and irrevocable, even if the promisor never
knew.

**Sections 46 to 50 — time and place.**

| Situation | Rule |
|---|---|
| No time specified, no application by promisee needed (s. 46) | Perform within a **reasonable time**; what is reasonable is a question of fact |
| Day specified, no application needed (s. 47) | The promisor may perform **at any time during the usual hours of business on that day**, at the proper place |
| Day specified, promisee must apply for performance (s. 48) | The application must be made at a **proper place** and **within the usual hours of business** |
| No place fixed, no application needed (s. 49) | The **promisor must apply to the promisee** to appoint a reasonable place, and perform there |
| Manner or time sanctioned by the promisee (s. 50) | Performance in any manner or at any time the promisee **prescribes or sanctions** is good |

**Section 55 — time as the essence of the contract.** Where a party promises to do a thing at or
before a specified time and fails:

- If the intention of the parties was that **time should be of the essence**, the contract (or the
  unperformed part of it) becomes **voidable at the option of the promisee**.
- If time was **not** of the essence, the contract does **not** become voidable, but the promisee is
  entitled to **compensation** for any loss caused by the failure.
- If the promisee **accepts** performance at a time other than that agreed, he **cannot claim
  compensation** for the delay **unless, at the time of acceptance, he gives notice to the promisor of
  his intention to do so**. That last rule decides many scenario questions.

Whether time is of the essence depends on the intention of the parties, gathered from the terms and
the nature of the transaction. In **commercial and mercantile contracts** — especially for goods
whose price fluctuates — time is ordinarily of the essence. In contracts for the **sale of immovable
property**, time is ordinarily **not** of the essence, even where a date is fixed, unless the contract
says so expressly and the circumstances support it.

## 17. Joint promisors, reciprocal promises and appropriation of payments

**Sections 42 to 45 — joint promises.**

- **Section 42 — devolution of joint liabilities.** All joint promisors must, during their joint
  lives, jointly fulfil the promise. After the death of any of them, his **representatives jointly
  with the survivors**; and after the death of the last survivor, the representatives of all
  jointly.
- **Section 43 — any one may be compelled to perform.** "When two or more persons make a joint
  promise, the promisee may, in the absence of express agreement to the contrary, compel **any one or
  more** of such joint promisors to perform the whole of the promise." This makes joint liability in
  India effectively **joint and several**, and it is where India departs from English law.
  - **Right of contribution.** Each of two or more joint promisors may compel every other joint
    promisor to **contribute equally** with himself to the performance of the promise, unless a
    contrary intention appears from the contract.
  - **Sharing a defaulter's share.** If any one of two or more joint promisors makes **default** in
    contribution, the remaining joint promisors must bear the loss arising from that default **in
    equal shares**.
- **Section 44 — release of one joint promisor.** A release of one joint promisor by the promisee
  **does not discharge the other joint promisors**, and it does **not free the released promisor from
  his responsibility to contribute** to the other joint promisors. Both halves are examined; the
  second is the half students forget.
- **Section 45 — devolution of joint rights.** Where a promise is made to several persons jointly,
  the right to claim performance rests with **all of them jointly**; on the death of one, with his
  representative jointly with the survivors.

**Sections 51 to 54 — reciprocal promises.**

- **Section 51.** Where reciprocal promises are to be **simultaneously performed**, no promisor need
  perform unless the other is **ready and willing** to perform his.
- **Section 52 — order of performance.** Where the order is expressly fixed by the contract, that
  order governs; where it is not, they must be performed in the order **the nature of the transaction
  requires**.
- **Section 53 — one party prevents the other.** Where a contract contains reciprocal promises and
  one party **prevents** the other from performing, the contract becomes **voidable at the option of
  the party so prevented**, and he is entitled to **compensation** for any loss he sustains from the
  non-performance.
- **Section 54 — dependent promises.** Where one promise cannot be performed until the other has been
  performed, and the party who should perform first **fails to do so**, he cannot claim performance of
  the other promise and **must compensate** the other party for any loss caused by his default.

Classification of reciprocal promises: **mutual and independent** (each performs without waiting),
**conditional and dependent** (one waits for the other), and **mutual and concurrent** (both to be
performed at the same time).

Two further rules on legality: **section 57** — where a set of reciprocal promises is legal and a
second set illegal, the first set is a **contract** and the second **void**; **section 58** — in an
alternative promise with one legal branch and one illegal branch, only the **legal branch** can be
enforced.

**Sections 59 to 61 — appropriation of payments.** This is the ladder to apply when a debtor owing
several debts pays an amount not matching any of them.

<div class="formula">Step 1 (s. 59) — DEBTOR appropriates: express intimation, or circumstances implying which debt. Step 2 (s. 60) — no debtor appropriation, so the CREDITOR appropriates: to any lawful debt actually due, INCLUDING a time-barred debt. Step 3 (s. 61) — neither appropriates: pay off in ORDER OF TIME, oldest first, whether time-barred or not; if debts are of equal standing, proportionately.</div>

Points that decide questions:

- Under section 59 the creditor must apply the payment as the debtor directs, or refuse the payment
  altogether. He cannot take the money and apply it elsewhere.
- Under section 60 the creditor's discretion extends to a **time-barred** debt but **not** to a
  disputed debt or one that is not lawfully due.
- Section 61's "order of time" is the statutory version of the rule in **Clayton's case** — in a
  running account, the first item on the debit side is discharged by the first item on the credit
  side, first in, first out.
- In all three sections, payments are applied to **principal**, not interest, unless the parties
  agree otherwise; and this whole ladder applies only where the debts are between the **same
  parties** in the same right.

## 18. Discharge of a contract — the six routes

A contract is discharged — the parties stop being bound — in six ways.

1. **By performance.** Actual performance by both parties, or **attempted performance (tender)** under
   section 38 by the party who is willing while the other refuses.
2. **By mutual agreement — sections 62 and 63.** "If the parties to a contract agree to substitute a
   new contract for it, or to rescind or alter it, the original contract need not be performed."
   - **Novation** — a new contract replaces the old, either between the same parties on new terms or
     between one of them and a **new party**. Both parties must consent, and the new contract must be
     **valid and enforceable**; if it is not, the old contract revives.
   - **Rescission** — the contract is cancelled by agreement without a substitute.
   - **Alteration** — one or more terms are changed with mutual consent; the parties stay the same.
   - **Remission — section 63.** "Every promisee may dispense with or remit, wholly or in part, the
     performance of the promise made to him, or may extend the time for such performance, or may
     accept instead of it any satisfaction which he thinks fit." **No consideration is needed** for a
     remission in India — a major departure from English law, where part payment does not discharge
     the whole debt.
   - **Waiver** — abandonment of a right, and **merger** — an inferior right merges into a superior
     one acquired under the same subject matter.
3. **By impossibility of performance — section 56.** See §19.
4. **By lapse of time.** Under the Limitation Act 1963 a contractual claim must generally be brought
   within **three years**; once the period expires the remedy is barred, so the contract is
   effectively discharged although the debt itself survives (which is why section 25(3) exists).
5. **By operation of law.** Death of a party in a contract of personal skill; **insolvency** of a
   party; **merger** of rights; and **unauthorised material alteration** of a written contract by one
   party without the other's consent, which discharges the other party.
6. **By breach.** Actual breach — refusal or failure at the time performance is due — and
   anticipatory breach under section 39. Breach gives the aggrieved party the right to treat the
   contract as at an end and to sue for compensation.

Two more points that appear in one-liners: the **assignment** of a contract transfers the *benefit*
(rights) freely, unless the contract is personal in nature, but the *burden* (liabilities) can be
transferred only by **novation**, which needs the other party's consent. And **sections 64 to 67**
tie off the loose ends — restoration of benefit on rescission of a voidable contract (s. 64),
restoration under a void agreement or a contract that becomes void (s. 65), the mode of communicating
rescission (s. 66), and the promisor's excuse where the promisee **neglects to afford reasonable
facilities** for performance (s. 67).

## 19. Impossibility and frustration — section 56

Section 56 has three paragraphs, and they do different jobs.

1. **Initial impossibility.** "An agreement to do an act impossible in itself is void." The Act's own
   illustration is the one everyone remembers: "A agrees with B to discover treasure by magic. The
   agreement is void."
2. **Supervening impossibility (frustration).** A contract to do an act which, after the contract is
   made, **becomes impossible, or by reason of some event which the promisor could not prevent
   becomes unlawful**, becomes **void when the act becomes impossible or unlawful**.
3. **Compensation for known impossibility.** Where one party **knew, or with reasonable diligence
   might have known**, that the act was impossible or unlawful, and the other did not, the promisor
   must **compensate** the other for the loss sustained through the non-performance.

**Events that DO frustrate a contract**

- **Destruction of the subject matter** — *Taylor v Caldwell*, where a music hall hired for concerts
  burned down before the dates.
- **Death or incapacity** where the contract requires **personal performance**.
- **Change of law or subsequent illegality** — the promise becomes unlawful after the contract is
  made.
- **Non-occurrence of the state of things forming the foundation of the contract** — *Krell v Henry*,
  the room hired to watch a coronation procession that was cancelled.
- **Outbreak of war** or a declaration of war making the counterparty an alien enemy.

**Events that do NOT frustrate a contract**

- **Difficulty of performance.** Performance becoming harder, longer or more inconvenient is not
  impossibility.
- **Commercial impossibility** — a steep rise in raw-material prices or in freight, or the contract
  becoming unprofitable. This is the most heavily examined negative.
- **Default of a third party** on whose work the promisor depended.
- **Strikes, lock-outs and civil disturbance**, unless the contract expressly provides for them.
- **Self-induced impossibility** — the promisor's own act or election.
- **Failure of one of several objects** — if the contract had several purposes and only one fails.
- **Partial impossibility** where the main purpose can still be achieved.

**Consequence — section 65.** "When an agreement is discovered to be void, or when a contract becomes
void, any person who has received any advantage under such agreement or contract is bound to restore
it, or to make compensation for it, to the person from whom he received it." So the advance paid
comes back; the parties are restored, not compensated for their lost bargain.

  <p>
    Sunrise Decor contracts on 1 April 2026 to erect and run a lighting installation at a heritage
    hall for a three-day festival from 10 to 12 October 2026, for ₹18,00,000, of which ₹5,00,000 is
    paid as advance. Consider three separate developments: (i) on 2 September the hall is gutted by
    fire; (ii) on 2 September the price of imported LED fittings doubles, so Sunrise would lose
    ₹4,00,000 on the job; (iii) on 2 September the State Government, citing a public-health order,
    prohibits all indoor gatherings for the rest of the year.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(i) Fire destroys the hall</div>
    Destruction of the subject matter without the fault of either party. The contract becomes
    <strong>void</strong> under the second paragraph of s. 56 from 2 September
    (<em>Taylor v Caldwell</em>). Under <strong>s. 65</strong> the advance of
    <strong>₹5,00,000 must be restored</strong>. Neither party can claim damages, because there is no
    breach — the contract simply ended.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(ii) Prices double</div>
    <strong>Not frustration.</strong> This is commercial impossibility: performance has become
    unprofitable, not impossible. Sunrise must perform and bear the ₹4,00,000 loss; if it walks away
    it is in <strong>breach</strong> and liable in damages under s. 73.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(iii) Government prohibition</div>
    <strong>Supervening illegality</strong> — performance has become unlawful by an event Sunrise

    become?" but "is performance now impossible or unlawful?"

## 20. Breach of contract and the measure of damages — sections 39 and 73

**Section 39 — anticipatory breach.** "When a party to a contract has refused to perform, or disabled
himself from performing, his promise **in its entirety**, the promisee may **put an end to the
contract**, unless he has signified, by words or conduct, his **acquiescence in its continuance**."

So on an anticipatory breach the aggrieved party has an **election**:

- **Rescind at once** and sue for damages immediately, without waiting for the performance date. The
  damages are measured by the difference between the contract price and the market price **on the
  date of the breach**.
- **Keep the contract alive**, wait for the date of performance, and then sue. The risk is real: the
  contract remains alive **for both parties**, so if it is discharged in the meantime by frustration
  under section 56, the guilty party escapes altogether; and damages will then be measured by the
  market price on the **date fixed for performance**.

**Actual breach** occurs at or during the time of performance — a refusal on the due date, or a
failure to perform an instalment where the contract is entire.

**Section 73 — compensation for loss caused by breach.** "When a contract has been broken, the party
who suffers by such breach is entitled to receive, from the party who has broken the contract,
compensation for any loss or damage caused to him thereby, **which naturally arose in the usual course
of things from such breach**, or **which the parties knew, when they made the contract, to be likely
to result from the breach of it**. Such compensation is **not** to be given for any **remote and
indirect** loss or damage sustained by reason of the breach."

That sentence is the rule in **Hadley v Baxendale** in statutory form. In plain words:

<div class="formula">First limb — ORDINARY (general) damages: what any reasonable person would expect to follow from this breach. Always recoverable. Second limb — SPECIAL damages: unusual losses, recoverable ONLY if the special circumstances were made known to the defaulting party WHEN THE CONTRACT WAS MADE. Anything else — remote and indirect — is not recoverable at all.</div>

In *Hadley v Baxendale* itself a mill's broken crankshaft was delayed by the carrier; the mill's lost
profits were disallowed because the carrier had not been told that the mill stood idle without the
shaft.

**The duty to mitigate.** The Explanation to section 73 requires that "the means which existed of
remedying the inconvenience caused by the non-performance of the contract must be taken into account"
in estimating the loss. So the aggrieved party must take **reasonable steps to reduce his loss** — buy
substitute goods, take alternative employment — and **cannot recover the loss he could reasonably have
avoided**. He is, however, entitled to recover the reasonable **costs of mitigating**.

**Kinds of damages**

- **Ordinary or general damages** — flow naturally; the ordinary measure in a sale is the difference
  between the contract price and the **market price on the date of breach**.
- **Special damages** — recoverable only on prior notice of the special circumstances, at the time of
  contracting.
- **Nominal damages** — a token sum where a legal right is infringed but no real loss follows.
- **Vindictive or exemplary damages** — punitive damages are **not** normally awarded for breach of
  contract in India. Two exceptions: **breach of a promise to marry**, and **wrongful dishonour of a
  cheque by a banker** where the customer had sufficient funds — and in the second, the rule is that
  the **smaller the amount of the cheque, the larger the damages**, because the injury to credit is
  greater.
- **Damages for inconvenience, physical discomfort or mental distress** caused by the breach, in the
  narrow cases where the contract's very purpose was comfort or peace of mind.
- **Liquidated damages** — see §21.

  <p>
    On 1 June, Vaibhav Alloys contracts to deliver 500 quintals of a special alloy to Sundaram
    Engineering at <strong>₹4,000 per quintal</strong>, delivery on 1 August. On 1 August Vaibhav
    fails to deliver. The market rate on 1 August is <strong>₹4,700 per quintal</strong>. Sundaram
    delays and finally buys the alloy on 20 September, by when the rate is ₹5,300. Sundaram also had

    can Sundaram recover?
  </p>

  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 2 — the delay in buying (Explanation to s. 73)</div>

    (500 × ₹600 = ₹3,00,000) is a loss it <strong>could reasonably have avoided</strong> and is
    <strong>not recoverable</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 3 — the lost resale profit (second limb of s. 73)</div>
    The shipyard contract was a <strong>special circumstance never communicated</strong> to Vaibhav
    when the contract was made. Under the second limb and <em>Hadley v Baxendale</em>, the lost
    resale profit is <strong>not recoverable</strong>. Had Sundaram disclosed it on 1 June, it would
    have been.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 4 — the idle factory</div>
    Loss from the plant standing idle is <strong>remote and indirect</strong> on these facts, and is
    expressly excluded by the last sentence of s. 73.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Conclusion</div>
    Sundaram recovers <strong>₹3,50,000</strong> — the market-price differential on the date of
    breach — and nothing more.
  </div>
</Callout>

## 21. Liquidated damages and penalty — section 74

Parties often write into the contract the sum payable on breach. **Section 74**: "When a contract has
been broken, if a sum is named in the contract as the amount to be paid in case of such breach, or if
the contract contains any other stipulation by way of penalty, the party complaining of the breach is
entitled, **whether or not actual damage or loss is proved** to have been caused thereby, to receive
from the party who has broken the contract **reasonable compensation not exceeding the amount so
named** or, as the case may be, the penalty stipulated for."

**The Indian position, stated the way the examiner wants it:**

<div class="formula">India draws NO distinction between liquidated damages and a penalty. In both cases the court awards REASONABLE COMPENSATION, and the sum named in the contract operates only as a CEILING — never as an automatic entitlement.</div>

Consequences to carry into the exam:

- If the proved loss is **less** than the named sum, only the proved loss is awarded.
- If the proved loss is **more** than the named sum, the award is **capped at the named sum**.
- The words "whether or not actual damage or loss is proved" mean the claimant need not fail merely
  because the loss is hard to quantify; where loss is genuinely difficult to prove, the named sum
  may be treated as a reasonable pre-estimate. It does **not** mean a claimant who has demonstrably
  suffered **no loss at all** collects the named sum.
- **Compare England**, where a genuine pre-estimate of loss (liquidated damages) is enforced as
  written, and only a sum fixed *in terrorem* (a penalty) is cut down. India cuts both down.
- **Explanation.** A stipulation for **increased interest from the date of default** may be a
  stipulation by way of penalty. (A reasonable rate of interest agreed from the outset is not.)
- **Exception.** Where a person gives a **bail bond, recognizance, or other bond under the law**, or
  gives a bond under the direction of the Government for the performance of a public duty, he is
  liable **on breach to pay the whole sum** mentioned in it. Government bonds are the only place the
  full named sum is recovered automatically.
- **Forfeiture of earnest money** is treated under section 74: a reasonable earnest deposit may be
  forfeited, but a forfeiture that is really a penalty is cut down to reasonable compensation.

## 22. The remaining remedies — quantum meruit, rescission and the Specific Relief Act

**The five remedies for breach**, which is the list to open any remedies answer with:

1. **Rescission of the contract** — section 39 read with **section 75**: "A person who rightfully
   rescinds a contract is entitled to compensation for any damage which he has sustained through the
   non-fulfilment of the contract." So rescinding does not cost you your damages.
2. **Suit for damages** — sections 73 and 74 (§20 and §21).
3. **Suit upon quantum meruit.**
4. **Suit for specific performance.**
5. **Suit for an injunction.**

**Quantum meruit** means "as much as is earned" — a claim for the **value of the work actually done**,
rather than for the contract price or for loss of bargain. It is available:

- where the **contract is discovered to be void, or becomes void** — section 65;
- where something is done **without any intention of doing it gratuitously** and the other party
  enjoys the benefit — section 70;
- where the contract is **divisible** and the party not in default has performed a severable part;
- where an **indivisible** contract has been completely but **badly** performed, the party at fault may
  recover the lump sum **less a deduction** for the bad work;
- where **one party prevents the other** from completing performance, or fails to afford reasonable
  facilities (sections 53 and 67);
- generally, the claim lies at the instance of the party **not in default**; a party who has himself
  broken the contract cannot normally sue on quantum meruit.

**Specific performance and injunction** are governed by the **Specific Relief Act 1963**, not by this
Act — say so in the exam. Specific performance is the court's direction to actually perform the
contract, and after the Specific Relief (Amendment) Act 2018 it is available **as a rule** rather than
as a matter of the court's discretion. It is **not** granted where damages are an adequate remedy,
where the contract is for **personal services**, where the contract requires **continuous supervision**
by the court, or where its terms are uncertain. An **injunction** is the negative counterpart — an
order restraining the breach of a negative stipulation, as where a singer contracted to sing at one
theatre and only there.

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked scenario 6 — quantum meruit (moderate)">
  <p>
    Aarti agrees to write a series of twelve articles for a magazine for a lump sum of ₹1,20,000,
    payable on completion of all twelve. She writes and delivers six, which the magazine publishes.
    The magazine then closes the series and refuses to take any more, and refuses to pay anything,
    saying the contract price was payable only on completion of all twelve. Advise Aarti.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 1 — who broke the contract?</div>
    The magazine did. It <strong>prevented Aarti from completing</strong> performance, which by itself
    makes the contract voidable at her option under <strong>s. 53</strong>, with compensation for the
    loss caused.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 2 — what can she claim?</div>
    Aarti may treat the contract as at an end and sue on <strong>quantum meruit</strong> for the value
    of the six articles actually written and used. She is not confined to the entire-contract rule,
    because it was the other party's breach that stopped completion. On these facts a proportionate
    ₹60,000 for six of twelve articles is the natural starting figure, the court awarding a
    reasonable value for the work done.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Step 3 — or the alternative claim</div>
    She may instead sue for <strong>damages under s. 73</strong> for the loss of her bargain — the
    ₹1,20,000 she would have earned, less the cost and effort saved on the six articles she no longer
    has to write. She elects between the two; she cannot have both.
  </div>
</Callout>

## 23. Contingent contracts — sections 31 to 36

**Section 31 — the definition.** "A **contingent contract** is a contract to do or not to do
something, if some event, **collateral** to such contract, does or does not happen." The Act's
illustration: A contracts to pay B ₹10,000 if B's house is burnt — this is a contingent contract.

Essentials: (i) performance depends on the happening or non-happening of a **future uncertain event**;
(ii) the event is **collateral** to the contract, not the performance itself; (iii) the event must
**not be the mere will of the promisor** (though it may be the act of a party, as in "if you sell me
your land"); (iv) the event must be **uncertain**. A contract of **insurance**, a **guarantee** and an
**indemnity** are all contingent contracts.

**The enforcement rules** — five sections, and the table is what you memorise.

| Section | Contract is contingent on | When enforceable | When void |
|---|---|---|---|
| 32 | An **uncertain future event happening** | Only when and if the event **happens** | If the event becomes **impossible** |
| 33 | An **uncertain future event NOT happening** | When the happening of the event becomes **impossible** | If the event happens |
| 34 | The **future conduct of a living person** | The event is deemed **impossible** when that person does anything making it impossible that he should so act within any definite time, or otherwise than under further contingencies | — |
| 35, first part | An event **happening within a fixed time** | If the event happens within the time | If the time expires without the event, or the event becomes impossible before the time expires |
| 35, second part | An event **NOT happening within a fixed time** | When the fixed time expires without the event happening, **or** earlier if it becomes certain the event will not happen | If the event happens within the time |
| 36 | An **impossible event** | Never | **Void**, whether or not the parties knew of the impossibility when they made the agreement |

Section 36's illustration is the one to quote: "A agrees to pay B 1,000 rupees if two straight lines
should enclose a space. The agreement is void."

**Contingent contract compared with a wagering agreement**

| | Contingent contract | Wagering agreement |
|---|---|---|
| Validity | **Valid** (unless s. 36 applies) | **Void** (s. 30) |
| The uncertain event | **Collateral** to the contract | The **sole** determining factor; the contract is about the bet itself |
| Interest of the parties | The parties have a real interest in the subject matter beyond the stake | Neither has any interest other than the sum won or lost |
| Reciprocal chances | Not necessarily — the loss may fall on one side only | Each party must have **mutual chances** of gain and loss |
| Example | A fire-insurance policy | A bet on whether a particular building will burn |

**Contingent contracts are not conditional in the sense of a condition precedent to formation** — the
contract exists at once; only its **performance** waits on the event. That distinction answers many
one-liners.

## 24. Quasi-contracts — sections 68 to 72

The Act calls these "certain relations resembling those created by contract". There is no offer, no
acceptance and no consideration. The law imposes the obligation to prevent **unjust enrichment** —
the principle expressed in the maxim *nemo debet locupletari ex aliena jactura*, no one should grow
rich out of another's loss.

| Section | Situation | Who pays whom |
|---|---|---|
| 68 | **Necessaries supplied** to a person incapable of contracting (a minor, a person of unsound mind), or to anyone he is legally bound to support | The supplier is reimbursed **from the property of the incapable person** — never personally |
| 69 | A person **interested in the payment of money** which another is bound by law to pay, pays it | He is entitled to be **reimbursed by the other** |
| 70 | A person **lawfully does something for another, or delivers something to him, not intending to do so gratuitously**, and the other **enjoys the benefit** | The other must **compensate** him, or restore the thing |
| 71 | A person **finds goods belonging to another** and takes them into his custody | He is subject to the **same responsibility as a bailee** |
| 72 | A person to whom **money is paid, or a thing delivered, by mistake or under coercion** | He **must repay or return** it |

The illustrations are worth carrying:

> Section 68 — "A supplies B, a lunatic, with necessaries suited to his condition in life. A is
> entitled to be reimbursed from B's property."
>
> Section 70 — "A, a tradesman, leaves goods at B's house by mistake. B treats the goods as his own.
> He is bound to pay A for them."
>
> Section 72 — "A and B jointly owe 100 rupees to C. A alone pays the amount to C, and B, not knowing
> this fact, pays 100 rupees over again to C. C is bound to repay the amount to B."

**Section 70 in detail**, because it is the most examined of the five. Three conditions must all be
satisfied: (i) the act must be done **lawfully**; (ii) it must be done **without any intention of
acting gratuitously**; and (iii) the other person must have **enjoyed the benefit** of it. If B in the
illustration had returned the goods or refused them, nothing would be payable — the benefit must
actually be taken.

**Section 71** — the finder of goods is a **bailee** and therefore must take reasonable care of the
goods, must not use them for his own purpose, and must try to find the true owner. He has a **lien**
on the goods for expenses incurred, and may sue for any reward the owner offered; and he may sell the
goods where the owner cannot be found with reasonable diligence, or refuses to pay the lawful charges,
if the goods are in danger of perishing or the lawful charges amount to two-thirds of their value.

**Section 72** covers money paid under a **mistake of law** as well as a mistake of fact — a point
worth noticing beside section 21, which says a mistake of Indian law does not make a contract
voidable. The two coexist: the contract stands, but money paid under the mistaken belief can still be
recovered.

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

- **"Void" written where "voidable" is meant.** Coercion, undue influence, fraud and misrepresentation
  produce a **voidable** contract. Only bilateral mistake of fact, and the agreements expressly
  declared void, produce a **void** agreement. Half the marks in a scenario question sit on this one
  word.
- **Void agreement versus void contract.** A minor's agreement was **never** a contract (void ab
  initio, s. 2(g)); a frustrated contract **was** one and stopped being one (s. 2(j)). Options are
  routinely written to test this.
- **Cross offers treated as a contract.** Two identical offers crossing in the post form **no
  contract** — neither is an acceptance.
- **Counter offer treated as acceptance.** An acceptance with the smallest variation is a counter
  offer and **destroys** the original offer (*Hyde v Wrench*). Watch for the distinction from a mere
  inquiry, which does not.
- **Posting the revocation instead of delivering it.** For a revocation, posting completes the
  communication only **as against the sender**. It binds the other side when it **reaches** him. The
  offeror's revocation must therefore reach the offeree **before the offeree posts** the acceptance.
- **Privity of consideration confused with privity of contract.** India abolished the first
  (s. 2(d): "the promisee or any other person") and retains the second. Get to the third party
  through the exceptions — trust or charge, family arrangement, acknowledgement, assignment.
- **"Consideration must be adequate."** It must be **real and lawful**, not adequate (Explanation 2 to
  s. 25). Inadequacy is only evidence on the question of free consent.
- **Ratification of a minor's agreement.** Impossible. A fresh promise after majority needs **fresh
  consideration**. And a decree for necessaries runs against the minor's **property**, not against
  the minor.
- **Undue influence answered by "he was of sound mind".** Soundness of mind is irrelevant; s. 16 asks
  about **domination of the will** and an **unfair advantage**, and s. 16(3) shifts the burden once
  the bargain is unconscionable.
- **Applying the "means of discovering the truth" defence to fraud.** The Exception to s. 19 rescues
  a defendant only where consent was caused by **misrepresentation, or by silence** amounting to
  fraud — never where he actively lied.
- **Damages measured at the wrong date.** In the ordinary case, the measure is the market price on
  the **date of breach**, not on the date the aggrieved party got round to buying a substitute.
  Anything extra suffered by delay falls foul of the **duty to mitigate**.
- **Awarding the full penalty under s. 74.** India awards **reasonable compensation not exceeding**
  the sum named. The named sum is a **ceiling**, not an entitlement — except for a bail bond or a
  bond given under a direction of the Government.
- **Calling a hard bargain "frustrated".** A price rise, a strike, or a defaulting supplier is
  **not** impossibility under s. 56. Only impossibility or supervening illegality frustrates.
- **Restitution forgotten.** When a contract becomes void under s. 56, the advance comes back under
  **s. 65**; when a voidable contract is rescinded, benefits go back under **s. 64**; money paid
  under coercion or mistake comes back under **s. 72**. Every scenario answer should end with the
  restitution section.
- **Appropriation done in the wrong order.** Debtor first (s. 59), then creditor (s. 60, and he may
  choose a time-barred debt), then oldest-first (s. 61). Students routinely jump straight to
  oldest-first.
- **Joint promisors treated as liable only for their share.** Section 43 lets the promisee recover the
  **whole** from any one of them; the sharing happens afterwards by contribution, and a defaulter's
  share is borne **equally** by the rest.
- **Releasing one joint promisor thought to discharge the others.** Section 44 says it does not — and
  the released promisor still owes contribution to his co-promisors.
- **Wager and contingent contract mixed up.** The event in a wager is the **whole** contract; in a
  contingent contract it is **collateral** to it. Insurance is contingent, not a wager, because of
  **insurable interest**.

## One-page revision summary

**The ladder (s. 2):** proposal 2(a) → acceptance/promise 2(b) → consideration 2(d) → agreement 2(e)
→ contract 2(h) = agreement + enforceability · void 2(g) · voidable 2(i) · becomes void 2(j).
**Section 10 essentials:** free consent · competent parties · lawful consideration · lawful object ·
not expressly declared void · certainty · possibility · legal formalities where required.

**Offer and acceptance:** invitation to offer — shop display, auction announcement, tender notice,
prospectus, catalogue · cross offers = **no contract** · counter offer **kills** the offer ·
acceptance must be absolute and unqualified (s. 7); prescribed manner not followed → proposer must
object within a reasonable time, else he **accepts the acceptance** · acceptance by performing
conditions (s. 8) · **silence is not acceptance** (*Felthouse v Bindley*) · offer lapses by notice,
time, failure of a condition precedent, or death/insanity **known** to the acceptor (s. 6).

<div class="formula">POSTAL RULE (ss. 4 and 5): Acceptance binds the OFFEROR when POSTED, and the ACCEPTOR when RECEIVED. Offeror may revoke until the acceptance is POSTED; acceptor may revoke until the acceptance is RECEIVED. A revocation binds the other side only when it REACHES him.</div>

**Consideration:** s. 2(d) — at the desire of the promisor, by the promisee **or any other person**,
past/present/future · adequacy irrelevant (Expln 2 to s. 25) · must be real and lawful ·
**s. 25 exceptions**: natural love and affection (written + registered + near relation), past
voluntary service, time-barred debt (written + signed) · plus completed gift, agency (s. 185),
remission (s. 63), bailment, charity acted upon · privity of **consideration** gone; privity of
**contract** survives, with exceptions (trust or charge, family arrangement, acknowledgement,
assignment, covenants running with land, agency).

**Capacity (s. 11):** majority 18 · sound mind at the time of contracting (s. 12) · not disqualified
(alien enemy, foreign sovereign, convict, insolvent, corporation acting ultra vires). **Minor:** void
ab initio (*Mohori Bibee*) · no ratification · may be promisee, agent, and admitted to the benefits of
partnership · no estoppel · necessaries recoverable from his **property** (s. 68) · restitution only
of what is traceable.

**Free consent (s. 14):** coercion (s. 15) · undue influence (s. 16) · fraud (s. 17) ·
misrepresentation (s. 18) · mistake (ss. 20–22). Effects: first four → **voidable** (ss. 19, 19A);
bilateral mistake of fact → **void** (s. 20); unilateral mistake of fact and mistake of Indian law →
**valid** (ss. 21, 22); mistake of foreign law = mistake of fact. Silence is fraud only where there
is a duty to speak, where silence equals speech, in half-truths, and on a change of circumstances.
Exception to s. 19 (means of discovering the truth) saves misrepresentation and silence, never active
fraud.

**Void agreements:** s. 23 unlawful object or consideration (forbidden by law, defeats a law,
fraudulent, injury to person or property, immoral or opposed to public policy) · s. 24 partial
illegality kills an indivisible agreement · s. 26 restraint of marriage (except a minor's) · s. 27
restraint of trade, exceptions: **sale of goodwill** and the Partnership Act sections; during-service
restraints valid, post-service restraints void · s. 28 restraint of legal proceedings, exceptions:
arbitration of future and existing disputes, and bank guarantees with a period of **not less than one
year** · s. 29 uncertainty · s. 30 wagers void, **horse-race prizes of ₹500 or upwards** carved out;
wagers are void but **not illegal**, so collateral transactions survive (except in Gujarat and
Maharashtra).

**Performance:** s. 37 perform or offer to perform · s. 38 valid tender = unconditional, proper time
and place, whole obligation, reasonable opportunity to inspect, exact legal tender for money · s. 40
personal-skill contracts performed personally · s. 41 acceptance of performance from a third person
discharges the promisor · ss. 42–45 joint promisors: any one may be compelled to perform the whole
(s. 43), equal contribution, a defaulter's share shared equally, release of one does not discharge
the others (s. 44) · ss. 46–50 time and place · s. 55 time of the essence — voidable; if not of the
essence — compensation only; accepting late performance forfeits compensation **unless notice is
given at the time of acceptance**.

<div class="formula">APPROPRIATION: s. 59 debtor's intimation → s. 60 creditor's discretion (may take a TIME-BARRED debt) → s. 61 oldest first (Clayton's case), equal-standing debts proportionately.</div>

**Discharge:** performance · agreement (novation, rescission, alteration, **remission — no
consideration needed**, ss. 62–63) · impossibility (s. 56) · lapse of time · operation of law ·
breach. **Frustration yes:** destruction of subject matter, death/incapacity in personal contracts,
supervening illegality, non-occurrence of the contemplated event, war. **Frustration no:** difficulty,
commercial impossibility or price rise, third-party default, strikes, self-induced impossibility,
failure of one of several objects. Restitution on a contract becoming void → **s. 65**.

**Breach and remedies:** s. 39 anticipatory breach — rescind now (damages at the **breach-date**
market) or wait (damages at the **performance-date** market, at the risk of intervening frustration) ·
s. 73 ordinary damages always, special damages **only if the special circumstances were communicated
when the contract was made** (*Hadley v Baxendale*), remote and indirect loss never, and the
Explanation imposes a **duty to mitigate** · exemplary damages only for breach of a promise to marry
and wrongful dishonour of a cheque · s. 74 **reasonable compensation not exceeding** the sum named,
the exception being bonds under the law or a Government direction · s. 75 a person who **rightfully
rescinds** still gets compensation · quantum meruit for the value of work done · specific performance
and injunction under the **Specific Relief Act 1963**.

**Contingent (ss. 31–36):** collateral uncertain event · s. 32 enforce on happening, void on
impossibility · s. 33 enforce when the happening becomes impossible · s. 34 conduct of a living
person · s. 35 fixed-time rules · s. 36 impossible event → **void**, known or not.

**Quasi-contracts (ss. 68–72):** 68 necessaries, from the incapable person's **property** · 69
reimbursement of a person interested in paying another's legal liability · 70 non-gratuitous act
enjoyed by the other · 71 finder of goods = bailee · 72 money paid by **mistake or under coercion** is
repayable.
