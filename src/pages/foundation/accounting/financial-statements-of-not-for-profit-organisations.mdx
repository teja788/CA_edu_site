The Green Park Sports Club in your colony has a treasurer — Mrs. Kulkarni, a retired bank
officer. All year she collects subscriptions, pays the badminton coach, buys shuttlecocks, and
notes every rupee in a cash book. At the AGM the members ask two different questions: **where did
the cash go**, and **did the club live within its means this year?** The first is answered by a
plain cash summary. The second needs proper accrual accounting — members who haven't paid yet,
electricity bills not yet paid, equipment quietly wearing out. This chapter teaches the three
statements that answer both questions.

In the exam this chapter is a dependable scorer: a subscriptions numerical is almost always
present, plus treatment questions (entrance fees, legacies, special funds) and at least one
conceptual Receipts &amp; Payments vs Income &amp; Expenditure question. Every one of those
patterns is worked out below.

## 1. What a not-for-profit organisation is

A **not-for-profit organisation (NPO)** exists to render service, not to earn profit — sports
clubs, schools, hospitals, libraries, temples, charitable societies, trade unions, residents'
welfare associations. Its features:

- **Service motive.** Any excess of income over expenditure is called a **surplus**, not profit —
  and it is **never distributed** to members. It is ploughed back into the organisation.
- **No proprietors.** There are members and a managing committee (or trustees), not owners. So
  there is no Capital account — instead there is a **Capital Fund** (also called General Fund or
  Accumulated Fund).
- **Funding.** Money comes mainly from subscriptions, donations, legacies, grants, and entrance
  fees — not from sale of goods.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The double-entry machinery is exactly what you already know — only the labels change.
    Profit &amp; Loss Account → <strong>Income &amp; Expenditure Account</strong>; Capital →
    <strong>Capital Fund</strong>; Net profit → <strong>Surplus</strong> (excess of income over
    expenditure); Net loss → <strong>Deficit</strong>. Same rules, new nameplates.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. The three statements at a glance

| Statement | What it is | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Receipts &amp; Payments Account | A summary of the cash book for the year | Cash |
| Income &amp; Expenditure Account | The NPO's version of a P&amp;L Account | Accrual |
| Balance Sheet | Assets, liabilities, capital fund and special funds | Accrual |

The whole chapter is the discipline of moving from the first statement (what the treasurer
actually has — cash records) to the second and third (what the members deserve to see — accrual
truth).

## 3. Receipts &amp; Payments Account: the summarised cash book

The **Receipts &amp; Payments (R&amp;P) Account** is nothing more than the cash book, totalled
item-wise for the year. Because it is a summarised cash book, it is a **real account**. Its
features decide a whole family of MCQs:

- **Starts and ends with cash.** Opens with the opening cash and bank balances, closes with the
  closing balances. (A favourable closing balance is carried down on the payments side; a
  closing balance appearing on the **receipts** side is a **bank overdraft**.)
- **Every receipt and payment goes in** — **capital or revenue**, it does not care. Subscriptions
  and a new billiards table sit in the same statement.
- **Any period's money goes in.** Subscription received this year for last year or next year is
  still cash received this year — it is recorded in full.
- **Cash only.** No outstanding incomes or expenses, no credit purchases, and **no non-cash
  items** — depreciation can never appear in an R&amp;P Account.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The closing balance of the R&amp;P Account is <strong>cash and bank at the year end — not
    surplus</strong>. A club can hold plenty of cash while running a deficit (say a legacy came
    in), or run a surplus with little cash (members haven't paid up). The R&amp;P Account can
    never tell you whether the club lived within its means.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Income &amp; Expenditure Account: the accrual statement

The **Income &amp; Expenditure (I&amp;E) Account** is the NPO's Profit &amp; Loss Account — a
**nominal account** prepared on the **accrual basis**. Expenditure sits on the debit side, income
on the credit side, and the balance is a **surplus** (income exceeds expenditure) or **deficit**,
transferred to the Capital Fund in the balance sheet.

Two filters decide what gets in, and they are the heart of every conversion question:

1. **Revenue items only.** No capital receipts (legacy, sale proceeds of an asset, specific
   donations), no capital payments (purchase of assets, investments).
2. **Current year only.** Each item is adjusted for outstanding and advance amounts at **both**
   ends of the year, so only this year's income and this year's expense remain.

And one addition: because the statement is accrual-based, **non-cash charges come in** —
depreciation, and loss on sale of an asset. It has no opening or closing cash balances.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Memorise the mirror image: R&amp;P = <strong>any period, any nature, cash only</strong>;
    I&amp;E = <strong>current period, revenue nature, accrual</strong> (non-cash items included).
    Nearly every theory MCQ on the two statements is one of these six phrases, straight or
    twisted.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Subscriptions: the computation the examiner loves

Subscriptions are the main revenue income of most NPOs, and the examiner's favourite numerical.
The task is always: from **cash received during the year**, work out the **income belonging to
the year**. Apply the belongs-to-this-year test to each adjustment:

- **Outstanding at start** — last year's income, received in this year's cash: **deduct**.
- **Outstanding at end** — this year's income, cash not yet received: **add**.
- **Advance at start** — this year's income, received in last year's cash: **add**.
- **Advance at end** — next year's income, sitting in this year's cash: **deduct**.

<div class="formula">Subscription income for the year = Received during the year − Outstanding at start + Outstanding at end + Advance at start − Advance at end</div>

Notice the pattern: anything **at start** carries the opposite sign of the same thing **at end**.
If you can say *why* each sign is what it is (the belongs-to-this-year test), you cannot be
trapped by any permutation — including the reverse question, where income is given and cash
received must be found (just re-arrange the same equation).

The same computation can be laid out as a **Subscriptions Account** — opening balances brought
down, cash credited, closing balances carried down, and income for the year emerging as the
balancing figure. Worked example 1 shows both routes on the same numbers; in the exam, use the
equation for MCQs and the account when a question demands the ledger format.

## 6. Consumables consumed: sports material, medicines, stationery

A club buys shuttlecocks; a hospital buys medicines; a school buys chalk. The I&amp;E Account
shows not what was *bought*, but what was **consumed** during the year. Two steps:

<div class="formula">Credit purchases = Payments to creditors during the year + Closing creditors − Opening creditors</div>
<div class="formula">Amount consumed = Opening stock + Total purchases (cash + credit) − Closing stock</div>

Stock of consumables at the year end appears as an asset in the balance sheet; creditors for
consumables appear as a liability. Worked example 2 runs the full chain.

## 7. How each special receipt is treated

NPO questions revolve around a fixed cast of receipts. Learn the treatment and the reason:

| Item | Treatment | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Subscriptions | Income (adjusted, §5) | Recurring revenue receipt |
| Entrance / admission fees | **Income**, unless the question directs capitalisation | SM convention — recurring in practice |
| Life membership fees | **Capitalise** — add to capital fund (never income of the receipt year) | One payment buys services for life |
| General donation | Income | No strings attached — revenue receipt |
| Specific donation (e.g. for building) | Credit to that **specific fund** — capital receipt, whatever the amount | Tied to a purpose, not to the year |
| Legacy | **Capitalise** (specific legacy → that fund) | Received under a will — non-recurring |
| Government grant — maintenance | Income | Meets revenue expenses |
| Government grant — building | Capitalise (building fund) | Meets capital cost |
| Sale of old sports material / newspapers | Income — full proceeds | Consumables already expensed |
| Sale of a fixed asset | Only **profit or loss** to I&amp;E; proceeds are a capital receipt | The asset was never an expense |
| Honorarium paid | Expenditure in I&amp;E | Payment for services of a non-employee |

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Two conventions here vary between books, and the SM line is what the exam follows:
    <strong>entrance fees are treated as income unless the question says otherwise</strong>, and
    a legacy is capitalised — the SM notes that a <strong>small general legacy may be treated as
    income</strong>. If a question is silent, state the assumption you used; in MCQs, the SM
    convention is the intended answer.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The classic error of this chapter: a club sells an old cycle (book value ₹20,000) for
    ₹14,500, and the student credits ₹14,500 to I&amp;E as income. Wrong — only the
    <strong>loss of ₹5,500</strong> is debited to I&amp;E. Contrast: sale of old
    <strong>sports material</strong> (a consumable, already expensed when consumed) IS income in
    full. Asset → result only; consumable scrap → full proceeds.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. Special funds: tournament, prize and building funds

When money is earmarked — a Tournament Fund, a Prize Fund, a Building Fund — the club keeps that
money's story **out of the I&amp;E Account**. This is **fund-based accounting**:

- **Incomes of the fund** (donations for the fund, interest on the fund's investments) are
  **credited (added) to the fund**, not to I&amp;E.
- **Expenses of the fund** (tournament expenses, prizes awarded) are **debited (deducted) from
  the fund**, not from I&amp;E.
- The net fund balance appears on the **liabilities side** of the balance sheet; the fund's
  investments appear on the assets side.
- **If expenses exceed the fund**, the excess is debited to the I&amp;E Account — a fund can be
  exhausted, but it is never shown negative.
- **Building fund:** when construction money is actually spent, the building appears as an asset,
  and the amount utilised is **transferred from the Building Fund to the Capital Fund** — the
  earmarking has served its purpose.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    One-line test the examiner sets again and again: tournament expenses of ₹70,000 when the
    Tournament Fund holds ₹58,000 (including the year's tournament receipts) → ₹58,000 dies
    inside the fund, and only the excess <strong>₹12,000 is debited to I&amp;E</strong>. Sending
    the whole ₹70,000 to I&amp;E is the planted wrong option.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. The Balance Sheet and the capital fund

An NPO's balance sheet looks familiar: assets on one side; liabilities, special funds and the
**Capital Fund** on the other. Two computations matter:

**Opening capital fund.** Questions rarely hand it to you. Prepare an **opening statement of
affairs** — list the assets and liabilities existing on the first day — and the capital fund is
the **balancing figure**:

<div class="formula">Opening capital fund = Total assets at start − Total liabilities at start</div>

Don't forget the sneaky opening items: outstanding subscriptions (asset), subscriptions received
in advance (liability), accrued interest, stock of consumables, outstanding expenses.

**Closing capital fund.** Roll the fund forward:

<div class="formula">Closing capital fund = Opening capital fund + Surplus (or − Deficit) + Items capitalised during the year (life membership fees, legacies, entrance fees if capitalised)</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Watch the sides: <strong>outstanding subscriptions are an asset</strong> (members owe the
    club); <strong>subscriptions received in advance are a liability</strong> (the club owes next
    year's service). Swapping them corrupts both the statement of affairs and the closing balance
    sheet — a favourite MCQ distractor.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. Converting Receipts &amp; Payments into Income &amp; Expenditure

Every full-length problem is this conversion. Run the same four steps, in order:

1. **Drop the cash frame.** Opening and closing cash/bank balances belong to the R&amp;P Account
   only — they never enter I&amp;E.
2. **Strip capital items.** Remove capital receipts (legacies, life membership, specific
   donations, sale proceeds of assets, building grants) and capital payments (assets bought,
   investments made). Park them for the balance sheet.
3. **Re-time every revenue item.** Adjust each income and expense for outstanding and
   advance/prepaid amounts at **both ends**:

   <div class="formula">Expense for the year = Paid during the year − Outstanding at start + Outstanding at end + Prepaid at start − Prepaid at end</div>

4. **Add the non-cash charges.** Depreciation, and loss on sale of assets (or credit the profit
   on sale). These never touched cash, so the R&amp;P Account never showed them.

The result balances to a surplus or deficit — carry it to the capital fund, then build the
closing balance sheet.

## Fully worked examples

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — subscriptions, all four adjustments (the examiner's favourite)">
  <p>
    Green Park Sports Club received subscriptions of ₹1,84,000 in cash during 2025–26. Members
    owed ₹18,000 on 1 April 2025 and ₹22,000 on 31 March 2026. Subscriptions received in advance
    were ₹6,000 on 1 April 2025 and ₹9,000 on 31 March 2026. Find the subscription income for
    2025–26.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Equation route</div>
    Income = 1,84,000 − 18,000 (opening outstanding: last year's money) + 22,000 (closing
    outstanding: this year's money, not yet in) + 6,000 (opening advance: this year's money,
    received earlier) − 9,000 (closing advance: next year's money) = <strong>₹1,85,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Ledger-account route (same answer)</div>
    <em>Subscriptions Account</em><br />
    Dr: To Outstanding Subscriptions b/d 18,000 · To Income &amp; Expenditure A/c (balancing
    figure) <strong>1,85,000</strong> · To Advance Subscriptions c/d 9,000 → total 2,12,000<br />
    Cr: By Advance Subscriptions b/d 6,000 · By Bank A/c 1,84,000 · By Outstanding Subscriptions
    c/d 22,000 → total 2,12,000<br />
    Both sides agree at ₹2,12,000, so the balancing figure ₹1,85,000 is the income.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — sports material consumed, with creditors (moderate)">
  <p>
    Green Park paid ₹64,000 to creditors for sports material during 2025–26 and bought a further
    ₹12,000 for cash. Stock of sports material: ₹9,000 on 1 April 2025, ₹11,500 on 31 March 2026.
    Creditors for sports material: ₹7,000 on 1 April 2025, ₹10,500 on 31 March 2026. How much
    sports material was consumed?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Credit purchases first</div>
    Credit purchases = 64,000 + 10,500 (closing creditors) − 7,000 (opening creditors) =
    67,500<br />
    Total purchases = 67,500 + 12,000 (cash) = <strong>₹79,500</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Then consumption</div>
    Consumed = 9,000 (opening stock) + 79,500 − 11,500 (closing stock) =
    <strong>₹77,000</strong> to the debit of I&amp;E. Closing stock ₹11,500 and creditors
    ₹10,500 go to the balance sheet.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — Receipts &amp; Payments to Income &amp; Expenditure (moderate-hard)">
  <p>
    Sunrise Sports Club's R&amp;P Account for the year ended 31 March 2026 — Receipts: balance
    b/d ₹22,000; subscriptions ₹1,50,000; entrance fees ₹20,000; sale of old sports material
    ₹4,000; sale of old furniture (book value ₹9,000) ₹7,500; interest on investments ₹6,000.
    Payments: salaries ₹72,000; rent ₹33,000; sports material purchased ₹48,000; new furniture
    ₹40,000; balance c/d ₹16,500. Adjustments: subscriptions outstanding — ₹6,000 at start (all
    received during the year), ₹10,000 at end; salaries outstanding at end ₹6,000; rent includes
    ₹3,000 paid for April 2026; stock of sports material ₹5,000 at start and ₹8,000 at end;
    depreciate furniture ₹4,500; treat entrance fees as income. Prepare the I&amp;E Account.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Strip the capital items</div>
    New furniture ₹40,000 → asset, not expenditure. Sale proceeds of furniture ₹7,500 → not
    income; only the result goes to I&amp;E: 7,500 − 9,000 = <strong>loss ₹1,500</strong>.
    Opening and closing cash balances stay in the R&amp;P Account.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Re-time the revenue items</div>
    Subscriptions: 1,50,000 − 6,000 + 10,000 = 1,54,000<br />
    Salaries: 72,000 + 6,000 outstanding = 78,000<br />
    Rent: 33,000 − 3,000 prepaid = 30,000<br />
    Sports material consumed: 5,000 + 48,000 − 8,000 = 45,000
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — The Income &amp; Expenditure Account</div>
    Income: subscriptions 1,54,000 + entrance fees 20,000 + sale of old sports material 4,000 +
    interest 6,000 = 1,84,000<br />
    Expenditure: salaries 78,000 + rent 30,000 + sports material consumed 45,000 + depreciation
    4,500 + loss on sale of furniture 1,500 = 1,59,000<br />
    <strong>Surplus = 1,84,000 − 1,59,000 = ₹25,000</strong>, carried to the capital fund.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — opening capital fund, then the closing balance sheet (moderate)">
  <p>
    Jan Kalyan Library's position on 1 April 2025: cash ₹14,000; books ₹1,20,000; furniture
    ₹36,000; subscriptions outstanding ₹8,000; salaries outstanding ₹4,000; subscriptions
    received in advance ₹2,500. During 2025–26 the library earned a surplus of ₹23,500 and
    received life membership fees of ₹15,000. Find the opening and closing capital fund.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Opening statement of affairs</div>
    Assets: 14,000 + 1,20,000 + 36,000 + 8,000 (outstanding subscriptions are an asset) =
    1,78,000<br />
    Liabilities: 4,000 + 2,500 (advance subscriptions are a liability) = 6,500<br />
    Opening capital fund (balancing figure) = 1,78,000 − 6,500 = <strong>₹1,71,500</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Roll the fund forward</div>
    Closing capital fund = 1,71,500 + 23,500 (surplus) + 15,000 (life membership fees —
    capitalised, never income) = <strong>₹2,10,000</strong>
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — tournament fund, surplus and shortfall (hard: fund-based accounting)">
  <p>
    Green Park's Tournament Fund stood at ₹60,000 on 1 April 2025. During 2025–26 the club
    received donations for the tournament of ₹25,000 and interest of ₹4,800 on tournament fund
    investments, and spent ₹78,000 on conducting the tournament. (a) Show the fund at the year
    end. (b) What changes if the expenses had been ₹95,000?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Case (a): the fund survives</div>
    Fund = 60,000 + 25,000 + 4,800 − 78,000 = <strong>₹11,800</strong>, shown on the liabilities
    side. Nothing touches the I&amp;E Account — incomes and expenses of the fund live inside the
    fund.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Case (b): the fund is exhausted</div>
    Available = 60,000 + 25,000 + 4,800 = 89,800; expenses 95,000.<br />
    Excess = 95,000 − 89,800 = <strong>₹5,200 debited to I&amp;E</strong>. The fund disappears
    from the balance sheet — it is never shown as a negative figure.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

- **Sign slips in the subscriptions equation.** Adding the opening outstanding (instead of
  deducting) or treating the closing advance as income are the two planted wrong options in
  nearly every subscriptions MCQ. Run the belongs-to-this-year test on each figure.
- **Full sale proceeds of a fixed asset credited to I&amp;E.** Only the profit or loss on sale
  goes to I&amp;E; the proceeds are a capital receipt. (Sale of old *sports material* or
  newspapers, in contrast, is income in full.)
- **R&amp;P balance read as surplus.** The R&amp;P closing balance is cash and bank, nothing
  more. Surplus lives only in the I&amp;E Account.
- **Capital items smuggled into I&amp;E.** A new table tennis table, a legacy, life membership
  fees — none of these is income or expenditure of the year.
- **Tournament expenses debited to I&amp;E while a Tournament Fund exists.** Fund expenses die
  inside the fund; only the excess over the fund reaches I&amp;E.
- **Depreciation skipped** "because no cash was paid". I&amp;E is accrual — depreciation and
  loss on sale must be charged even though the R&amp;P Account never shows them.
- **Outstanding subscriptions shown as a liability** (or advance subscriptions as an asset).
  Members owing the club = asset; the club owing service = liability.
- **Life membership fees credited to income** in the year of receipt. They are capitalised —
  added to the capital fund.

## One-page revision summary

**The cast:** NPO = service motive, surplus never distributed, Capital Fund instead of Capital ·
R&amp;P Account = summarised cash book (real account): any period, any nature, cash only, starts
and ends with cash-bank balances · I&amp;E Account = accrual P&amp;L (nominal account): current
year, revenue only, non-cash items included, result = surplus/deficit → capital fund · Balance
sheet: special funds shown separately on the liabilities side.

**Formulas:**

<div class="formula">Subscription income = Received − Outstanding at start + Outstanding at end + Advance at start − Advance at end</div>
<div class="formula">Expense for the year = Paid − Outstanding at start + Outstanding at end + Prepaid at start − Prepaid at end</div>
<div class="formula">Credit purchases = Payments to creditors + Closing creditors − Opening creditors</div>
<div class="formula">Consumed = Opening stock + Purchases − Closing stock</div>
<div class="formula">Opening capital fund = Assets at start − Liabilities at start</div>
<div class="formula">Closing capital fund = Opening fund + Surplus (− Deficit) + Amounts capitalised</div>

**Treatments that answer MCQs:** entrance fees → income unless directed otherwise (SM
convention) · life membership → capitalise · legacy → capitalise (small general legacy may be
income per SM) · specific donation/grant → that fund, any amount · general donation,
maintenance grant → income · sale of old sports material → income in full · sale of asset →
only profit/loss to I&amp;E · honorarium → expense · fund incomes/expenses → inside the fund;
excess expenses → I&amp;E · building fund → transfer amount utilised to capital fund ·
outstanding subscriptions = asset; advance subscriptions = liability · opening capital fund =
balancing figure of the opening statement of affairs.
