Meena runs a general store in Nashik. Every day money moves: stock arrives from wholesalers,
customers pay at the counter, the electricity bill goes out, cash gets deposited in the bank.
This chapter is the assembly line that turns each of those events into dependable accounts —
record it first in a **journal** or day book, sort it into the **ledger**, prove the arithmetic
with a **trial balance**, and when a mistake slips through, **rectify** it without tearing pages
out of the books.

In the exam this is the workhorse chapter: expect quick classification MCQs (which account, which
rule), short numericals on cash book and ledger balances, and at least one rectification question —
the examiner's favourite, because one error tests journal, ledger and trial balance in a single
stroke. Learn the five worked patterns below and every variant becomes familiar.

## 1. One transaction, two effects: the double entry system

Buy goods worth ₹5,000 for cash, and two things happen at the same instant: goods come in, cash
goes out. Every transaction has this two-sided character — a benefit received and a benefit
given. **Double entry** records both sides, every time: one account is **debited** and another is
**credited** with an equal amount. Nothing is ever recorded only once.

- **Debit (Dr)** — an entry on the *left* side of an account.
- **Credit (Cr)** — an entry on the *right* side of an account.

Neither word means good or bad, gain or loss. They are directions, nothing more — which direction
increases an account depends on the *kind* of account (§2).

Because every debit has an equal and opposite credit, the totals of all debits and all credits in
the books must always agree — this is the fact the trial balance (§8) exploits. The same idea in
equation form:

<div class="formula">Assets = Liabilities + Capital (every transaction keeps this equation in balance)</div>

When Meena starts her store with ₹3,00,000 of her own money, assets (cash) rise by ₹3,00,000 and
capital rises by ₹3,00,000 — the equation balances. When she buys a fridge for ₹40,000 cash, one
asset rises and another falls — still balanced. There is no transaction that breaks it.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Double entry is a complete, self-checking <strong>system</strong>. Its half-hearted cousin —
    recording only some transactions or only one aspect — is called <strong>single entry</strong>
    (incomplete records), and it offers no arithmetic check at all. Exam one-liner: every debit
    has an equal and corresponding credit.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. Kinds of accounts and the rules of debit and credit

Two classification maps exist. Both describe the same city; the exam tests both.

**The traditional (British) map — three kinds of accounts:**

| Kind | What it covers | Sub-types |
|---|---|---|
| Personal | Accounts of persons and bodies the business deals with | Natural (Ravi's A/c), artificial (a firm, a bank, a club), representative (outstanding salaries, prepaid insurance) |
| Real | Properties and possessions of the business | Tangible (cash, machinery, stock), intangible (goodwill, patents) |
| Nominal | Expenses, losses, incomes and gains | Rent A/c, wages A/c, commission received A/c |

Each kind has its **golden rule**:

<div class="formula">Personal: Debit the receiver, credit the giver</div>
<div class="formula">Real: Debit what comes in, credit what goes out</div>
<div class="formula">Nominal: Debit all expenses and losses, credit all incomes and gains</div>

A **representative personal** account deserves a second look: Outstanding Salaries A/c *looks*
like an expense, but it actually stands for the employees to whom salary is owed — a group of
persons — so it is personal, not nominal. Prepaid insurance similarly represents the insurer who
owes the business cover for the months paid in advance.

**The modern (accounting equation) map — five classes**, each with a fixed debit/credit effect:

| Class | Increase | Decrease |
|---|---|---|
| Asset | Debit | Credit |
| Liability | Credit | Debit |
| Capital | Credit | Debit |
| Income / gain | Credit | Debit |
| Expense / loss | Debit | Credit |

Both maps always produce the same entry. Goods bought for cash: traditional says debit Purchases
(nominal — an expense of buying), credit Cash (real — it goes out); modern says expense up →
debit, asset down → credit. Same result.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Capital A/c and Drawings A/c are <strong>personal accounts</strong> — they belong to the
    owner, a person — not nominal accounts. And a bank account is a personal account (the bank is
    an artificial person), not a real account, even though it feels like money. These two
    classifications are the most-missed MCQs in this section.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. The journal: where every transaction enters first

The **journal** is the book of original (first) entry: transactions go in **chronologically**, as
they happen, each with its debit, its credit and a one-line **narration** explaining the event.
From here the amounts are copied — *posted* — into the ledger. The standard format:

| Date | Particulars | L.F. | Debit ₹ | Credit ₹ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2026 Apr 5 | Purchases A/c … Dr | 14 | 40,000 | |
| | To Cash A/c | 2 | | 40,000 |
| | (Being goods bought for cash) | | | |

**L.F.** (ledger folio) records the page of the ledger where each account lives — the
cross-reference that lets an auditor walk from journal to ledger and back.

**Compound entries.** When one event touches more than two accounts, write a single entry with
several debits or credits. Meena buys a weighing machine for ₹24,000, pays ₹10,000 by cheque and
owes the rest: debit Machinery ₹24,000, credit Bank ₹10,000 *and* credit the supplier ₹14,000.
Total debits still equal total credits — that discipline never relaxes.

**The opening entry.** On the first day of a new year, the closing balances of the old year are
brought in through one compound entry: debit every asset, credit every liability, and credit the
balancing figure to Capital:

<div class="formula">Opening capital = Total assets − Total liabilities</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The journal records <strong>both aspects together</strong> in one place; the ledger shows
    each account's story separately. When a question asks where a transaction is
    <strong>first</strong> recorded, the answer is the journal (or the relevant subsidiary book,
    §5) — never the ledger, and never the trial balance.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. The ledger: posting and balancing

The **ledger** is the principal book: one account per head, each shaped like a **T** with a debit
(left) and credit (right) side. **Posting** copies each journal line into the affected accounts —
and the naming convention flips: in the account being debited, write the *other* account's name
prefixed **"To"**; in the account being credited, write the other account's name prefixed
**"By"**. The prefixes are pure tradition — they carry no meaning beyond marking the side.

**Balancing an account**, step by step:

1. Total both sides (rough work).
2. Find the difference between the two totals.
3. Write the difference on the **lighter** side as **"Balance c/d"** (carried down) — now both
   sides total the same, and the account is ruled off.
4. Bring the same figure down on the **opposite** side next period as **"Balance b/d"**
   (brought down).

If the debit side was heavier, the account has a **debit balance** (the b/d appears on the debit
side). Assets and expenses normally show debit balances; liabilities, capital and incomes show
credit balances.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Not every account is balanced. <strong>Nominal accounts are not carried forward</strong> —
    at year end they are closed by transfer to the Trading and Profit &amp; Loss Account. Only
    personal and real accounts carry balances into the next year (and those balances feed the
    opening entry, §3).
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Subsidiary books: splitting the journal

A busy firm cannot push hundreds of daily transactions through one journal — one book means one
clerk and an unmanageable queue. So the journal is split into **subsidiary books** (books of
original entry), each taking one high-volume class of transaction, with the leftovers going to
the **journal proper**:

| Book | What goes in | Source document |
|---|---|---|
| Purchases book | Credit purchases of goods (things traded in) | Inward invoice |
| Sales book | Credit sales of goods | Outward invoice |
| Purchase returns (returns outward) book | Goods sent back to suppliers | Debit note |
| Sales returns (returns inward) book | Goods customers send back | Credit note |
| Cash book (§6) | All cash and bank movements | Receipts, pay-in slips |
| Journal proper | Everything the other books cannot take | Vouchers |

The four goods books take **only credit transactions of goods**. Two filters, both strict:

- **Cash purchases and cash sales go to the cash book**, never to the purchases or sales book.
- **Assets bought or sold on credit go to the journal proper** — a machine bought on credit is
  not "purchases", because machines are not what the firm trades in.

**Trade discount vs cash discount** — the distinction the examiner never tires of:

- **Trade discount** is a deduction from the *list price* (for bulk orders, for being a
  dealer). It is shown on the invoice and the transaction is recorded **net** — trade discount
  itself never appears in any account.
- **Cash discount** is a reward for *prompt payment*. It IS recorded — Discount Allowed A/c (a
  loss, on receipts) or Discount Received A/c (a gain, on payments) — and it appears in the
  discount columns of the cash book (§6).

**The journal proper** keeps: opening entries, closing entries, rectification entries (§9),
adjusting entries, transfer entries, credit purchases and sales of assets, and rare items such as
goods lost by fire or given as charity.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    "Furniture bought on credit from Modern Furnishers, recorded in the purchases book" — this
    single line hides <strong>two</strong> exam points: the entry belongs in the journal proper,
    and if it is left in the purchases book, Furniture ends up debited to Purchases A/c — an
    <strong>error of principle</strong> (§9) that overstates expenses and understates assets.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. The cash book: single, double and triple column

The cash book is a hybrid: it is a **book of original entry AND the ledger account** for cash
(and bank) at the same time — cash transactions are recorded here first, and no separate Cash A/c
is opened in the ledger. Receipts go on the debit side, payments on the credit side.

Three sizes:

1. **Single column** — cash column only.
2. **Double column** — cash and bank columns on each side.
3. **Triple column** — cash, bank *and* discount columns on each side: discount **allowed** on
   the debit (receipts) side, discount **received** on the credit (payments) side.

**Contra entries.** When money moves *between* cash and bank — cash deposited into the bank, or
cash withdrawn from the bank **for office use** — both the debit and the credit of the entry sit
inside the cash book itself. Mark both legs with **"C"** in the L.F. column; nothing is posted to
any ledger account. (Cash withdrawn for the owner's *personal* use is not a contra — that is
Drawings, a real posting.)

**Discount columns are memoranda columns.** They are **totalled, never balanced** — the discount
column is not an account. The period's total of the discount-allowed column is posted in one
stroke to the debit of Discount Allowed A/c; the discount-received total to the credit of
Discount Received A/c.

**Balances.** The cash column can only ever show a **debit balance** (you cannot pay out more
physical cash than you have). The bank column can show either — a credit balance in the bank
column is an **overdraft**.

Cheques received and deposited into the bank the same day are entered straight into the bank
column. A cheque held back is treated as cash first, with a contra entry when it is later banked.

## 7. Petty cash and the imprest system

Stamps, autos, tea, courier — payments too small for the main cash book. A **petty cashier**
handles them from a fixed float. Under the **imprest system**:

1. The chief cashier advances a round sum — the **imprest** (say ₹6,000) — at the start of the
   period.
2. The petty cashier pays small expenses from it, recording each in an **analytical petty cash
   book** (one column per expense head: postage, cartage, stationery…).
3. At the period's end the chief cashier reimburses **exactly what was spent** — restoring the
   float to the original imprest.

<div class="formula">Reimbursement = total petty expenses of the period (so the float returns to the imprest amount)</div>

The system is self-policing: the petty cashier always holds cash + vouchers equal to the imprest,
the chief cashier reviews every voucher before reimbursing, and the analytical columns give
ready-made totals for posting each expense head to the ledger.

## 8. Trial balance: the arithmetic checkpoint

A **trial balance** is a *statement* (not an account, and not part of the double entry itself)
listing every ledger balance — debit balances in one column, credit balances in the other — **on
a particular date**. Because every debit in the system has an equal credit, the two columns must
agree. Its purposes: check arithmetical accuracy, provide the raw material for the final
accounts, and give a bird's-eye view of every account at once.

Three ways to prepare it: the **balance method** (list closing balances — the standard method),
the **total method** (list the gross totals of each side of every account), and the **compound**
method (both sets of columns). Exams assume the balance method unless told otherwise.

Which side does a balance go? The pattern follows §4:

| Debit column | Credit column |
|---|---|
| Assets — cash, bank, debtors, stock, machinery | Liabilities — creditors, loans, bank overdraft |
| Expenses and losses — purchases, wages, rent paid | Capital |
| Drawings | Incomes and gains — sales, commission received |
| Returns inward (sales returns) | Returns outward (purchase returns) |

**Errors a trial balance catches** — anything that disturbs one side only (one-sided errors):
a subsidiary book overcast or undercast, an amount posted to the wrong side, a wrong amount
posted on one side, one aspect not posted at all, an account balanced wrongly.

**Errors it never catches** — anything that hits both sides equally: complete omission of a
transaction, errors of principle, compensating errors, posting to the *wrong account* on the
right side with the right amount, and recording a wrong amount in the book of original entry
(both postings carry the same wrong figure).

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    An agreed trial balance proves <strong>arithmetical accuracy only</strong>. It is never
    conclusive proof that the books are correct — five whole families of error (§9) leave it
    perfectly balanced. This exact sentence, as an MCQ, appears attempt after attempt.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Rectification of errors

Never erase, never overwrite. An error, once found, is cancelled by a fresh **rectification
entry**. First, know the enemy — four classes:

| Class | Meaning | Example | TB disturbed? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Error of principle | Right amount, wrong *class* of account — capital and revenue confused | Repairs debited to Machinery A/c | No |
| Error of omission — complete | Transaction skipped entirely | Credit sale never recorded anywhere | No |
| Error of omission — partial | One aspect recorded, the other missed | Sale entered in sales book, never posted to the customer | Yes |
| Error of commission | Wrong amount, wrong account of the *same* class, wrong side, or wrong casting | Sale to Mohan posted to Sohan; purchases book overcast | One-sided ones, yes |
| Compensating errors | Two or more errors cancelling each other's effect | An excess debit of ₹500 offset elsewhere by an excess credit of ₹500 | No |

The working distinction is **one-sided vs two-sided**: a one-sided error disturbs a single
account (or a single side) and throws the trial balance out; a two-sided error keeps debits equal
to credits and the trial balance agreed.

**Stage 1 — error found before the trial balance.** A two-sided error is fixed with a normal
journal entry (debit the account short of debits, credit the account short of credits). A
one-sided error needs no journal entry at this stage — post the correction directly into the one
affected account with a note ("difference in amount posted on…").

**Stage 2 — error found after the trial balance, suspense account in use.** When the trial
balance refuses to agree and the final accounts cannot wait, the difference is parked in a
**Suspense Account**:

<div class="formula">Suspense goes on the SHORTER column of the trial balance — debit column short → suspense debit balance; credit column short → suspense credit balance</div>

Now every **one-sided** rectification runs *through* suspense: correct the affected account, and
let Suspense absorb the other leg. Two-sided errors are still rectified by ordinary entries that
never touch Suspense. When every error is found, the Suspense Account closes to nil — the
built-in proof that the hunt is over.

**Stage 3 — error found in the next year, after final accounts.** Last year's nominal accounts
are already closed into last year's profit, so they cannot be debited or credited again. Their
place is taken by a **Profit &amp; Loss Adjustment Account**: rectification entries that would
have touched a nominal account touch P&amp;L Adjustment instead, and its final balance is
transferred to Capital A/c. The companion skill is computing the **effect on profit**:

<div class="formula">Corrected profit = draft profit + expenses wrongly overstated + incomes wrongly omitted − expenses wrongly omitted − incomes wrongly overstated</div>

Two cross-links close the loop. A **capital-vs-revenue misclassification is an error of
principle**: installation wages debited to Wages A/c overstates expenses and understates the
asset, so profit is *understated* until rectified. And a swap **between two expense heads of the
same statement side** (stationery debited to Purchases) moves gross profit but leaves **net
profit unchanged** — the examiner's favourite zero-effect trap.

## Fully worked examples

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — journalising a batch of transactions (easy)">
  <p>
    Journalise for Meena General Stores, April 2026: (1) Apr 1 — Meena starts business with cash
    ₹3,00,000. (2) Apr 3 — opens a bank account, depositing ₹2,00,000. (3) Apr 5 — buys goods for
    cash ₹40,000. (4) Apr 9 — sells goods on credit to Farhan for ₹35,000. (5) Apr 12 — buys a
    weighing machine from Zaveri Traders for ₹24,000, paying ₹10,000 by cheque, balance on
    credit. (6) Apr 18 — pays shop rent ₹9,000 by cheque.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — the entries with their reasons</div>
    Apr 1: Cash A/c Dr 3,00,000 — To Capital A/c 3,00,000 <em>(cash comes in — debit; the owner, a personal account, gives — credit)</em><br />
    Apr 3: Bank A/c Dr 2,00,000 — To Cash A/c 2,00,000 <em>(the bank, a personal account, is the receiver; cash goes out)</em><br />
    Apr 5: Purchases A/c Dr 40,000 — To Cash A/c 40,000 <em>(expense of buying goods — debit; cash goes out)</em><br />
    Apr 9: Farhan A/c Dr 35,000 — To Sales A/c 35,000 <em>(Farhan receives the goods' benefit; sales is an income-type nominal account)</em><br />
    Apr 12: Machinery A/c Dr 24,000 — To Bank A/c 10,000, To Zaveri Traders A/c 14,000 <em>(a compound entry: one debit, two credits; the machine is an asset, never Purchases)</em><br />
    Apr 18: Rent A/c Dr 9,000 — To Bank A/c 9,000 <em>(expense — debit)</em>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — the self-check</div>
    Total debits = 3,00,000 + 2,00,000 + 40,000 + 35,000 + 24,000 + 9,000 = <strong>₹6,08,000</strong> = total credits. In every batch of journal entries, this equality must hold before you move on.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — posting and balancing a ledger account (moderate)">
  <p>
    Continue from Worked example 1. On Apr 15 Farhan returns goods worth ₹5,000; on Apr 25 he
    pays ₹20,000 in cash. Prepare Farhan's Account for April 2026 and balance it.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Farhan's Account (Dr side | Cr side)</div>
    Debit side: Apr 9 To Sales A/c 35,000<br />
    Credit side: Apr 15 By Sales Returns A/c 5,000 · Apr 25 By Cash A/c 20,000 · Apr 30 By Balance c/d <strong>10,000</strong><br />
    Both sides total ₹35,000; on May 1 the account reopens with "To Balance b/d ₹10,000".
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — reading the balance</div>
    35,000 − 5,000 − 20,000 = <strong>₹10,000 debit balance</strong> — Farhan still owes Meena
    ₹10,000; he is a debtor (an asset). The c/d entry sits on the credit (lighter) side, and the
    b/d entry on the debit side — the b/d side names the balance.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — triple-column cash book (moderate)">
  <p>
    Prepare the triple-column cash book of Anand Traders for March 2026: Mar 1 — cash in hand
    ₹7,500, bank balance ₹36,000. Mar 5 — received a cheque from Bela for ₹14,700 in full
    settlement of her account of ₹15,000, banked the same day. Mar 8 — cash sales ₹21,500.
    Mar 12 — deposited ₹18,000 cash into the bank. Mar 17 — paid Chirag by cheque ₹9,800 in full
    settlement of ₹10,000. Mar 22 — withdrew ₹4,000 from the bank for shop use. Mar 26 — paid
    electricity bill ₹2,300 in cash. Mar 31 — bank charges ₹150.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — classify before you compute</div>
    Bela: bank +14,700, discount ALLOWED column 300 (14,700 accepted against 15,000).<br />
    Chirag: bank −9,800, discount RECEIVED column 200.<br />
    Mar 12 and Mar 22 are CONTRA entries ("C"): Mar 12 — cash −18,000, bank +18,000; Mar 22 —
    bank −4,000, cash +4,000. Neither is posted to any ledger account.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — the column balances</div>
    Cash: 7,500 + 21,500 − 18,000 + 4,000 − 2,300 = <strong>₹12,700</strong><br />
    Bank: 36,000 + 14,700 + 18,000 − 9,800 − 4,000 − 150 = <strong>₹54,750</strong><br />
    Discount columns: allowed total ₹300, received total ₹200 — totalled only, never balanced;
    the totals go to Discount Allowed A/c (Dr) and Discount Received A/c (Cr).
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — rectification through a suspense account (moderate-hard)">
  <p>
    The trial balance of Gopal Stores did not agree — the debit column was short by ₹1,900, and
    the difference was placed in a Suspense Account. Later, three errors surfaced: (i) the sales
    book was overcast by ₹2,500; (ii) a credit purchase from Hari of ₹3,200, correctly entered in
    the purchases book, was posted to Hari's account as ₹2,600; (iii) repairs to the delivery
    scooter ₹1,100 were debited to Vehicles A/c. Rectify and close the Suspense Account.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — the rectification entries</div>
    (i) Sales A/c Dr 2,500 — To Suspense A/c 2,500 <em>(sales book total posts to the CREDIT of Sales; an overcast means 2,500 excess credit — one-sided, so Suspense takes the other leg)</em><br />
    (ii) Suspense A/c Dr 600 — To Hari A/c 600 <em>(Hari was credited 2,600 instead of 3,200 — short credit of 600, one-sided)</em><br />
    (iii) Repairs A/c Dr 1,100 — To Vehicles A/c 1,100 <em>(error of principle, two-sided — Suspense is NOT touched)</em>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Suspense Account closes to nil</div>
    Debit side: opening balance b/d 1,900 + Hari 600 = 2,500<br />
    Credit side: Sales 2,500<br />
    Both sides total <strong>₹2,500</strong> — the account closes exactly, proving all
    trial-balance errors have been found. (Check the opening: error (i) put 2,500 excess in the
    credit column, error (ii) left the credit column 600 short — net credit excess 1,900, so the
    debit column was short by 1,900. Consistent.)
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — effect of errors on profit (hard: the examiner's favourite)">
  <p>
    Kabir Electricals reports a draft net profit of ₹2,40,000 for 2025–26. Before the accounts
    are finalised, five errors are found: (i) wages of ₹15,000 for installing a new machine were
    debited to Wages A/c; (ii) a credit sale of ₹18,000 was completely omitted; (iii) the
    purchases book was overcast by ₹6,000; (iv) old furniture sold for ₹10,000 was credited to
    Sales A/c; (v) stationery of ₹2,000 was debited to Purchases A/c. Find the corrected net
    profit.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — effect of each error on net profit</div>
    (i) Installation wages are capital expenditure — expense overstated → rectifying ADDS 15,000<br />
    (ii) Omitted credit sale — income omitted → ADDS 18,000<br />
    (iii) Purchases overcast — expense overstated → ADDS 6,000<br />
    (iv) Furniture sale is not sales revenue — income overstated → LESS 10,000 <em>(the credit belongs to Furniture A/c, an asset)</em><br />
    (v) Stationery inside Purchases — both are expenses against profit: gross profit is understated but NET profit is unchanged → <strong>nil effect</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — corrected profit</div>
    2,40,000 + 15,000 + 18,000 + 6,000 − 10,000 = <strong>₹2,69,000</strong><br />
    (Error (iv)'s full rectification: Sales A/c Dr 10,000 — To Furniture A/c 10,000, assuming
    sale at book value as Foundation problems do unless told otherwise.)
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

- **Trade discount in the books.** Trade discount is never recorded as a separate item — entries
  are made net of it. Only *cash* discount reaches the discount columns and the Discount
  Allowed/Received accounts. Options showing the gross list price are planted for this slip.
- **Assets in the goods books.** Machinery or furniture bought on credit belongs in the journal
  proper. Left in the purchases book, it becomes an error of principle too.
- **Contra entries posted to the ledger.** The "C"-marked pair lives entirely inside the cash
  book — posting it anywhere doubles the entry.
- **Balancing the discount columns.** They are totalled, never balanced — they are memoranda
  columns, not accounts.
- **Suspense for two-sided errors.** Suspense absorbs only the missing leg of *one-sided*
  errors. A rectification of a two-sided error that routes through Suspense is always a wrong
  option.
- **Reading an agreed trial balance as proof of accuracy.** Complete omission, principle,
  compensating, wrong-account and original-entry errors all survive an agreed trial balance.
- **Overcast/undercast direction.** An overcast purchases book means *excess debit* (profit
  understated); an overcast sales book means *excess credit*. Half the wrong options in
  rectification MCQs just flip this direction.
- **Zero-effect swaps.** Stationery debited to Purchases changes gross profit but not net
  profit. When a question asks for the effect on *net* profit, the swap between two P&amp;L-side
  expense heads contributes nothing.
- **Cash column with a credit balance.** Impossible — only the bank column can run into
  overdraft. Any option showing a credit cash balance is self-condemned.

## One-page revision summary

**The chain:** transaction → journal / subsidiary book (first record) → ledger (posting) →
balancing → trial balance (arithmetic check) → final accounts. Errors found later are
rectified — never erased.

**Rules and formulas:**

<div class="formula">Assets = Liabilities + Capital · Opening capital = Assets − Liabilities</div>
<div class="formula">Personal: debit the receiver, credit the giver · Real: debit what comes in, credit what goes out · Nominal: debit expenses and losses, credit incomes and gains</div>
<div class="formula">Modern map: assets and expenses rise by DEBIT; liabilities, capital and incomes rise by CREDIT</div>
<div class="formula">Suspense = trial balance difference, parked on the shorter column; closes to nil when all one-sided errors are rectified</div>
<div class="formula">Petty cash reimbursement = expenses of the period (imprest restored)</div>
<div class="formula">Corrected profit = draft profit + overstated expenses + omitted incomes − omitted expenses − overstated incomes</div>

**Rules that answer theory MCQs:** journal = chronological book of original entry; ledger =
principal book · "To" on the debit side, "By" on the credit side — convention only · nominal
accounts close to Trading/P&amp;L, not carried forward · subsidiary goods books take only credit
transactions of goods; cash → cash book, credit assets → journal proper · trade discount never
recorded, cash discount recorded · contra entries marked "C", not posted · discount columns
totalled, not balanced · cash column: debit balance only; bank column can be overdrawn · trial
balance = a statement on a date, arithmetic proof only · one-sided errors disturb it, two-sided
errors do not · suspense on the shorter side; closes when the hunt ends · after final accounts,
rectify through P&amp;L Adjustment A/c (balance to Capital) · capital-vs-revenue confusion =
error of principle.
