Manju runs a general store in Mysuru. She keeps a cash book with a bank column: every cheque she
deposits goes on the debit side, every cheque she issues goes on the credit side. The bank keeps
its own record of the same account and hands her a copy — the pass book. On 31 March her cash
book says one figure and the pass book says another. Nobody has stolen anything, and usually
nobody has even made a mistake: the two records simply move at different speeds. The **bank
reconciliation statement (BRS)** is the short statement that explains the gap, rupee by rupee.

In the exam this chapter is generous: every numerical reuses one skill — start from one book's
balance and walk item by item to the other book's balance — dressed up in four different starting
points. Master the direction logic once and every permutation (favourable or overdraft, cash book
or pass book, with or without errors) becomes the same question.

## 1. Two records of the same money

Two books describe one bank account. In *your* books, the **bank column of the cash book**:
debit side for money in (cheques deposited, cash banked), credit side for money out (cheques
issued, withdrawals). In the *bank's* books, your account sits in the bank's own ledger — and
the **pass book** (today, the bank statement — same thing) is simply a copy of that account
given to you.

Here is the twist that explains the whole chapter: **the two books look at the same money from
opposite sides.** To you, money at the bank is an asset — a debit balance. To the bank, that
same money is money it *owes you* — you are the bank's creditor, so the bank shows your money
as a *credit* balance. Deposit ₹5,000 and you debit your cash book while the bank credits your
account. Every entry is mirrored.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Fix this pair permanently: <strong>your debit is the bank's credit.</strong> A debit balance
    in the cash book and a credit balance in the pass book mean the same happy thing — money
    lying at the bank. Neither book is "right"; they are two viewpoints of one account.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. The four balances and their signs

| Book | Balance | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Cash book (bank column) | Debit | **Favourable** — money at the bank |
| Cash book (bank column) | Credit | **Overdraft** — you owe the bank |
| Pass book | Credit | **Favourable** |
| Pass book | Debit | **Overdraft** |

An **overdraft** is the bank letting you draw more than you have: the account turns negative and
the roles flip — the bank becomes *your* creditor. In your cash book that shows as a credit
balance in the bank column; in the pass book, as a debit balance.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Everyday instinct says "credit means money came in". In your <em>own</em> cash book the bank
    column follows asset rules: <strong>debit balance = favourable</strong>. It is in the
    <em>pass book</em> — the bank's mirror — that credit = favourable. A large share of the
    theory marks in this chapter is just keeping these four labels straight.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. Why the balances differ: timing differences

On any given date, some transactions stand recorded in one book but not yet in the other. These
**timing differences** need no correction — time cures them. Group them by *who moved first*:

**You recorded first; the bank has not caught up:**

- **Cheques issued but not yet presented** — you credited the cash book the day you posted the
  cheque; the bank debits your account only when the payee presents it, days or weeks later.
- **Cheques deposited but not yet collected or credited** — you debited the cash book the day
  you banked them; the bank credits you only when the clearing actually brings the money in.

**The bank recorded first; you learn only when the statement arrives:**

- **Bank charges** — collection charges, folio or SMS charges, debited by the bank on its own.
- **Interest charged by the bank** on an overdraft.
- **Interest allowed by the bank** on your balance or linked deposits.
- **Standing instructions** — insurance premium, loan instalment or rent the bank pays
  automatically on your mandate.
- **Direct deposits by customers** — a debtor pays straight into your account.
- **Interest or dividends collected by the bank** on your investments.
- **Dishonour of a cheque deposited or a bill discounted** — the cheque you banked bounced, so
  the bank removes the credit and usually debits its charges too.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Every timing item is a one-book entry <em>so far</em>. The entire BRS technique is asking,
    item by item: <strong>which book already contains this, and which does not?</strong>
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Why the balances differ: errors

The second family of causes is **errors** — and unlike timing items, errors do need correcting
(yours in the cash book; the bank's by the bank).

**Your errors (in the cash book):** omitting a banked cheque entirely; a wrong amount (the
transposition ₹2,470 written as ₹2,740); the **wrong side** (a receipt entered on the credit
side); the **wrong column** (a cheque payment entered in the cash column); overcasting or
undercasting a side.

**The bank's errors (in the pass book):** debiting your account with another customer's cheque,
crediting someone else's deposit to you, or posting a wrong figure.

Two rules make error items painless:

1. Ask **which book is wrong**, and by how much the error moves *that book's* balance away from
   the truth.
2. A wrong-**side** error moves the balance by **twice** the amount — the correct entry is
   missing *and* an opposite entry sits in its place. A wrong-**amount** error moves it only by
   the *difference*. An omission or wrong-**column** error moves it by the full amount, once.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    A receipt of ₹3,000 entered on the payment side leaves the cash book ₹6,000 too low — not
    ₹3,000. The missing debit is 3,000 and the intruding credit is another 3,000. Doubling
    belongs to wrong-side errors only; never double a transposition difference.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Preparing the BRS: the direction discipline

A BRS starts from one book's balance on a date, adjusts for every item the two books currently
disagree on, and lands on the other book's balance. It is headed **"as on"** a date — a
snapshot, not a statement *for* a period.

The whole technique is one question per item: **does this item leave the target book's balance
higher or lower than my starting figure?**

The cleanest way to never slip on signs: call favourable balances **plus** and overdrafts
**minus**. Then a single line covers all four starting points:

<div class="formula">Balance as per pass book = Balance as per cash book + Cheques issued but not presented − Cheques deposited but not yet credited + Bank credits not yet in the cash book (interest allowed, direct deposits, collections) − Bank debits not yet in the cash book (charges, interest on overdraft, standing orders, dishonours)</div>

Why the first plus: you have already reduced your book for a cheque the bank has not yet paid,
so the bank's figure stands *higher* than yours. Why the first minus: you have already added a
deposit the bank has not yet credited, so the bank's figure stands *lower*. Reason out every
other item — including errors — the same way: find which way the wrong (or waiting) book leans,
and push the figure the other way.

Going from the pass book back to the cash book is the same line run backwards — **flip every
sign**:

<div class="formula">Balance as per cash book = Balance as per pass book − Cheques issued but not presented + Cheques deposited but not yet credited − Bank credits not yet in the cash book + Bank debits not yet in the cash book</div>

The ICAI Study Material presents the BRS with plus and minus columns; older books use Add/Less
rows. The arithmetic is identical — the columns are simply the signs written sideways.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    In MCQs, the three wrong options are usually this very computation with one sign flipped,
    the whole direction reversed, or one item ignored. Finished early? Re-derive the answer
    starting from the <em>other</em> book — landing back on your starting figure proves every
    sign in one pass.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. Starting from an overdraft

Nothing new is needed for overdrafts — only discipline. Write the overdraft as a **minus**
figure, run the same formula, and read the sign of the result.

Mini example: the cash book shows an overdraft of ₹20,000. Cheques issued but not presented
₹6,000; cheques deposited but not yet credited ₹9,000; bank charges ₹500 not yet in the cash
book.

<div class="formula">Balance as per pass book = (−20,000) + 6,000 − 9,000 − 500 = −23,500 → overdraft of ₹23,500</div>

Read it in words to build trust: the bank has not yet paid out ₹6,000 you already deducted — the
bank's picture is *less* overdrawn than yours. The bank has not yet received ₹9,000 you already
counted in — the bank's picture is *more* overdrawn. Charges the bank has already taken deepen
its overdraft further.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Rote grids ("add this if favourable, less if overdraft…") are exactly where marks die. One
    honest minus sign replaces the whole memorised table — and survives an examiner who mixes an
    overdraft start with three errors.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. The amended cash book approach

By the time the statement arrives, the bank-first items of §3 are *known* — so a well-kept cash
book should simply record them. That is the **amended (adjusted) cash book** approach: first
bring the cash book up to date, then reconcile only what is left.

**Record in the cash book (these belong in your books):**

- bank charges, interest charged by the bank, interest allowed by the bank;
- standing-instruction payments (premium, instalment, rent);
- direct deposits by customers; interest and dividends collected by the bank;
- dishonoured cheques and bills — credit the bank column, debit the customer who let you down;
- **your own errors**, corrected in your book, where they live.

**Keep for the BRS (nothing to record — your book is already right):**

- cheques issued but not yet presented;
- cheques deposited but not yet credited;
- **the bank's errors** — only the bank can fix its own ledger.

<div class="formula">Adjusted cash book balance = Given cash book balance + bank credits now recorded − bank debits now recorded ± correction of your own errors</div>
<div class="formula">Balance as per pass book = Adjusted cash book balance + Cheques issued but not presented − Cheques deposited but not credited ± the bank's errors</div>

At the year end this split is not a matter of style: the **balance sheet must show the adjusted
cash book balance** as cash at bank, so the amendment entries must actually be passed.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Read the requirement line carefully. "Prepare a BRS <strong>without adjusting</strong> the
    cash book" — every item goes straight into the statement. "Prepare the <strong>amended cash
    book and then</strong> a BRS" — split the items as above. Same facts, two different answers;
    the wording decides.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. What a BRS is, and what it is not

A BRS is a **statement, not an account**. It sits outside the double-entry system: nothing is
posted from it, it has no debit and credit sides, and it is not part of the ledger. (The
*amendment entries* of §7 are ordinary cash book entries — the statement itself records
nothing.)

It is prepared by **the customer** — you, not the bank — usually whenever a statement arrives
(monthly, or more often for busy accounts) and always at the year end.

Why prepare it, beyond the exam:

- it **explains the difference** on a date, item by item, so neither book is blindly "trusted";
- it **catches errors** in both books early;
- it **discourages embezzlement** — a cashier who pockets a cheque shows up as a deposit that
  never reaches the pass book;
- it reveals **slow collections** and **stale cheques** — a cheque unpresented for months needs
  chasing or reversing;
- it fixes the **true bank position** for the final accounts.

## Fully worked examples

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — from a favourable cash book balance (easy)">
  <p>
    On 31 March 2026 the bank column of Rao Stores' cash book shows a debit balance of ₹52,400.
    Comparison with the pass book shows: (a) cheques issued of ₹9,800 not presented by 31 March;
    (b) cheques of ₹14,500 deposited on 29–30 March credited by the bank only in April; (c) bank
    charges of ₹350 debited by the bank; (d) interest of ₹1,150 allowed by the bank — (c) and
    (d) not yet in the cash book. Find the balance as per the pass book.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — set each direction</div>
    Start: cash book (Dr, favourable) +52,400<br />
    (a) bank has not yet paid these out → pass book stands higher: +9,800<br />
    (b) bank has not yet received these → pass book stands lower: −14,500<br />
    (c) bank has already taken the charges: −350<br />
    (d) bank has already allowed the interest: +1,150
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — assemble</div>
    52,400 + 9,800 − 14,500 − 350 + 1,150 = <strong>₹48,500 favourable as per pass book</strong>
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — from an overdraft per pass book (moderate)">
  <p>
    On 30 June 2026 the pass book of Kale Traders shows an overdraft of ₹31,700. (a) Cheques
    issued but not presented ₹12,000; (b) cheques deposited but not yet collected ₹18,600;
    (c) bank charges ₹400 and (d) an insurance premium of ₹2,500 paid under standing
    instruction appear only in the pass book; (e) a customer deposited ₹6,000 directly into the
    bank, not yet in the cash book. Find the balance as per the cash book.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — walking pass book → cash book flips every sign</div>
    Start: pass book overdraft −31,700<br />
    (a) the cash book was already reduced for these: −12,000<br />
    (b) the cash book already contains these deposits: +18,600<br />
    (e) the cash book does not yet contain this money: −6,000<br />
    (c) + (d) the cash book has not yet been reduced for these: +2,900
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — assemble and check</div>
    −31,700 − 12,000 + 18,600 − 6,000 + 2,900 = <strong>−28,200 → overdraft as per cash book ₹28,200</strong><br />
    Check (run forward): −28,200 + 12,000 − 18,600 + 6,000 − 2,900 = −31,700 ✓
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — amended cash book, then BRS (moderate-hard)">
  <p>
    On 31 March 2026 the cash book of Desai &amp; Co. shows a debit bank balance of ₹36,900. The
    pass book reveals: (a) bank charges ₹300 and locker rent ₹1,200 debited by the bank;
    (b) interest allowed ₹900; (c) LIC premium ₹3,600 paid under standing instruction; (d) a
    customer, Firoz, paid ₹8,000 directly into the account; (e) a cheque of ₹4,700 deposited
    earlier was returned dishonoured — none of these entered in the cash book yet. Further:
    (f) cheques issued but not presented ₹11,300; (g) cheques deposited but not yet credited
    ₹16,400. Prepare the amended cash book, then the BRS.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — amend the cash book: items (a)–(e) belong in it</div>
    36,900 − 300 − 1,200 + 900 − 3,600 + 8,000 − 4,700 = <strong>₹36,000 (Dr)</strong><br />
    This ₹36,000 is the figure the balance sheet shows as cash at bank.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — BRS with only the timing items (f) and (g)</div>
    36,000 + 11,300 − 16,400 = <strong>₹30,900 favourable as per pass book</strong><br />
    (f) and (g) never enter the cash book — they are the bank's pending work, not yours.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — overdraft with a nest of errors (hard: the examiner's favourite)">
  <p>
    On 31 December 2025 the bank column of Bhalla Brothers' cash book shows an overdraft of
    ₹22,150. Investigation reveals: (a) cheques of ₹15,600 issued in December, of which cheques
    of ₹9,700 were presented by 31 December; (b) cheques of ₹13,800 deposited, of which only
    ₹8,200 had been credited; (c) a receipt of ₹2,600 from Mohan entered on the credit side of
    the bank column (the cheque was banked and duly collected); (d) a cheque issued for ₹3,190
    entered as ₹3,910 (presented and paid at the correct figure); (e) a payment by cheque of
    ₹1,800 entered in the cash column (the cheque was presented in December); (f) interest on
    overdraft ₹640 debited by the bank, not in the cash book; (g) the bank wrongly debited the
    account with another customer's cheque of ₹1,500. Find the balance as per the pass book.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — compute the timing slices</div>
    Unpresented cheques = 15,600 − 9,700 = 5,900 → +5,900<br />
    Uncredited deposits = 13,800 − 8,200 = 5,600 → −5,600
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — size each error</div>
    (c) wrong side: cash book is 2 × 2,600 = 5,200 too low → +5,200<br />
    (d) wrong amount: payment over-recorded by 3,910 − 3,190 = 720 → cash book 720 too low → +720<br />
    (e) wrong column: bank column missed a payment of 1,800 → cash book 1,800 too high → −1,800<br />
    (f) bank debit not yet recorded by the trader → −640<br />
    (g) the bank's own wrong debit → pass book stands 1,500 lower → −1,500
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — assemble</div>
    −22,150 + 5,900 − 5,600 + 5,200 + 720 − 1,800 − 640 − 1,500 = <strong>−19,870 → overdraft as per pass book ₹19,870</strong>
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

- **Running the statement in the wrong direction.** Starting from the pass book but applying
  cash-book-start signs. Every reverse question flips *every* sign — re-derive, don't recall.
- **Treating an overdraft like a favourable balance.** Write it as a minus figure first; the
  wrong option computed "positively" is nearly always present.
- **Doubling the wrong error.** Only wrong-*side* errors move the balance by twice the amount.
  A transposition moves it by the difference; an omission or wrong column by the amount, once.
- **Stuffing timing items into the amended cash book.** Cheques not presented and deposits not
  credited never get cash book entries — they are the bank's pending work.
- **Correcting the bank's error in your own book.** The bank's wrong debit stays a BRS line
  until the bank fixes its ledger; passing a cash book entry for it is double-wrong.
- **Missing the dishonour charges.** A bounced cheque usually costs the cheque amount *plus*
  the bank's charges — deduct both when starting from the cash book.
- **Reading "issued" as "presented".** When the stem gives cheques issued ₹15,600 and presented
  ₹9,700, the BRS item is the unpresented slice ₹5,900 — never the full issue.
- **Reading Dr/Cr with the wrong book's eyes.** A debit balance in the *pass book* is an
  overdraft, not money at bank.

## One-page revision summary

**The mirror:** your debit = the bank's credit · pass book = copy of your account in the bank's
ledger · to the bank you are a creditor (favourable) or debtor (overdraft).

**Four balances:** cash book Dr = favourable · cash book Cr = overdraft · pass book Cr =
favourable · pass book Dr = overdraft.

**Causes of difference:** timing (cheques issued not presented; deposits not credited; bank
charges/interest; standing instructions; direct deposits; collections; dishonours) vs errors
(trader's or bank's). Timing heals itself; errors need fixing by whoever made them.

**Formulas (favourable = plus, overdraft = minus):**

<div class="formula">Pass book balance = Cash book balance + cheques not presented − deposits not credited + bank credits not in cash book − bank debits not in cash book</div>
<div class="formula">Cash book balance = Pass book balance with every sign flipped</div>
<div class="formula">Adjusted cash book = given balance ± bank-first items and own errors; then BRS carries only timing items and the bank's errors</div>

**Error sizing:** wrong side = twice the amount · wrong amount = the difference · omission or
wrong column = the amount once · always ask which book is wrong, and which way it leans.

**Rules that answer theory MCQs:** BRS is a statement, not an account — outside double entry ·
prepared by the customer, periodically and at year end · can start from any of the four
balances · balance sheet shows the *adjusted* cash book balance · purposes: explain the
difference, catch errors, deter embezzlement, expose collection delays and stale cheques.
