Kailash runs a hardware shop in Belgaum. Every evening he counts the cash box and writes the total
in a notebook. In a second diary he lists who owes him money and whom he owes. He has no ledger, no
journal, no trial balance — and at the end of the year his banker still asks, "What was your
profit?"

That is this chapter. You are handed a shoebox instead of a set of books, and you must produce a
figure the owner can rely on. There are two ways to do it: a quick way that gives you *only* the
net profit (the **net worth** or **statement of affairs** method), and a longer way that
reconstructs enough of the double entry to produce a full Trading and Profit &amp; Loss Account
(the **conversion** method). The exam loves both — the first as a four-line computation, the second
as a full-length problem built out of two or three missing figures.

## 1. What incomplete records are, and who keeps them

"Single entry system" is a misleading name. It is **not a system at all** — it is the *absence* of
one. What actually survives in such books is a mixture:

- **Double entry** for **personal accounts** — debtors and creditors — because the trader must know who owes him what.
- **Single entry**, a one-sided memorandum, for **cash** — a running cash book, often mixing business and private receipts.
- **No entry at all** for most **real and nominal** accounts — furniture, purchases, sales, rent, salaries, discounts. They exist as bills in a file, not as accounts.

Who keeps books this way? Small sole proprietors and small partnership firms — a trader whose volume
does not justify a book-keeper, who wants simplicity and privacy, and whom no statute compels
otherwise. **Companies cannot**: a company must maintain proper books on the double entry basis, so
incomplete records are never a company's answer, nor are they open to any entity under statutory audit.

The defects follow straight from the definition: **no trial balance can be extracted**, so there is
no arithmetical check at all; **gross profit cannot be ascertained**, because there is no Trading
Account, so the trader cannot see his margin or price sensibly; the **true and fair position is
doubtful**, resting on estimates and counts rather than ledger balances; **frauds and errors go
undetected**, because nothing has to agree with anything; and the accounts are **not acceptable** to
tax authorities, banks or an incoming partner without reconstruction.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A favourite one-mark question: <strong>is single entry a "system"?</strong> The answer the
    examiner wants is that it is a <em>mechanically defective, unscientific</em> way of keeping
    books — a mixture of double entry, single entry and no entry — and not a system in any
    meaningful sense.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. Statement of Affairs versus Balance Sheet

Both look like a list of assets and liabilities. They are not the same document, and the
difference is examined almost every attempt.

| | Statement of Affairs | Balance Sheet |
|---|---|---|
| Source of figures | Estimates, physical counts, vouchers, the trader's memory | Ledger balances thrown up by a trial balance |
| Capital | Is the **balancing figure**: Capital = Assets − Liabilities | Is taken **from the Capital Account**, which is independently built up |
| Reliability | Lower — an omitted asset or liability simply changes capital, and nothing complains | Higher — an omission would throw the trial balance out |
| Purpose | To *find* capital when it is not known | To *present* a position already known |
| Prepared under | Incomplete records | Double entry |

<div class="formula">Capital (statement of affairs) = Assets − Liabilities</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Writing "Balance Sheet" as the heading in a net-worth question. Under incomplete records the
    capital figure is <strong>derived by subtraction</strong>, so the correct heading is
    <strong>Statement of Affairs</strong>. Marks are given for the heading itself.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. The net worth method: profit from the movement in capital

If the owner puts nothing in and takes nothing out, any increase in his capital during the year
*is* his profit. Drawings and fresh capital disturb that, so you undo them:

<div class="formula">Profit = Closing capital + Drawings − Further capital introduced − Opening capital</div>

Read it as a story. Closing capital is what is left **after** the owner has helped himself, so add
the drawings back; fresh capital brought in swelled the closing capital without any trading effort,
so take it out. What remains, over and above where he started, is profit — and a negative answer is
a **loss**. The same equation rearranges to whatever the question hides:

<div class="formula">Closing capital = Opening capital + Further capital + Profit − Drawings</div>
<div class="formula">Drawings = Opening capital + Further capital + Profit − Closing capital</div>
<div class="formula">Further capital = Closing capital + Drawings − Profit − Opening capital</div>

You get **opening capital** from a statement of affairs drawn at the *beginning* of the year and
**closing capital** from one drawn at the *end*. So a net-worth question is really three steps:
opening statement of affairs → closing statement of affairs → the formula.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Drawings are not only cash. <strong>Goods taken for personal use</strong> (at cost),
    the owner's life insurance premium paid from the shop's bank account, his household electricity
    bill, income tax on his personal income — all are drawings. Income tax of a
    <em>sole proprietor</em> is a personal expense, never a business expense.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Adjustments before you sign off the net worth profit

The closing statement of affairs is built from raw figures. Before it can yield a believable profit,
the usual year-end adjustments must be pushed through it — every one of them changes **closing
capital**, and therefore the profit:

- **Depreciation** not yet provided → reduce the asset. **Bad debts** and a **provision for doubtful debts** → reduce debtors.
- **Outstanding expenses** (wages, rent, interest) → add a liability. **Prepaid expenses** and **accrued income** → add an asset.
- **Assets or liabilities simply left out** of the first draft → put them in.
- **Interest on capital** and **interest on drawings**, where asked for, are adjusted after the profit is found, exactly as in Ch 7.

Work adjustment by adjustment on the closing capital figure, then apply the formula once. Never try
to adjust "the profit" directly — you will lose the sign somewhere. And note the two things the
method **cannot** do: it gives you neither **gross profit** nor the **individual expenses**, only
one number with no explanation of how it arose. When the question wants a Trading and Profit &amp;
Loss Account, you must convert.

## 5. The conversion method: what it is and the order of work

Conversion means rebuilding the missing double entry from whatever evidence exists — the cash book,
the debtors and creditors diaries, the bill file, counterfoils and invoices — until you can draw up
a proper Trading Account, Profit &amp; Loss Account and Balance Sheet. The order almost never changes:

1. **Opening Statement of Affairs** → opening capital, and every opening balance needed in steps 3–5.
2. **Cash book (or Cash and Bank summary)** → reconstruct it. Missing cash sales, missing drawings or the missing closing balance appear as the **balancing figure**.
3. **Total Debtors Account** → credit sales (or receipts, or closing debtors).
4. **Total Creditors Account** → credit purchases (or payments, or closing creditors).
5. **Bills Receivable and Bills Payable Accounts** → feed steps 3 and 4 with the bills figures those accounts need.
6. **Trading and Profit &amp; Loss Account**, then the **Balance Sheet**, which must tally.

Steps 3, 4 and 5 are all the same trick: open a T-account, put in every figure you know, and let
the one you do not know be the balancing figure.

## 6. Reconstructing the cash book

The cash book is a ledger account for cash. So:

<div class="formula">Opening cash and bank + Total receipts − Total payments = Closing cash and bank</div>

Any single unknown in that line can be found, and two patterns dominate. **Missing cash sales:**
every payment, both balances and the receipts from debtors are known, so cash sales are whatever is
needed to make the account balance. **Missing drawings:** all receipts and all business payments are
known, and so are both balances; the gap is what the owner took.

Watch the traps. **Capital expenditure paid in cash** (furniture, machinery) is a payment in the
cash book even though it never reaches the Profit &amp; Loss Account. A **loan received** is a
receipt but not income. **Cash deposited into or drawn from the bank** is a contra — it must not be
counted as a receipt *and* a payment in a combined cash-and-bank summary, or you will double-count.

**Splitting cash sales from credit sales.** Only credit sales pass through the Total Debtors
Account; only cash sales pass through the cash book. So:

<div class="formula">Total sales = Cash sales (from the cash book) + Credit sales (from the Total Debtors A/c)</div>

The same split applies to purchases. If a question says "20% of the sales were for cash" and gives
you credit sales of ₹8,00,000, then credit sales are 80% of the total: total sales = 8,00,000 ×
100/80 = ₹10,00,000, and cash sales = ₹2,00,000. Read whether the percentage is of *total* sales or
*of credit* sales — that one word decides the answer.

## 7. Total Debtors Account: finding credit sales

Everything that increases what customers owe goes on the debit; everything that reduces it goes on
the credit.

| Debit side (increases) | Credit side (decreases) |
|---|---|
| Opening balance of debtors | Cash and cheques received from customers |
| **Credit sales** | Discount allowed |
| Bills receivable **dishonoured** | Sales returns (returns inward) |
| Cheques received earlier now dishonoured | Bad debts written off |
| Interest charged on overdue accounts | Bills receivable **received** from customers |
| | Transfer / set-off to the creditors ledger |
| | Closing balance of debtors |

<div class="formula">Credit sales = Receipts + Discount allowed + Returns inward + Bad debts + B/R received + Set-off + Closing debtors − Opening debtors − B/R dishonoured</div>

Two lines earn most of the marks. **Bills receivable received** from a customer *reduce* debtors —
the debt has changed its clothes, from a book debt into a bill — so they sit on the credit side.
When such a bill is later **dishonoured**, the customer owes again and the amount comes **back to
the debit** of the debtors account. Forgetting the second leg is the classic slip.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Cash sales must <strong>never</strong> be entered in the Total Debtors Account. A cash sale
    creates no debtor. Putting it in inflates credit sales by exactly the cash sales figure — and
    that wrong number is always sitting there as an option.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. Total Creditors Account: finding credit purchases

The mirror image. Everything that increases what you owe suppliers is a credit; everything that
settles it is a debit.

| Debit side (decreases) | Credit side (increases) |
|---|---|
| Cash and cheques paid to suppliers | Opening balance of creditors |
| Discount received | **Credit purchases** |
| Purchase returns (returns outward) | Bills payable **dishonoured** |
| Bills payable **accepted** in their favour | |
| Transfer / set-off from the debtors ledger | |
| Closing balance of creditors | |

<div class="formula">Credit purchases = Payments + Discount received + Returns outward + B/P accepted + Set-off + Closing creditors − Opening creditors − B/P dishonoured</div>

A **set-off** (a person who is both customer and supplier, the smaller balance being transferred)
appears twice: on the **credit** of the Total Debtors Account and on the **debit** of the Total
Creditors Account. One transaction, two accounts, opposite sides — exactly as double entry
demands.

## 9. Bills receivable and bills payable accounts

These are small accounts, and their only job is to hand a figure to §7 or §8.

**Bills Receivable Account.** Debit: opening B/R, plus bills received from customers during the
year. Credit: cash collected on maturity, bills discounted, bills endorsed to creditors, bills
dishonoured, and closing B/R.

<div class="formula">B/R received during the year = Collections + Discounted + Endorsed + Dishonoured + Closing B/R − Opening B/R</div>

**Bills Payable Account.** Credit: opening B/P, plus bills accepted during the year. Debit: cash
paid on maturity, bills dishonoured, and closing B/P.

<div class="formula">B/P accepted during the year = Payments on maturity + Dishonoured + Closing B/P − Opening B/P</div>

**Where dishonour goes.** A dishonour always travels *out* of the bills account and *back* into the
personal account. A **bill receivable dishonoured** leaves the B/R Account (credit) and returns to
the **debit** of the Total Debtors Account, so it **reduces** the computed credit sales. A **bill
payable dishonoured** leaves the B/P Account (debit) and returns to the **credit** of the Total
Creditors Account, so it **reduces** the computed credit purchases. Read that pair twice: almost
every hard MCQ in this chapter is built on a student adding a dishonour where it should be
subtracted.

## 10. Gross profit ratio and the memorandum trading account

When purchases, sales or closing stock is missing and the question gives you a **gross profit
ratio**, you rebuild a Trading Account as a *working* — a **memorandum** trading account. It is
called memorandum because it is not part of the double entry: it is never posted or balanced into
the ledger, and it exists only to release the one figure you want.

<div class="formula">Cost of goods sold = Opening stock + Net purchases + Direct expenses − Closing stock</div>
<div class="formula">Gross profit = Net sales − Cost of goods sold</div>

If the gross profit ratio is quoted **on sales** (the usual case):

<div class="formula">Gross profit = Net sales × GP ratio &nbsp;·&nbsp; Cost of goods sold = Net sales × (1 − GP ratio)</div>

Now put the four Trading Account figures on the table, fill in the three you know, and the fourth
falls out. The commonest applications:

- **Stock destroyed by fire or theft.** Build the account up to the date of the fire: stock on that date = opening stock + net purchases + direct expenses − cost of goods sold. Then **loss of stock = stock on the date of the fire − salvaged stock**, and the insurance claim is the lower of the loss and the policy amount.
- **Missing sales.** Sales = Cost of goods sold ÷ (1 − GP ratio on sales).
- **Missing purchases.** Purchases = Cost of goods sold + Closing stock − Opening stock − Direct expenses.

## 11. Mark-up on cost versus margin on sales

A trader who says "I make 25%" has told you nothing until you know **25% of what**. **Mark-up** is
gross profit expressed as a percentage **of cost**; **margin** is the same rupee profit expressed as
a percentage **of sales**. Since sales exceed cost, the mark-up percentage is always the larger.

<div class="formula">Margin = Mark-up ÷ (100 + Mark-up) &nbsp;·&nbsp; Mark-up = Margin ÷ (100 − Margin)</div>

Take cost = 100 and the arithmetic writes itself. A 25% mark-up on cost means cost 100, profit 25,
sales 125 — a margin of 25/125 = **20% on sales**. Run the pairs you should know by heart:

| Mark-up on cost | Margin on sales |
|---|---|
| 20% | 16⅔% |
| 25% | 20% |
| 33⅓% | 25% |
| 50% | 33⅓% |
| 100% | 50% |

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    "Gross profit is 25% <strong>on cost</strong>" applied to <em>sales</em>, or "25%
    <strong>on sales</strong>" applied to <em>cost</em>. Both wrong figures are planted as options
    in every ratio question. Underline the two words <em>on cost</em> or <em>on sales</em> in the
    stem before you touch the calculator.
  </p>
</Callout>

## Fully worked examples

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — net worth method with drawings and fresh capital (easy)">
  <p>
    Radha Stores keeps no proper books. On 1 April 2025 the business had: cash ₹12,000, bank
    ₹58,000, debtors ₹1,46,000, stock ₹1,90,000, furniture ₹80,000, creditors ₹1,24,000 and
    outstanding wages ₹8,000. On 31 March 2026: cash ₹9,000, bank ₹96,000, debtors ₹1,88,000, stock
    ₹2,35,000, furniture ₹80,000 (to be shown after depreciation of ₹8,000), creditors ₹1,41,000,
    bills payable ₹35,000 and outstanding wages ₹6,000. Radha withdrew ₹9,000 a month and, in
    October 2025, sold personal jewellery and brought ₹60,000 into the business. Find the profit.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Statement of Affairs as at 1 April 2025</div>
    Assets: 12,000 + 58,000 + 1,46,000 + 1,90,000 + 80,000 = 4,86,000<br />
    Liabilities: 1,24,000 + 8,000 = 1,32,000<br />
    Opening capital = 4,86,000 − 1,32,000 = <strong>₹3,54,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Statement of Affairs as at 31 March 2026</div>
    Furniture after depreciation = 80,000 − 8,000 = 72,000<br />
    Assets: 9,000 + 96,000 + 1,88,000 + 2,35,000 + 72,000 = 6,00,000<br />
    Liabilities: 1,41,000 + 35,000 + 6,000 = 1,82,000<br />
    Closing capital = 6,00,000 − 1,82,000 = <strong>₹4,18,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Profit</div>
    Drawings = 9,000 × 12 = 1,08,000<br />
    Profit = 4,18,000 + 1,08,000 − 60,000 − 3,54,000 = <strong>₹1,12,000</strong><br />
    (The ₹60,000 is <em>deducted</em> — it made capital grow without earning anything.)
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — Total Debtors Account to find credit sales (moderate)">
  <p>
    Anwar Traders: debtors on 1 April 2025 ₹2,15,000; on 31 March 2026 ₹2,68,000. During the year —
    cash and cheques received from customers ₹9,84,000; discount allowed ₹21,000; sales returns
    ₹46,000; bad debts written off ₹14,000; bills receivable accepted by customers ₹1,80,000; one
    such bill for ₹25,000 was dishonoured on maturity; ₹9,000 was set off against the same party's
    balance in the creditors ledger. Cash sales for the year were ₹1,55,000. Find credit sales and
    total sales.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Credit side of the Total Debtors A/c</div>
    Receipts 9,84,000 + Discount 21,000 + Returns 46,000 + Bad debts 14,000 + B/R received 1,80,000
    + Set-off 9,000 + Closing 2,68,000 = <strong>15,22,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Debit side, and the balancing figure</div>
    Known debits: Opening 2,15,000 + B/R dishonoured 25,000 = 2,40,000<br />
    Credit sales = 15,22,000 − 2,40,000 = <strong>₹12,82,000</strong><br />
    The dishonoured ₹25,000 is a <em>debit</em>, so it comes off the credit-sales figure — it did
    not arise from a fresh sale.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Total sales</div>
    12,82,000 (credit) + 1,55,000 (cash, from the cash book) = <strong>₹14,37,000</strong>
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — Total Creditors with a bills payable interplay (moderate-hard)">
  <p>
    On 1 April 2025 a trader owed creditors ₹1,86,000 and had bills payable outstanding of ₹42,000.
    During the year he paid suppliers ₹8,30,000 by cheque, received discounts of ₹19,000 and
    returned goods worth ₹27,000. He paid ₹2,10,000 in cash on bills that matured, and one bill for
    ₹20,000 was dishonoured on its due date. On 31 March 2026 creditors stood at ₹2,04,000 and
    bills payable at ₹55,000. Find the credit purchases for the year.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Bills Payable A/c (get the acceptances first)</div>
    Debits: paid on maturity 2,10,000 + dishonoured 20,000 + closing 55,000 = 2,85,000<br />
    Less opening credit 42,000<br />
    Bills accepted during the year = <strong>₹2,43,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Total Creditors A/c</div>
    Debit side: paid 8,30,000 + discount 19,000 + returns 27,000 + B/P accepted 2,43,000 + closing
    2,04,000 = 13,23,000<br />
    Known credits: opening 1,86,000 + B/P dishonoured 20,000 = 2,06,000<br />
    Credit purchases = 13,23,000 − 2,06,000 = <strong>₹11,17,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — why the dishonour is a credit here</div>
    When his own acceptance bounced, the bill died and the supplier became a book creditor again.
    The ₹20,000 therefore <em>increases</em> creditors without any purchase behind it, so it must
    be taken out before the purchases figure is read off.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — memorandum trading account: stock lost in a fire (hard)">
  <p>
    A fire on 20 September 2025 destroyed Suresh's godown. Stock on 1 April 2025 was ₹4,20,000.
    From 1 April to 20 September: purchases ₹16,80,000, purchase returns ₹40,000, wages ₹1,20,000,
    sales ₹21,00,000, sales returns ₹60,000. The gross profit has been steady at 25% on sales.
    Stock worth ₹1,10,000 was salvaged. Find the loss of stock.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Cost of goods sold to the date of the fire</div>
    Net sales = 21,00,000 − 60,000 = 20,40,000<br />
    Gross profit = 25% of 20,40,000 = 5,10,000<br />
    Cost of goods sold = 20,40,000 − 5,10,000 = <strong>₹15,30,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Stock on the date of the fire</div>
    Net purchases = 16,80,000 − 40,000 = 16,40,000<br />
    Goods available = 4,20,000 + 16,40,000 + 1,20,000 (wages, a direct expense) = 21,80,000<br />
    Stock on 20 Sep 2025 = 21,80,000 − 15,30,000 = <strong>₹6,50,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Loss</div>
    Loss of stock = 6,50,000 − 1,10,000 (salvage) = <strong>₹5,40,000</strong><br />
    Had the ratio been "25% <em>on cost</em>", cost of goods sold would have been 20,40,000 ×
    100/125 = 16,32,000 and the answer would change completely. Read the two words.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — a compact full conversion (hard: the full-length pattern)">
  <p>
    Meher Handlooms, year ended 31 March 2026. <strong>1 April 2025:</strong> cash ₹10,000, bank
    ₹70,000, debtors ₹1,20,000, stock ₹1,60,000, furniture ₹90,000, creditors ₹1,10,000.
    <strong>Cash and bank summary:</strong> received from debtors ₹6,30,000, cash sales ₹2,40,000;
    paid to creditors ₹5,10,000, rent ₹72,000, salaries ₹1,44,000, sundry expenses ₹36,000,
    furniture bought ₹40,000, drawings ₹60,000; closing balances ₹88,000. <strong>Other
    information:</strong> closing debtors ₹1,55,000, closing creditors ₹1,32,000, discount allowed
    ₹8,000, discount received ₹6,000, bad debts ₹5,000, closing stock ₹1,85,000; depreciate
    furniture by 10% on the closing balance. Prepare the final accounts in outline.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Opening capital</div>
    Assets 10,000 + 70,000 + 1,20,000 + 1,60,000 + 90,000 = 4,50,000; less creditors 1,10,000<br />
    Opening capital = <strong>₹3,40,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Credit sales and total sales</div>
    Credit sales = 6,30,000 + 8,000 + 5,000 + 1,55,000 − 1,20,000 = <strong>₹6,78,000</strong><br />
    Total sales = 6,78,000 + 2,40,000 (cash sales) = <strong>₹9,18,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Credit purchases</div>
    Credit purchases = 5,10,000 + 6,000 + 1,32,000 − 1,10,000 = <strong>₹5,38,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — Trading Account</div>
    Cost of goods sold = 1,60,000 + 5,38,000 − 1,85,000 = 5,13,000<br />
    Gross profit = 9,18,000 − 5,13,000 = <strong>₹4,05,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 5 — Profit &amp; Loss Account</div>
    Depreciation = 10% of (90,000 + 40,000) = 13,000<br />
    4,05,000 + 6,000 (discount received) − 72,000 − 1,44,000 − 36,000 − 8,000 − 5,000 − 13,000<br />
    Net profit = <strong>₹1,33,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 6 — Balance Sheet, and the check that matters</div>
    Closing capital = 3,40,000 + 1,33,000 − 60,000 = <strong>₹4,13,000</strong><br />
    Assets: cash and bank 88,000 + debtors 1,55,000 + stock 1,85,000 + furniture 1,17,000 =
    5,45,000; less creditors 1,32,000 = <strong>₹4,13,000</strong><br />
    The two agree — that agreement, not the neatness of the format, is the proof that the
    conversion is complete.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

- **Adding further capital instead of deducting it.** The wrong figure — out by twice the fresh capital — is always sitting there as an option.
- **Deducting drawings instead of adding them.** Closing capital is already *after* drawings; you add them back.
- **Calling it a Balance Sheet.** Under incomplete records the heading is Statement of Affairs, because capital is the balancing figure.
- **Putting cash sales into the Total Debtors Account** (or cash purchases into the Total Creditors Account). Only credit transactions pass through those accounts.
- **Dropping the second leg of a dishonour.** A dishonoured B/R goes back to the *debit* of debtors and **reduces** credit sales; a dishonoured B/P goes back to the *credit* of creditors and **reduces** credit purchases.
- **Forgetting that bills received reduce debtors.** A customer's acceptance settles the book debt; leaving it out understates credit sales by the whole B/R figure.
- **Mark-up read as margin.** 25% on cost is 20% on sales. Applying 25% to sales, or 20% to cost, gives two of the four options in every ratio question.
- **Omitting capital payments from the cash book.** Furniture bought for cash is still a payment; leave it out and the missing cash sales figure is wrong by exactly that amount.
- **Double-counting a contra.** Cash paid into the bank is not a receipt in a combined cash-and-bank summary.
- **Ignoring an omitted liability in the opening statement of affairs.** It overstates opening capital and therefore *understates* the profit — and vice versa for an omitted asset.
- **Treating the proprietor's income tax or life insurance premium as a business expense.** Both are drawings.

## One-page revision summary

**Nature:** single entry = mixture of double entry (personal accounts), single entry (cash) and no
entry (real and nominal) · kept by small sole traders and firms; never by a company · defects: no
trial balance, no gross profit, no true and fair view, frauds undetected.

**Statement of Affairs vs Balance Sheet:** capital is the *balancing figure* vs taken from the
Capital A/c · estimates vs ledger balances · used to *find* capital vs to *present* it.

**Formulas:**

<div class="formula">Capital = Assets − Liabilities</div>
<div class="formula">Profit = Closing capital + Drawings − Further capital − Opening capital</div>
<div class="formula">Credit sales = Receipts + Discount allowed + Returns inward + Bad debts + B/R received + Set-off + Closing debtors − Opening debtors − B/R dishonoured</div>
<div class="formula">Credit purchases = Payments + Discount received + Returns outward + B/P accepted + Set-off + Closing creditors − Opening creditors − B/P dishonoured</div>
<div class="formula">B/R received = Collections + Discounted + Endorsed + Dishonoured + Closing B/R − Opening B/R</div>
<div class="formula">B/P accepted = Payments + Dishonoured + Closing B/P − Opening B/P</div>
<div class="formula">Cost of goods sold = Opening stock + Net purchases + Direct expenses − Closing stock</div>
<div class="formula">Margin = Mark-up ÷ (100 + Mark-up) · Mark-up = Margin ÷ (100 − Margin)</div>

**Conversion order:** opening statement of affairs → cash book → total debtors → total creditors →
bills accounts → Trading and P&amp;L → Balance Sheet (which must tally with capital built as
opening + profit + fresh capital − drawings).

**Ratios worth memorising:** 20% on cost = 16⅔% on sales · 25% on cost = 20% on sales · 33⅓% on
cost = 25% on sales · 50% on cost = 33⅓% on sales · 100% on cost = 50% on sales.
