A cloth wholesaler in Surat sells ₹80,000 of fabric to a retailer who cannot pay today but will
pay in three months. A verbal promise is worth nothing at the bank. What the wholesaler wants is
a piece of paper the retailer has *signed*, with a fixed amount and a fixed date on it — paper
solid enough that a bank will hand over cash for it tomorrow. That piece of paper is a **bill of
exchange** or a **promissory note**.

This chapter is two chapters wearing one coat. Half of it is **law**: what these instruments are,
and the rules that decide the exact date money falls due. The other half is **accounting**: the
journal entries in the two parties' books when the bill is held, discounted, endorsed, dishonoured,
renewed or retired early. Examiners love this chapter because the due-date rules give them
one-mark questions with exactly one right answer, and the accommodation-bill sums give them a
full-length problem. Both halves are learnable in an afternoon.

## 1. Two instruments, one idea

Both instruments say "this much money, on this date, to this person". They differ in *who does the
speaking*.

A **promissory note** (s. 4) is a written **unconditional promise**, signed by the person who owes
the money, to pay a certain sum to (or to the order of) a certain person or to the bearer. The
debtor writes it. Two parties: **maker** (the debtor, who promises) and **payee** (the creditor).

A **bill of exchange** (s. 5) is a written **unconditional order**, signed by the person who is
owed the money, directing another person to pay a certain sum to (or to the order of) a certain
person or to the bearer. The creditor writes it. Three parties: **drawer** (the creditor, who
gives the order), **drawee** (the debtor, who is ordered to pay) and **payee** (whoever is to
receive the money — very often the drawer himself).

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Hold on to the one-word difference: a note is a <strong>promise</strong> (made by the debtor);
    a bill is an <strong>order</strong> (given by the creditor). Everything else follows from that.
    Because a bill is an order given to somebody else, it needs that somebody's consent —
    so <strong>a bill requires acceptance, a promissory note never does</strong>. The maker of a
    note has already signed; there is nobody left to accept.
  </p>
</Callout>

Five features both must have, or the paper is not a negotiable instrument at all: **in writing**
and **signed**; **unconditional** ("I will pay ₹50,000 if the goods are sold" is not a promissory
note — but a stated future date is allowed, because a date is certain to arrive); a **certain sum
of money only** (not goods, not "₹50,000 and 200 metres of cloth"); payable **to a certain person,
to his order, or to bearer**; and duly **stamped**. A bank note or currency note is expressly
excluded from s. 4 — that is why the rupee note in your pocket is not a promissory note in law.

## 2. The parties, and who does what

| Role | On a promissory note | On a bill of exchange |
|---|---|---|
| Writes and signs it | Maker (the debtor) | Drawer (the creditor) |
| Ordered to pay | — | Drawee; once he signs, he is the **acceptor** |
| Receives the money | Payee | Payee (often the drawer) |
| Acceptance needed? | No | **Yes** |

Two more words: the **holder** is the person entitled to the instrument and to the money on it
(the payee is the first holder), and **endorsement** is signing the back to transfer it — the
signer is the **endorser**, the person it passes to the **endorsee**.

The same bill has two names depending on whose books you are in. In the **drawer's/holder's**
books it is a **Bills Receivable** (an asset). In the **acceptor's** books the very same paper is a
**Bills Payable** (a liability). Every entry in this chapter is easier if you first ask: *whose
books am I sitting in?*

## 3. Tenor: on demand, after date, after sight

**Tenor** is the period the instrument runs for. Three kinds:

- **On demand** (or "at sight", "on presentment") — payable the moment it is presented. **No days
  of grace.**
- **After date** — the period runs from the **date written on the instrument**.
- **After sight** — the period runs from the date the drawee **accepts** the bill (for a note,
  from the date it is presented for sight). This is the single most-tested distinction in the
  chapter.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    A bill dated 1 June and accepted on 8 June, payable "two months after sight", runs from
    <strong>8 June</strong>, not 1 June. Every after-sight question plants an option computed from
    the bill's date. If you have not been told an acceptance date, the bill cannot be an
    after-sight bill — read again.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Finding the due date

Three steps, in this order, every single time.

**Step 1 — find the day the bill is *expressed* to be payable.**

- *After date*: count from the date of the bill.
- *After sight*: count from the date of acceptance.
- **Months** (s. 23): the period ends on the **corresponding day** of the later month —
  30 November plus three months ends on 28 February (or 29 February in a leap year), because
  February has no 30th. The rule is: corresponding day, and if there is none, the **last day of
  that month**. Never convert months into 30-day blocks.
- **Days** (s. 24): count actual calendar days and **exclude the starting day**. 60 days from
  12 May = 19 days left in May + 30 in June + 11 in July = 11 July.

**Step 2 — add three days of grace.**

<div class="formula">Due date = day the bill is expressed to be payable + 3 days of grace</div>

Section 22 gives every time instrument three days of grace: it is at maturity "on the third day
after the day on which it is expressed to be payable". Grace is not a favour a bank may withhold —
it is part of the legal due date. But an instrument payable **on demand or at sight gets no
grace at all**.

**Step 3 — test the date you have landed on against the holiday rules** (next section).

Notice the order. Grace is added *before* the holiday test, never after — you test the date the
grace produced.

## 5. Holidays: the two rules that move a due date

**Public holiday → move BACK.** Section 25: when the day of maturity is a public holiday, the
instrument is deemed due on the **next preceding business day**. Public holiday here includes
Sundays and days notified by the Central Government. The three dates the examiner reaches for
again and again are **26 January**, **15 August** and **2 October** — learn to spot them at the
end of a calculation.

**Emergency holiday → move FORWARD.** If the holiday was *not* known when the bill was drawn —
an emergency holiday declared suddenly by the Government — the settled commercial practice, and
the treatment the Study Material applies, is that payment falls due on the **next succeeding
business day**.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Two directions, one memory hook: a <strong>known</strong> holiday you could plan around, so the
    law makes you pay <strong>early</strong> (preceding day). An <strong>emergency</strong> holiday
    nobody could plan around, so payment waits for the <strong>next</strong> working day.
  </p>
</Callout>

Unless a question says otherwise, treat Saturdays as ordinary working days for this purpose; only
Sundays and notified holidays move a due date.

## 6. Trade bills and accommodation bills

A **trade bill** is drawn out of a **genuine sale or purchase**. Real goods moved, a real debt
exists, and the bill merely records it in a stronger form.

An **accommodation bill** is drawn with **no underlying trade at all**. Two parties who trust each
other create a bill purely so that one (or both) can discount it and use the cash — "kite flying",
in market slang. The paper looks identical; only the purpose differs. The party who accepts without
owing anything is the **accommodating party**; the party who uses the cash is the **accommodated
party**.

The accounting difference is the point. A trade bill *settles a debtor/creditor balance*, so the
entries run through the personal accounts of buyer and seller. In an accommodation bill there is no
debt to settle, so the parties simply owe each other the **cash they handed over**, and the
**discount charge is shared** in whatever ratio they used the money.

## 7. The four fates of a bill

Once a holder has a bill in hand, exactly four things can happen to it: he can **retain** it till
maturity and collect it himself; **discount** it with a bank for cash now, less a charge;
**endorse** it to a creditor of his own, settling that debt with the paper; or **send it to the
bank for collection** on his behalf. Learn the four and the entry-writing half of this chapter
collapses into one table. Assume A draws on B for ₹50,000.

| Event | In A's books (drawer / holder) | In B's books (acceptor) |
|---|---|---|
| Bill drawn and accepted | Bills Receivable A/c Dr 50,000 · To B 50,000 | A A/c Dr 50,000 · To Bills Payable A/c 50,000 |
| (1) Retained, met at maturity | Cash/Bank Dr 50,000 · To Bills Receivable A/c 50,000 | Bills Payable A/c Dr 50,000 · To Cash/Bank 50,000 |
| (2) Discounted | Bank Dr (net) + Discount A/c Dr (charge) · To Bills Receivable A/c 50,000 | **No entry** |
| (3) Endorsed to C | C A/c Dr 50,000 · To Bills Receivable A/c 50,000 | **No entry** |
| (4) Sent for collection | Bills sent for Collection A/c Dr 50,000 · To Bills Receivable A/c 50,000 | **No entry** |
| Collection realised | Bank Dr 50,000 · To Bills sent for Collection A/c 50,000 | Bills Payable A/c Dr 50,000 · To Bank 50,000 |

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Read the right-hand column again. What the holder does with the bill is <strong>none of the
    acceptor's business</strong> — discounting, endorsing and sending for collection produce
    <strong>no entry whatsoever</strong> in the acceptor's books. His liability is the same
    ₹50,000 whoever ends up holding the paper. The acceptor's books wake up again only at payment,
    or at dishonour.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. Discounting a bill

Discounting is borrowing against the bill. The bank pays the holder the face value **less a
charge for the unexpired period** between the date of discounting and the due date.

<div class="formula">Discount = Face value × Rate per annum × (Unexpired months ÷ 12)</div>
<div class="formula">Net proceeds = Face value − Discount</div>

Two things students get wrong. First, the period runs **from the date of discounting to the due
date** — not from the date of the bill, and not for the bill's whole tenor. A three-month bill
discounted one month after it was drawn carries only **two months** of discount. Second, the
discount is a **finance expense of the holder** (debited to Discount on Bills A/c and written off
to Profit &amp; Loss), not a reduction of the customer's debt.

## 9. Dishonour and noting charges

A bill is **dishonoured** when the acceptor fails to pay it on the due date. At that instant the
bill dies as an instrument and the **original debt comes back to life** — so both parties reverse
the bill and restore the personal account.

To have proof of dishonour, the holder gets the bill **noted** (and, for foreign bills, protested)
by a Notary Public. The small fee is called **noting charges**. Two rules: the **holder pays** the
notary, because he is the one who needs the evidence; and the **party ultimately liable bears** it,
so the holder recovers it back down the chain until it reaches the acceptor, whose default caused
it. Noting charges are therefore **never an expense of the drawer** in an ordinary trade dishonour
— they are added to the amount debited to the acceptor.

Entries on dishonour of a ₹50,000 bill with ₹500 noting charges:

| Situation | In the holder's books | In B's (acceptor's) books |
|---|---|---|
| Bill was **retained** | B A/c Dr 50,500 · To Bills Receivable A/c 50,000 · To Cash (noting) 500 | Bills Payable A/c Dr 50,000 · Noting Charges A/c Dr 500 · To A 50,500 |
| Bill was **discounted** | B A/c Dr 50,500 · To Bank 50,500 | same as above |
| Bill was **endorsed to C** | B A/c Dr 50,500 · To C 50,500 | same as above |
| Bill was **sent for collection** | B A/c Dr 50,500 · To Bills sent for Collection 50,000 · To Cash 500 | same as above |

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    On dishonour of a <strong>discounted</strong> bill, the credit goes to <strong>Bank</strong>,
    not to Bills Receivable. The Bills Receivable A/c was already emptied when the bill was
    discounted — there is nothing left in it to credit. The bank simply takes back the full face
    value plus the noting charges it paid. The same logic gives Creditor C the credit in the
    endorsement row.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. Renewal of a bill

Sometimes the acceptor knows in advance that he cannot pay and asks for more time. The parties
**cancel the old bill and draw a fresh one** for a longer tenor. Because the acceptor is keeping
the drawer's money longer, he pays **interest for the extension**.

Three steps, always in this sequence. **(1) Cancel the old bill** exactly as though it were
dishonoured — both parties reverse it and restore the personal account, adding the noting charges
if it was actually noted first. **(2) Charge interest** for the extra period:

<div class="formula">Interest = Amount carried forward × Rate p.a. × (Months of extension ÷ 12)</div>

In the drawer's books, Acceptor A/c Dr · To Interest A/c (income); in the acceptor's books,
Interest A/c Dr · To Drawer (expense). **(3) Draw the new bill** for the balance now owing,
including the interest if it is being carried into the new bill rather than paid in cash.

The trap is step 2's base. If the acceptor pays part in cash and renews only the rest, interest is
charged on the **balance renewed**, not on the original face value — and never on the cash he has
just paid.

## 11. Retirement under rebate

The mirror image of renewal. The acceptor has spare cash and pays **before** the due date. In
return the holder allows him a **rebate** (also called a discount for early payment) for the period
the money is being received early.

<div class="formula">Rebate = Amount of the bill × Rate p.a. × (Unexpired months ÷ 12)</div>

The acceptor pays face value **minus** the rebate; the bill is then said to be **retired**.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Get the direction right, because it is worth a mark on its own. The <strong>holder gives up</strong>
    the rebate, so in the drawer's/holder's books it is an <strong>expense</strong> —
    Rebate on Bills Discounted (or Discount Allowed) A/c is <strong>debited</strong>. The
    <strong>acceptor saves</strong> it, so in his books it is an <strong>income</strong> — Rebate
    A/c is <strong>credited</strong>. Whoever gives the concession takes the debit.
  </p>
</Callout>

Entries for a ₹50,000 bill retired one month early with a ₹500 rebate:

- **Holder:** Bank A/c Dr 49,500 · Rebate on Bills A/c Dr 500 · To Bills Receivable A/c 50,000
- **Acceptor:** Bills Payable A/c Dr 50,000 · To Bank 49,500 · To Rebate A/c 500

## 12. Insolvency of the acceptor

When the acceptor is declared insolvent, the bill is dishonoured automatically — no presentment is
needed, because it is already known he cannot pay. The holder passes the ordinary dishonour entry,
then receives whatever the estate can pay, quoted as so many **paise in the rupee** (₹25,000 owing
with a first and final dividend of 40 paise in the rupee brings in ₹10,000), and writes the rest
off as a bad debt:

<div class="formula">Bad debts = Amount owing × (100 paise − dividend in paise) ÷ 100 paise</div>

Here ₹15,000, debited to Bad Debts A/c and closed to Profit &amp; Loss. Note the base: the dividend
is calculated on the **whole amount owing**, which includes any noting charges added on dishonour,
not on the bill's face value alone.

## 13. Accommodation bills: the accounting

Because no goods moved, the accommodation entries follow the **cash**, not a trade debt.

**One party accommodated.** A draws on B for ₹90,000 for three months purely for A's convenience,
discounts it, and keeps all the money. A alone bears the discount, and A alone must remit the full
₹90,000 to B before the due date so that B can meet the bill.

**Both parties accommodated — the sum you will actually be set.** A and B agree to share the
proceeds in an agreed ratio. Then:

<div class="formula">Discount = Face value × Rate p.a. × (Tenor months ÷ 12)</div>
<div class="formula">Each party's share of proceeds = Net proceeds × his ratio</div>
<div class="formula">Each party's share of the discount = Total discount × his ratio</div>

The party who discounted the bill remits the *other* party's share of the **net proceeds** — the
cash actually received, not the face value. The other party's share of the discount is his own
expense; he never gets it back. On the due date, the acceptor pays the whole face value and the
drawer reimburses him with **his own share of the face value**.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The classic accommodation slip is remitting a share of the <strong>face value</strong> instead
    of a share of the <strong>net proceeds</strong>. If ₹90,000 is discounted for ₹87,300 and split
    2 : 1, the drawer sends ₹29,100 (= 87,300 × 1/3), not ₹30,000. Sending ₹30,000 would mean the
    drawer silently absorbed the other party's discount.
  </p>
</Callout>

**If the accommodating party becomes insolvent**, the other party proves in his estate for the
amount owed and receives a dividend, writing the shortfall off as a bad debt exactly as in §12.

## 14. Bills receivable and bills payable books

When bills are numerous, a firm keeps two subsidiary books instead of journalising each one: a
**Bills Receivable Book** (bills drawn or received, with the drawee's name, date, tenor, due date
and amount) and a **Bills Payable Book** (bills accepted). Their periodic totals are posted to
Bills Receivable A/c and Bills Payable A/c, and the individual names are posted to the personal
accounts — exactly the pattern you met in the purchases and sales books in Chapter 2.

## Fully worked examples

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — five due dates, every rule in one place (moderate)">
  <p>
    Find the due date of each of the following bills. Assume the Government has notified
    26 January, 15 August and 2 October as public holidays, and treat Saturdays as working days.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(a) Bill dated 3 May 2026, payable three months after date</div>
    Corresponding day three months later = 3 August 2026 (a Monday)<br />
    + 3 days of grace = <strong>6 August 2026</strong>. Not a Sunday, not a notified holiday — it stands.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(b) Bill dated 30 November 2026, payable three months after date</div>
    February 2027 has no 30th day, so under s. 23 the period ends on the <strong>last day</strong> of
    the month = 28 February 2027<br />
    + 3 days of grace = <strong>3 March 2027</strong> (a Wednesday).<br />
    28 February 2027 happens to be a Sunday, but that is irrelevant — the holiday test applies to
    the date <em>after</em> grace, not before it.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(c) Bill dated 12 May 2026, payable 60 days after date</div>
    Exclude 12 May. 19 days of May + 30 of June = 49; 11 more days reach 11 July 2026<br />
    + 3 days of grace = <strong>14 July 2026</strong>.<br />
    (Two months would have given 12 July + 3 = 15 July — a different answer. Days means days.)
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(d) Bill dated 1 June 2026, accepted on 8 June 2026, payable two months after sight</div>
    After sight runs from acceptance: 8 June + 2 months = 8 August 2026<br />
    + 3 days of grace = <strong>11 August 2026</strong>.<br />
    (Counting from the bill's date would have given 4 August — the planted wrong answer.)
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(e) Bill dated 12 May 2026, payable three months after date</div>
    12 August 2026 + 3 days of grace = 15 August 2026 — Independence Day, a notified public holiday.<br />
    Section 25: move to the next <em>preceding</em> business day = <strong>14 August 2026</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">(f) Same bill as (e), but an emergency holiday is declared for 14 August 2026</div>
    An emergency holiday was unknown when the bill was drawn, so the due date moves
    <em>forward</em> to the next succeeding business day: <strong>17 August 2026</strong>
    (15 August is the notified holiday and 16 August 2026 is a Sunday).
  </div>
</Callout>

  <p>
    On 3 May 2026 Surat Textiles draws a bill on Modi Garments for ₹60,000 payable three months
    after date; Modi accepts it the same day. On 6 June 2026 Surat Textiles discounts the bill with
    its bank at 12% per annum. Find the due date, the discount and the net proceeds, and pass the
    entry in Surat Textiles' books.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Due date and unexpired period</div>
    3 May + 3 months = 3 August 2026; + 3 days of grace = <strong>6 August 2026</strong><br />
    Discounted 6 June 2026 → unexpired period 6 June to 6 August = <strong>2 months</strong>
    (not 3 — one month of the bill had already run)
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Discount and proceeds</div>
    Discount = 60,000 × 12% × 2/12 = <strong>₹1,200</strong><br />
    Net proceeds = 60,000 − 1,200 = <strong>₹58,800</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Entry in Surat Textiles' books</div>
    Bank A/c ................ Dr 58,800<br />
    Discount on Bills A/c ... Dr 1,200<br />
    &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;To Bills Receivable A/c 60,000<br />
    In Modi Garments' books: <strong>no entry at all</strong>.
  </div>
</Callout>

  </p>
  <div class="working-note">

  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — In Surat Textiles' books (drawer/holder)</div>

    &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;To Bank A/c 60,500<br />
    Bills Receivable A/c is <em>not</em> credited — it was emptied on 6 June when the bill was

  <div class="working-note">

</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — renewal with part payment and interest (hard)">
  <p>
    Ravi holds a bill of ₹36,000 accepted by Sunil, due 6 August 2026. On the due date Sunil cannot
    pay. The bill is noted, noting charges ₹400. Sunil pays ₹16,400 in cash and accepts a fresh
    bill for the balance for three months, together with interest at 12% per annum. Find the amount
    of the new bill and pass the entries in Ravi's books.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Total owing after dishonour</div>
    36,000 + 400 noting charges = <strong>₹36,400</strong><br />
    Entry: Sunil A/c Dr 36,400 · To Bills Receivable A/c 36,000 · To Cash A/c 400
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Cash received and balance renewed</div>
    Cash received ₹16,400 — this clears the ₹400 noting charges plus ₹16,000 of the bill<br />
    Balance to be carried forward = 36,400 − 16,400 = <strong>₹20,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Interest for the extension</div>
    Interest = 20,000 × 12% × 3/12 = <strong>₹600</strong><br />
    Charged on the balance renewed, never on the original ₹36,000 and never on the cash already
    received.<br />
    Entry: Sunil A/c Dr 600 · To Interest A/c 600
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — The new bill</div>
    New bill = 20,000 + 600 = <strong>₹20,600</strong><br />
    Entry: Bills Receivable A/c Dr 20,600 · To Sunil A/c 20,600<br />
    Sunil's account now closes: debits 36,400 + 600 = 37,000; credits 16,400 + 20,600 = 37,000.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — accommodation bill, proceeds and discount shared (hard)">
  <p>
    Needing funds, Anita draws a bill on Bharat for ₹90,000 for three months on 1 June 2026;
    Bharat accepts it as an accommodation. Anita discounts it at once with her bank at 12% per
    annum and the two agree to share the proceeds between Anita and Bharat in the ratio 2 : 1.
    Find each party's cash and each party's discount, and show what happens on the due date.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Discount and net proceeds</div>
    Discount = 90,000 × 12% × 3/12 = <strong>₹2,700</strong><br />
    Net proceeds = 90,000 − 2,700 = <strong>₹87,300</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Sharing the cash</div>
    Anita (2/3) = 87,300 × 2/3 = <strong>₹58,200</strong><br />
    Bharat (1/3) = 87,300 × 1/3 = <strong>₹29,100</strong> — Anita remits this to Bharat<br />
    She remits a share of the <em>proceeds</em>, not ₹30,000 (a share of the face value).
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Sharing the discount</div>
    Anita bears 2,700 × 2/3 = <strong>₹1,800</strong>; Bharat bears 2,700 × 1/3 = <strong>₹900</strong><br />
    Each debits his own Discount A/c; each figure is a real expense of that party.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — On the due date, 4 September 2026 (1 June + 3 months = 1 September, + 3 days of grace)</div>
    Bharat, as acceptor, pays the bank the full <strong>₹90,000</strong><br />
    Anita remits Bharat her share of the <em>face value</em> = 90,000 × 2/3 = <strong>₹60,000</strong><br />
    Bharat's net cost = 90,000 paid − 60,000 received − 29,100 taken earlier = ₹900, exactly his
    share of the discount. That reconciliation is the best check you have on this sum.
  </div>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

- **Forgetting the three days of grace** — the commonest one-mark loss in the paper. Every *time*
  bill gets grace; only on-demand and at-sight bills do not.
- **Running an after-sight bill from the bill's date.** After sight runs from **acceptance**; if an
  acceptance date is given at all, the examiner wants it used.
- **Turning months into 30-day blocks.** Months use corresponding days (s. 23); only "days" bills
  are counted day by day, excluding the first day (s. 24).
- **Applying the holiday test before adding grace.** Add grace first, then test the date you landed
  on.
- **Moving a public holiday forward.** A notified public holiday moves the due date **back**; only
  an **emergency** holiday moves it forward.
- **Discounting for the bill's whole tenor.** The charge covers only **discounting date to
  maturity**.
- **Crediting Bills Receivable when a discounted bill is dishonoured.** Credit **Bank** (or the
  endorsee, if it had been endorsed) — Bills Receivable was cleared when the bill left.
- **Treating noting charges as the drawer's expense.** He *pays*; the defaulting acceptor *bears*.
- **Charging renewal interest on the original face value** after a part payment. Interest runs on
  the amount actually carried forward.
- **Rebate on the wrong side.** The holder allows it and debits it as an expense; the acceptor
  credits it as income.
- **Remitting face value instead of proceeds** on an accommodation bill — and forgetting that the
  discount splits in the same ratio as the cash.
- **Passing an entry in the acceptor's books when the holder discounts or endorses.** There is
  none. His books move only at payment, dishonour, renewal or retirement.

## One-page revision summary

**Definitions:** Promissory note (s. 4) = unconditional **promise** by the debtor; two parties
(maker, payee); **no acceptance**. Bill of exchange (s. 5) = unconditional **order** by the
creditor; three parties (drawer, drawee/acceptor, payee); **acceptance required**. Both must be in
writing, signed, unconditional, for a certain sum of money only, and duly stamped.

**Due date, in three steps:**

<div class="formula">1. Expressed date — after date: from the bill's date · after sight: from acceptance · months: corresponding day, else last day of the month (s. 23) · days: count actual days, exclude the first (s. 24)</div>
<div class="formula">2. Add 3 days of grace (s. 22) — but never on a demand or sight instrument</div>
<div class="formula">3. Public holiday → PRECEDING business day (s. 25) · Emergency holiday → NEXT business day</div>

**Money formulas:**

<div class="formula">Discount = Face value × Rate p.a. × Unexpired months ÷ 12 · Proceeds = Face value − Discount</div>
<div class="formula">Renewal interest = Balance carried forward × Rate p.a. × Months of extension ÷ 12</div>
<div class="formula">Rebate on retirement = Bill amount × Rate p.a. × Unexpired months ÷ 12</div>
<div class="formula">Insolvency: recovery = Amount owing × dividend in paise ÷ 100 · Bad debts = the balance</div>
<div class="formula">Accommodation: each share of proceeds = Net proceeds × ratio · each share of discount = Total discount × ratio</div>

**Rules that answer theory MCQs:** discounting, endorsing and sending for collection produce **no
entry in the acceptor's books** · on dishonour of a discounted bill credit **Bank**, of an endorsed
bill credit the **endorsee** · noting charges are paid by the holder and **borne by the party
liable** · dishonour revives the **original debt** in the personal account · rebate is the holder's
**expense** and the acceptor's **income** · accommodation bills split **proceeds** and **discount**
in the agreed ratio, and the face value on the due date · Bills Receivable is an **asset** in the
drawer's books and the same paper is Bills Payable, a **liability**, in the acceptor's.
