Meera runs a readymade-garments shop in Surat. All year she has bought stock, sold it, paid wages,
rent and carriage — and on 31 March she asks her accountant the only question that matters:
**"did I actually make money this year, and where does my business stand?"** Final accounts are
the answer, in three parts: a Trading Account for the margin on goods, a Profit &amp; Loss Account
for the overall profit, and a Balance Sheet for the position. Everything you learnt in Chapters 1
to 6 — journal, ledger, trial balance, rectification, depreciation — was preparation for this.

The exam tests the chapter in two ways: quick one-adjustment MCQs (where does carriage inwards
go? what is the provision charge?) and longer computations threading four or five adjustments
together. Every adjustment obeys one rule — **it hits the books in exactly two places** — so once
the table in §6 is yours, the chapter turns mechanical.

## 1. One question, three statements

Final accounts are prepared **from the trial balance plus a list of adjustments**, always in the
same order:

1. **Trading Account** → finds **gross profit**: what the goods themselves earned, before office
   and selling costs.
2. **Profit &amp; Loss Account** → starts with gross profit, absorbs all indirect expenses and
   other incomes, and ends at **net profit**.
3. **Balance Sheet** → net profit joins capital, and everything the business owns and owes is
   listed **as at** the closing date.

The order is forced: gross profit feeds the P&amp;L, and net profit feeds capital in the balance
sheet. Note the wording on top of each statement — Trading and P&amp;L are made **"for the year
ended…"** (they cover a period), while the balance sheet is **"as at…"** (a photograph of one
day).

One more distinction examiners like: Trading and P&amp;L are genuine **accounts** — part of the
double-entry system, closing off all nominal accounts. The balance sheet is **not an account** at
all; it is a **statement** listing the real and personal account balances that remain open. It
has no debit and credit sides in the ledger sense — "Liabilities" and "Assets" are customary
headings.

## 2. Capital vs revenue: what goes where

Chapter 2 taught the capital-vs-revenue test; final accounts are where it bites. In placement
terms:

- **Revenue expenditure** (benefit consumed this year — wages, rent, repairs, carriage) → debit
  of **Trading or P&amp;L**.
- **Capital expenditure** (benefit lasts years — machinery, furniture, installation costs) →
  **asset in the balance sheet**, entering the P&amp;L only slowly, as depreciation.
- **Revenue receipts** (sales, discount received, rent received) → credit of Trading or P&amp;L.
- **Capital receipts** (fresh capital, a loan taken) → **balance sheet**, as capital or
  liability. A loan is never income.

Misplace one item and *two* statements go wrong at once: repairs capitalised overstate both net
profit and assets. Classification is the quiet half of every final-accounts question.

## 3. The Trading Account: gross profit

The Trading Account isolates one thing: **the margin on goods bought and sold**. Only costs of
getting goods to a saleable state belong here — everything about running the office or finding
customers waits for the P&amp;L.

| Debit side | Credit side |
|---|---|
| Opening stock | Sales less sales returns (returns inward) |
| Purchases less purchase returns (returns outward) | Closing stock |
| Direct expenses: wages, carriage inwards, freight inwards, octroi, customs/import duty, factory fuel and power | Abnormal loss of stock, at cost (§9) |
| Gross profit c/d (balancing figure) | — or Gross loss c/d appears on the credit side instead |

**Direct expenses** are those incurred to buy the goods and bring them to the shop in saleable
condition: carriage **inwards**, freight **inwards**, octroi and import duty on purchases, wages
of loaders and factory workers, fuel and power of the workshop. What does **not** belong here:
**carriage outwards** (a delivery-to-customer cost — selling expense), salaries of office staff,
rent, insurance, advertising. All of those are indirect and go to the P&amp;L.

<div class="formula">Gross profit = Net sales − Cost of goods sold</div>
<div class="formula">Cost of goods sold = Opening stock + Net purchases + Direct expenses − Closing stock</div>

If cost of goods sold exceeds net sales, the balancing figure is a **gross loss**, carried to the
debit of the P&amp;L instead.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A combined trial-balance item <strong>"Wages and salaries"</strong> goes to the
    <strong>Trading Account</strong>, while <strong>"Salaries and wages"</strong> goes to the
    <strong>P&amp;L</strong> — by convention the first word tells you which element dominates.
    This is a presentation convention the SM follows, not a law; state it and move on.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Adjusted purchases.** Some trial balances show "Adjusted purchases" *and* closing stock inside
the trial balance. Adjusted purchases = opening stock + purchases − closing stock, meaning
closing stock has **already been absorbed**. In that case closing stock is **not** credited to
Trading again — it appears **only** in the balance sheet (see §6's once/twice rule).

## 4. The Profit and Loss Account: net profit

The P&amp;L opens with gross profit brought down on the credit side (or gross loss on the debit
side) and then collects every **indirect** expense and income:

- **Office and administration:** salaries, office rent, printing and stationery, insurance,
  audit fees, telephone.
- **Selling and distribution:** carriage outwards, advertising, salesmen's commission, bad
  debts, packing for delivery.
- **Finance and provisions:** interest on loans, discount allowed, provision for doubtful debts
  (the chain in §7), depreciation.
- **Abnormal items:** the uninsured part of stock lost by fire or theft (§9).
- **Credit side:** gross profit b/d, discount received, commission received, rent received,
  interest received, and any provision written back.

The closing balance is **net profit** — credited (added) to the capital account — or **net
loss**, deducted from capital.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Three things never enter the P&amp;L of a sole trader: <strong>drawings</strong>, the
    proprietor's <strong>income tax</strong>, and the proprietor's <strong>personal LIC
    premium</strong>. Income tax on a sole trader's income is a personal cost of the owner, so
    all three are treated as <strong>drawings</strong> and deducted from capital. An option that
    debits them to P&amp;L is a planted trap.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. The Balance Sheet: what you own and owe

The balance sheet lists, as at the closing date, every balance still open after Trading and
P&amp;L have swallowed the nominal accounts: assets on one side; capital and liabilities on the
other. Both sides must agree — the double-entry system guarantees it if every adjustment was
posted to both its places.

**There is no statutory format for a sole proprietor's balance sheet.** Companies must follow
Schedule III of the Companies Act 2013; a sole trader follows only custom. What custom does
prescribe is **marshalling** — arranging items in a deliberate order:

| Order of permanence | Order of liquidity |
|---|---|
| Most lasting first: goodwill, land and building, machinery, furniture … stock, debtors, bank, cash last | Most liquid first: cash, bank, debtors, stock … goodwill last |
| Preferred by most trading concerns | Preferred by banks and financial businesses |

On the liabilities side the same logic runs: under permanence, capital comes first, then
long-term loans, then current liabilities (creditors, bills payable, outstanding expenses);
under liquidity, the order reverses. Capital itself is shown as a working:
**opening capital + additional capital + net profit − drawings** (− net loss, if any).

## 6. The adjustments engine: every adjustment hits two places

Why two places? Items already **inside the trial balance** have had both legs of their double
entry posted — in final accounts they appear **once**. An **adjustment** is an entry the books
have *not yet* passed (stock counted after closing, salary unpaid, depreciation not recorded).
When you give effect to it, you are passing a fresh double entry — a debit *and* a credit — so
it must land in **two places**.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>Trial-balance item → once. Adjustment → twice.</strong> The reverse trap also
    appears: if "Outstanding wages" is already <em>inside</em> the trial balance, the adjusting
    entry was already passed — wages already include it, so the outstanding amount shows
    <strong>only once more, as a liability</strong> in the balance sheet. Never add it to wages
    again.
  </p>
</Callout>

The classic adjustments, with both effects:

| Adjustment | First effect | Second effect |
|---|---|---|
| Closing stock | Credit Trading | Asset in BS |
| Outstanding expense | Add to that expense (Trading/P&amp;L debit) | Liability in BS |
| Prepaid expense | Deduct from that expense | Asset in BS |
| Accrued income (earned, not received) | Add to that income (P&amp;L credit) | Asset in BS |
| Income received in advance | Deduct from that income | Liability in BS |
| Depreciation | Debit P&amp;L | Deduct from the asset in BS |
| Further bad debts | Add to bad debts (P&amp;L, via §7 chain) | Deduct from debtors in BS |
| New provision for doubtful debts | P&amp;L charge by the §7 formula | Deduct from debtors in BS |
| Provision for discount on debtors | Debit P&amp;L | Deduct from debtors (after the doubtful-debts provision) |
| Manager's commission payable | Debit P&amp;L (§8) | Liability (commission payable) in BS |
| Goods withdrawn by proprietor | Deduct from purchases, at cost | Add to drawings → deduct from capital |
| Goods given as free samples | Deduct from purchases | Advertising expense in P&amp;L |
| Interest on capital | Debit P&amp;L | Add to capital |
| Interest on drawings | Credit P&amp;L | Deduct from capital (add to drawings) |

Work every adjustment through this table and tick both boxes. In a full problem, a missed second
effect is exactly why a balance sheet refuses to tally.

## 7. Bad debts and the provision chain

Three layers, in order:

1. **Bad debts in the trial balance** — customers already written off during the year. Debtors
   in the trial balance are *already reduced* by these; the amount just sits in P&amp;L (once —
   it is a trial-balance item).
2. **Further bad debts** (adjustment) — write-offs decided at year end, *after* the trial
   balance. Two effects: add to bad debts, deduct from debtors.
3. **Provision for doubtful debts** — a prudence cushion for debts *likely* to fail, created as
   a percentage of **closing debtors after deducting further bad debts**.

The P&amp;L charge threads them together:

<div class="formula">P&amp;L charge = Bad debts (TB) + Further bad debts + New provision − Old provision</div>

The **old provision** (the credit balance in the trial balance) is money already set aside in
earlier years — so it is *deducted*. If the formula turns **negative**, the excess old provision
is **written back**: the amount appears as a **credit** in the P&amp;L, not a debit.

In the balance sheet: **Debtors − further bad debts − new provision**. The bad debts already in
the trial balance are *not* deducted again — the ledger did that when they were written off.

**Provision for discount on debtors** comes last in the chain. Cash discounts will only ever be
allowed to debtors who actually *pay* — so this provision is computed on **good debtors**:
debtors after deducting further bad debts *and* the doubtful-debts provision. It is a P&amp;L
debit and a further deduction from debtors in the balance sheet.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Two classic slips power most wrong options: <strong>adding</strong> the old provision
    instead of deducting it, and computing the new provision on debtors <strong>before</strong>
    removing further bad debts. Sequence is everything: write off first, then provide, then (if
    asked) provide for discount on what is left.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. Manager's commission: before vs after charging

A manager is often entitled to a percentage of profit — and the exam's favourite twist is
*which* profit.

**Case 1 — % of net profit *before* charging such commission.** The base is simply the net
profit before any commission:

<div class="formula">Commission = Net profit before commission × Rate ÷ 100</div>

**Case 2 — % of net profit *after* charging such commission.** Now the commission is a
percentage of a profit that has *already been reduced by the commission itself*. If profit after
charging is 100, commission is *rate*, so profit before charging must be 100 + rate:

<div class="formula">Commission = Net profit before commission × Rate ÷ (100 + Rate)</div>

Self-check the after-charging case every time: the commission you computed should equal rate %
of (net profit before commission − commission). If it does not, you divided by the wrong base.

Both cases have the same two effects: **debit P&amp;L**, and — since it is still unpaid — show
**commission payable** as a current liability in the balance sheet.

<Callout kind="example" label="Mini check — 10% after charging">
  <p>
    Net profit before commission ₹1,10,000; manager gets 10% of net profit after charging his
    commission. Commission = 1,10,000 × 10 ÷ 110 = <strong>₹10,000</strong>. Check: profit after
    charging = 1,00,000, and 10% of 1,00,000 = 10,000 ✓. The lazy answer, 10% × 1,10,000 =
    ₹11,000, fails the check.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Goods that leave without being sold

Four situations where stock exits without a normal sale. None of them is revenue — so none may
inflate sales; each is removed **at cost**.

**1. Goods withdrawn by the proprietor (drawings in kind).** The owner taking stock home is not
a customer buying it. Effects: **deduct cost from purchases** and **add cost to drawings**
(reducing capital). Always at **cost** — a business earns no profit selling to itself.

**2. Goods distributed as free samples.** Marketing, not sales. Effects: deduct cost from
purchases; debit **advertisement expense** in P&amp;L.

**3. Abnormal loss of stock (fire, theft).** The goods are gone for reasons that have nothing to
do with trading skill, so the Trading Account is protected: **credit Trading with the full cost
of the goods lost** (some solutions deduct from purchases instead — same effect on gross
profit). Then split the loss:

- Insurance claim **admitted** → shown as an **asset** (Insurance company account) in the BS.
- The **uninsured remainder** → **debit P&amp;L** as an abnormal loss.

Mini numbers: stock costing ₹40,000 burns; insurer admits ₹30,000. Trading credit 40,000; asset
30,000; P&amp;L debit 10,000. If nothing is insured, the whole ₹40,000 goes to P&amp;L.

**4. Goods sent on approval, wrongly treated as sales.** Sale-or-return goods still awaiting
customer approval at year end are *not* sales yet. Two reversals, each with two effects:

- Remove at **selling price**: deduct from **Sales** and from **Debtors**.
- Bring back at **cost**: add to **closing stock** (both Trading credit and BS asset).

Mini numbers: sales include ₹30,000 sent on approval (cost ₹24,000), customer silent on 31
March. Sales 7,50,000 → 7,20,000; debtors fall ₹30,000; closing stock rises ₹24,000.

## 10. Interest on capital and drawings

For a sole trader these are book adjustments that separate the owner's return from operating
profit:

- **Interest on capital** — the business "pays" the owner for the funds locked in: **debit
  P&amp;L**, **add to capital**.
- **Interest on drawings** — the owner "pays" the business for money pulled out early: **credit
  P&amp;L**, **deduct from capital** (equivalently, add to drawings).

Both are internal: no cash moves. The capital account working in the balance sheet becomes:
opening capital + additional capital + interest on capital + net profit − drawings − interest on
drawings.

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

- **Carriage direction.** Carriage/freight **inwards** → Trading; carriage **outwards** →
  P&amp;L. The wrong-side figure is always among the options.
- **Closing stock inside the trial balance.** With "adjusted purchases", closing stock is
  already absorbed — it goes **only** to the balance sheet. Crediting Trading again counts it
  twice.
- **Old provision added instead of deducted.** The chain is + new − old. An option roughly
  "your answer + 2 × old provision" is this trap.
- **Providing before writing off.** New provision is a % of debtors **after** further bad
  debts; the discount provision is computed after the doubtful-debts provision too.
- **After-charging commission on the wrong base.** Rate ÷ (100 + rate), and self-check against
  the reduced profit. Plain rate % of profit is the planted wrong option.
- **Drawings in kind at selling price.** Goods withdrawn or given as samples leave at **cost**;
  no business profits by consuming its own stock.
- **Approval goods left in sales.** Reverse sales *and* debtors at selling price; add stock at
  **cost** — mixing the two prices up moves gross profit by exactly the margin.
- **Abnormal loss netted silently.** Credit Trading with the **full cost**; the claim is an
  asset and only the uninsured part hits P&amp;L. Skipping the Trading credit understates gross
  profit.
- **Trial-balance items doubled.** Outstanding or prepaid amounts already inside the trial
  balance get their balance-sheet slot and nothing else — adjusting the expense again double
  counts.
- **Proprietor's income tax in P&amp;L.** It is drawings. So is the personal LIC premium paid
  from the business bank account.

## One-page revision summary

**Sequence:** Trading (gross profit) → P&amp;L (net profit) → Balance sheet (position). Trading
and P&amp;L are accounts for a period; the balance sheet is a statement as at a date. No
statutory format for a sole trader — marshal by permanence or liquidity.

**Formulas:**

<div class="formula">Cost of goods sold = Opening stock + Net purchases + Direct expenses − Closing stock</div>
<div class="formula">Gross profit = Net sales − Cost of goods sold</div>
<div class="formula">Adjusted purchases = Opening stock + Purchases − Closing stock (stock then appears only in the BS)</div>
<div class="formula">Provision chain charge = Bad debts (TB) + Further bad debts + New provision − Old provision (negative → P&amp;L credit)</div>
<div class="formula">Commission before charging = NP × Rate ÷ 100 · Commission after charging = NP × Rate ÷ (100 + Rate)</div>
<div class="formula">Closing capital = Opening + Additional capital + Interest on capital + Net profit − Drawings − Interest on drawings</div>

**The engine:** trial-balance item → once; adjustment → twice. Closing stock: Trading credit +
BS asset. Outstanding expense: add + liability. Prepaid: deduct + asset. Accrued income: add +
asset. Income in advance: deduct + liability. Depreciation: P&amp;L + off the asset. Further bad
debts: chain + off debtors. Drawings in kind: off purchases at cost + into drawings. Samples:
off purchases + advertising. Interest on capital: P&amp;L debit + add to capital; on drawings:
P&amp;L credit + off capital.

**Rules that answer theory MCQs:** wages-and-salaries → Trading, salaries-and-wages → P&amp;L
(convention) · discount provision only on good debtors, after the doubtful-debts provision ·
abnormal loss credited to Trading at cost · approval goods out of sales and debtors at selling
price, into stock at cost · proprietor's income tax = drawings · balance sheet is a statement,
not an account · a loan taken is a capital receipt, never income.
