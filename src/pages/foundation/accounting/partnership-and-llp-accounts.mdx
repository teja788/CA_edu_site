Two friends, Anil and Bhavna, open a stationery shop together. Anil puts in more money; Bhavna puts
in more hours. In the first good year they sit down to split the profit and the arguments begin.
Should Anil get something extra for the bigger capital? Should Bhavna get a salary for minding the
shop six days a week? Anil took ₹2,00,000 out of the till in June — does that cost him anything?
And when Bhavna's cousin joins next year and pays for a share of a business the two of them built,
who gets that money?

Every single one of those arguments has an accounting answer, and this chapter is that set of
answers. It is the largest chapter in Foundation Accounting and the most dependable source of a
full-length numerical in the exam. Work through it in order — the later units (admission,
retirement, death) simply reuse the tools built in the earlier ones.

## 1. What a partnership actually is

Section 4 of the **Indian Partnership Act 1932** defines it: partnership is the relation between
persons who have agreed to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting
for all. Pull that sentence apart and you get the three tests an examiner asks about:

1. **An agreement** between persons — it arises from contract, not from status. Members of a Hindu
   Undivided Family running a family business are not partners; they got there by birth.
2. **A business** carried on with a view to profit. Two neighbours sharing the rent of a godown are
   co-owners, not partners.
3. **Mutual agency** — "carried on by all or any of them acting for all". Each partner is both a
   principal and an agent of the others. This is the real test: a person who merely shares profits
   (a manager on a profit-linked bonus, a lender paid out of profits) is **not** a partner, because
   nobody is bound by his acts.

The persons are individually **partners**, collectively **a firm**, and the name they trade under is
the **firm name**. A firm is not a separate legal person: partners have **unlimited liability**,
jointly and severally, for the firm's debts — which is exactly what an LLP changes, in §14.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Sharing of profits is <strong>evidence</strong> of partnership, never <strong>proof</strong>.
    The clinching test is mutual agency. Every MCQ that describes "a manager who receives 20% of
    the profits" is testing this one point.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. The deed, and the rules that apply when there is no deed

A **partnership deed** is the written agreement: capitals, profit-sharing ratio, salaries, interest
on capital and drawings, admission and retirement terms, dispute settlement. It is not compulsory,
and registration of the firm is not compulsory either — but an unregistered firm cannot sue to
enforce its contractual rights, which is why almost every real firm registers.

When the deed is **silent, missing, or does not cover the point in dispute**, the Act supplies the
missing terms. Five of them carry almost all the exam marks:

| Question | Rule when the deed is silent |
|---|---|
| Do partners share profits in the capital ratio? | No — **equally**, however unequal the capitals. Losses are also shared equally. |
| Is a working partner paid a salary or commission? | **No remuneration** of any kind. |
| Is interest allowed on capital? | **No interest on capital.** |
| Is interest charged on drawings? | **No interest on drawings.** |
| A partner lends the firm money over and above his capital — is he paid? | **Yes: interest at 6% per annum** on that advance (s.13(d)). |

That last line is the one candidates get wrong. **Capital earns nothing; an advance earns 6%.** The
Act draws the line between money a partner agreed to subscribe (capital) and money he lent beyond
it (a loan to the firm).

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Two more silent-deed points worth a mark each. Interest on capital, where the deed
    <em>does</em> allow it, is payable <strong>only out of profits</strong> — so in a loss year it
    is not allowed at all. And a new partner cannot be admitted without the consent of
    <strong>all</strong> existing partners.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. Charge against profit vs appropriation of profit

This single distinction decides where an item goes, and therefore decides the answer to a whole
family of MCQs.

- A **charge** is a business expense: debited to the **Profit and Loss Account**, deducted *before*
  you arrive at net profit, allowed **whether or not there is a profit**.
- An **appropriation** is a distribution of profit already earned: it goes to the **Profit and Loss
  Appropriation Account** and is possible **only if there is a profit**.

| Item | Charge or appropriation? |
|---|---|
| Interest on a partner's **loan / advance** to the firm | **Charge** (P&amp;L Account) |
| **Rent** payable to a partner for his building used by the firm | **Charge** (P&amp;L Account) |
| Manager's salary and commission | **Charge** (P&amp;L Account) |
| Interest on **capital** | **Appropriation** |
| Partner's **salary or commission** | **Appropriation** |
| Transfer to **reserve** | **Appropriation** |
| Share of profit to partners | **Appropriation** |

A partner wearing his "outsider" hat — as a lender, or as a landlord — is dealt with as a charge.
A partner wearing his "owner" hat is dealt with as an appropriation.

## 4. The Profit &amp; Loss Appropriation Account

This account starts where the Profit and Loss Account stops. Its job is to divide the profit; it
never touches trading items. **Debit side:** interest on capital · partners' salaries and commission
· transfer to reserve · share of profit transferred to partners' capital or current accounts.
**Credit side:** net profit brought down from the P&amp;L Account · interest on drawings.

<div class="formula">Divisible profit = Net profit (after all charges) + Interest on drawings − Interest on capital − Partners' salary and commission − Transfer to reserve</div>

If the appropriations exceed the available profit, they are **not** all allowed in full. The
available profit is distributed **in the ratio of the appropriations claimed** — for example, if
interest on capital of ₹80,000 is due but only ₹60,000 of profit exists, each partner receives
three-quarters of his entitlement, unless the deed says interest on capital is a charge.

## 5. Interest on capital

Interest on capital is allowed **only if the deed provides for it**, at the deed's rate, and on the
capital *actually employed through the year*:

<div class="formula">Interest on capital = Opening capital × Rate + Additional capital × Rate × (months in use)/12</div>

If the question gives you **closing** capital, work backwards to the opening figure first — the
closing balance already contains this year's profit and drawings, and interest must never be
calculated on it:

<div class="formula">Opening capital = Closing capital − Additional capital introduced − Share of profit − Interest already credited + Drawings + Interest on drawings</div>

## 6. Interest on drawings

Charged only if the deed provides for it, and only for the **period the money was actually out of
the firm** — from the date of drawing to the year end.

**Product method (always safe).** Multiply each drawing by the months it stayed out, add the
products, then:

<div class="formula">Interest on drawings = Total of products (₹-months) × Rate × 1/12</div>

**Average-period shortcut** — only when the drawings are *equal in amount and evenly spaced*:

<div class="formula">Average period = (Months left after the first drawing + Months left after the last drawing) ÷ 2</div>

| Equal drawings made... | Average period |
|---|---|
| At the **beginning** of each month, for 12 months | **6.5 months** |
| At the **end** of each month, for 12 months | **5.5 months** |
| In the **middle** of each month, for 12 months | **6 months** |
| At the **beginning** of each quarter | **7.5 months** |
| At the **end** of each quarter | **4.5 months** |
| In the **middle** of each quarter | **6 months** |

Then interest = Total annual drawings × Rate × Average period ÷ 12.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Swapping 6.5 and 5.5. Money drawn at the <em>beginning</em> of a month is out of the firm
    <em>longer</em>, so it carries the <strong>larger</strong> average period, 6.5 months.
    Beginning-of-quarter 7.5 and end-of-quarter 4.5 follow the same logic. If a question says
    "drawings of ₹5,000 per month" without saying when, the convention is to take
    <strong>6 months</strong> (middle of the month).
  </p>
</Callout>

Two more cases. If a partner draws a **fixed sum for only part of the year** — say ₹10,000 a month
for the first six months — use the average-period formula from first principles, not the table. And
where **only the total drawings** are given with no dates at all, interest is taken for **6 months**.

## 7. Fixed and fluctuating capital accounts

**Fluctuating capital method (the default).** One account per partner, and everything goes through
it: opening and additional capital, interest on capital, salary, share of profit, drawings, interest
on drawings, share of loss. The balance therefore changes every year — hence the name.

**Fixed capital method.** Two accounts per partner. The **Capital Account** moves only when capital
is actually introduced or permanently withdrawn; the **Current Account** takes everything else —
interest on capital, salary, commission and share of profit as credits, drawings, interest on
drawings and share of loss as debits.

| | Fixed capital | Fluctuating capital |
|---|---|---|
| Number of accounts per partner | Two (Capital + Current) | One (Capital) |
| Capital balance | Unchanged unless capital is added or withdrawn | Changes every year |
| Can the balance be a debit? | The **Current Account can** show a debit balance; the Capital Account normally cannot | The Capital Account itself can go into debit |
| Used only when... | The deed specifically provides for it | No specific provision (this is the default) |

Under the fixed method, a **debit balance on a Current Account** appears on the assets side of the
balance sheet; a credit balance appears on the liabilities side with the capitals.

## 8. Guarantee of a minimum profit

Sometimes a partner — usually an incoming or junior one — is promised that his share will not fall
below a stated amount. Work it in three steps:

1. Divide the profit among all partners in the **normal profit-sharing ratio**.
2. If the guaranteed partner's share is **more than** the guarantee, stop; the guarantee does
   nothing.
3. If it is **less**, the shortfall is the **deficiency**, recovered from the guaranteeing partners
   in the ratio the deed states — or, if the deed is silent, in the **remaining partners' mutual
   profit-sharing ratio**.

Example: A, B and C share 5:3:2 and C is guaranteed a minimum of ₹1,00,000. Profit is ₹4,00,000.
Normal shares: A ₹2,00,000, B ₹1,20,000, C ₹80,000. C's deficiency is ₹20,000, borne by A and B in
5:3 → A ₹12,500 and B ₹7,500. Final: A ₹1,87,500, B ₹1,12,500, C ₹1,00,000 — still ₹4,00,000 in
total, which is your check.

If instead **one partner alone** guarantees C, the whole ₹20,000 comes off that partner's share. And
if the guarantee is given *by the firm to* a partner, all the others bear it in their mutual ratio.

## 9. Past adjustments: fixing old years with one entry

Sometimes an error surfaces after the accounts of one or more past years are closed — interest on
capital was omitted, a salary was never credited, profits were divided in the wrong ratio. You do
**not** reopen the old Appropriation Account. You prepare a small statement with two halves for each
partner — **what should have been given** (Cr) and **what was actually given** (Dr) — net them off,
and pass **one adjusting journal entry** through the partners' capital (or current) accounts.

Example: L, M and N have fixed capitals of ₹4,00,000, ₹3,00,000 and ₹2,00,000 and share profits
3:2:1. Interest on capital at 6% p.a. was omitted for one year.

| Partner | Interest that should have been credited | Extra profit already taken (3:2:1 of ₹54,000) | Net |
|---|---|---|---|
| L | 24,000 | 27,000 | 3,000 Dr |
| M | 18,000 | 18,000 | Nil |
| N | 12,000 | 9,000 | 3,000 Cr |
| | **54,000** | **54,000** | — |

Adjusting entry: **L's Capital A/c Dr ₹3,000 — To N's Capital A/c ₹3,000.** The two columns always
total the same figure, and the net column always sums to nil. That is your arithmetic check.

## 10. Goodwill: nature and valuation

**Goodwill** is the value of a firm's reputation — the extra earning power that lets an established
firm earn more than a normal return on the capital it employs. It is an **intangible asset**, and in
a partnership question it matters at exactly three moments: admission, retirement and death. It
grows out of location, product quality, a long customer list, efficient management, a recognised
brand and simple length of existence; it shrinks with poor service or an obsolete product.

### Average profit method

<div class="formula">Goodwill = Adjusted average profit × Number of years' purchase</div>

Before averaging, **adjust** the past profits so they represent normal, recurring earning power: add
back abnormal losses (fire, theft) and non-recurring expenses; deduct abnormal or non-trading gains
(profit on sale of a fixed asset, insurance windfalls, income from investments); deduct any expense
that will start to be incurred in future (a manager's salary the working partner used to provide
free); add back any expense that will stop.

Where recent years matter more, the question may ask for a **weighted** average — multiply each
year's adjusted profit by its weight (1, 2, 3, 4 with the latest year heaviest) and divide by the
total of the weights.

### Super profit method

**Super profit** is the excess of the firm's earning power over what a normal business of that size
would earn:

<div class="formula">Normal profit = Capital employed × Normal rate of return</div>
<div class="formula">Super profit = Adjusted average profit − Normal profit</div>
<div class="formula">Goodwill = Super profit × Number of years' purchase</div>

Capital employed here means **capital employed in the business** = all assets used in the business
(excluding goodwill, non-trade investments and fictitious assets) − outside liabilities. If the
super profit is nil or negative, the firm has no goodwill.

### Capitalisation methods

Two routes, and — worth knowing — **they always give the same answer** on the same data.

<div class="formula">Capitalisation of average profit: Goodwill = (Adjusted average profit ÷ Normal rate of return) − Capital employed</div>
<div class="formula">Capitalisation of super profit: Goodwill = Super profit ÷ Normal rate of return</div>

The first asks "what capital would a normal business need to earn this profit?" and treats the
excess over the actual capital as goodwill; the second capitalises only the surplus earning. Because
normal profit is itself capital employed × the rate, the two are algebraically identical.

### Annuity method

Super profit is expected for a limited number of years, so its **present value** — not its face
total — is the goodwill. Multiply the super profit by the annuity factor (present value of ₹1 per
year) the question gives you; Foundation questions always supply the factor.

<div class="formula">Goodwill = Super profit × Annuity factor for n years at the given rate</div>

### AS 26: goodwill is never raised in the books

This rule governs everything in §11 to §13. Under **AS 26**, only **purchased** goodwill — goodwill
for which a consideration in money or money's worth has actually been paid — can be recognised as an
asset. **Self-generated goodwill is never recorded.** So when partners reconstitute the firm, you do
**not** pass "Goodwill A/c Dr — To Old Partners' Capital A/c". The goodwill is **adjusted directly
through the partners' capital accounts**: the partner who gains a share compensates the partner who
sacrifices one, and goodwill never appears on the balance sheet as a result.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Raising goodwill in the books on admission and then writing it off again is the pre-AS-26
    treatment. Old question banks are full of it. In the current syllabus it is
    <strong>wrong</strong> — one net adjusting entry through capital accounts is the only accepted
    route. If goodwill already appears in the old balance sheet, it must be
    <strong>written off among the OLD partners in the OLD ratio</strong> before anything else is
    done.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 11. Admission of a partner

Admitting a partner needs the consent of all existing partners. The new partner acquires two rights:
a share in the **future profits** and a share in the **assets** of the firm. Six things happen, in
this order.

### Sacrificing ratio

The old partners give up part of their share so the new partner can have one.

<div class="formula">Sacrificing ratio = Old ratio − New ratio</div>

If the question does not say where the new partner's share comes from, assume the old partners
sacrifice **in their old ratio** — the sacrificing ratio then equals the old ratio, and each old
partner's new share is simply his old share × (1 − new partner's share). If the question instead
says the new partner acquires 1/6 from A and 1/12 from B, deduct those specific fractions and the
sacrificing ratio is 1/6 : 1/12 = 2 : 1.

### Premium for goodwill

The new partner pays for the share of a reputation he did not build. That payment is called the
**premium for goodwill**.

- **Premium brought in cash** and retained in the business: Cash/Bank A/c Dr; To Premium for
  Goodwill A/c. Then Premium for Goodwill A/c Dr; **To the sacrificing partners' Capital A/cs in
  the sacrificing ratio.**
- **Premium withdrawn** by the old partners: add the withdrawal entry after the above.
- **Premium paid privately**, outside the firm: **no entry at all** in the firm's books.
- **New partner unable to bring his share of goodwill in cash**: debit his Capital (or Current)
  Account with his share and credit the sacrificing partners.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The premium goes in the <strong>sacrificing ratio</strong>, never in the old ratio and never in
    the new ratio — unless the two happen to coincide, which is exactly what makes the trap work.
    Wrong options in an MCQ almost always share the premium in the old profit-sharing ratio.
  </p>
</Callout>

### Hidden goodwill on admission

Sometimes the question never states the goodwill; it hides it in the new partner's capital. Infer it:

<div class="formula">Implied total capital of the firm = New partner's capital × (Reciprocal of his share)</div>
<div class="formula">Hidden goodwill = Implied total capital − Combined adjusted capitals of all partners including the new one</div>

The combined capital figure must be the one **after** revaluation and reserve adjustments and
**including** the cash the new partner brings.

### Revaluation Account

Assets and liabilities are restated to fair values so the incoming partner neither gains nor suffers
from changes that belong to the past. **Debit** it with decreases in assets, increases in
liabilities, unrecorded liabilities now brought in, and provisions newly created or increased;
**credit** it with increases in assets, decreases in liabilities, unrecorded assets now brought in,
and provisions no longer needed.

<div class="formula">Revaluation profit (or loss) → OLD partners only, in the OLD ratio</div>

That line is the whole point of the account and the single most-tested sentence in this unit. If the
question says the assets and liabilities are to appear at their **old** values in the new balance
sheet, use a **Memorandum Revaluation Account** instead: its first half goes to the old partners in
the old ratio and the reversing second half to **all** partners, in the new ratio.

### Reserves, accumulated profits and losses

Any General Reserve, Reserve Fund, Workmen's Compensation Reserve (to the extent not needed) or
credit balance of the Profit and Loss Account was earned before the new partner arrived, so it is
transferred to the **old partners' capital accounts in the old ratio**. A **debit** balance of the
Profit and Loss Account, or Advertisement Suspense, is a past loss and is likewise debited to the
old partners in the old ratio.

### Adjustment of capitals

Two directions, and the question always tells you which. If the **new partner's capital is given**,
total capital of the firm = his capital ÷ his share; split that in the new ratio, compare with each
old partner's adjusted capital, and bring in or withdraw the difference in cash (or through a current
account). If the **old partners' capitals are given**, gross up their combined adjusted capital by
their combined share and take the new partner's share of the result.

## 12. Retirement of a partner

A partner may retire with the consent of all partners, or under the deed, or — in a partnership at
will — by written notice to all the others. The remaining partners continue: the firm is
reconstituted, not dissolved.

### Gaining ratio

The mirror image of the sacrificing ratio:

<div class="formula">Gaining ratio = New ratio − Old ratio</div>

If nothing is said, the continuing partners gain **in their old mutual ratio**, and the new ratio is
simply the old ratio of the continuing partners.

### Goodwill on retirement

The outgoing partner is entitled to his share of the firm's goodwill. Consistent with AS 26 no
goodwill is raised; the **gaining partners compensate the outgoing partner** directly:

<div class="formula">Gaining Partners' Capital A/cs Dr (in the gaining ratio) — To Retiring Partner's Capital A/c (with his share of goodwill)</div>

**Hidden goodwill on retirement.** If the partners fix the total amount payable to the retiring
partner without stating the goodwill, work it back: his share of goodwill = amount payable − his
capital balance after revaluation, reserves and everything else; gross that up by the reciprocal of
his share to get the firm's goodwill.

### Settling the outgoing partner

Everything else follows the admission template, except that the revaluation profit or loss and the
reserves now go to **all partners including the retiring one, in the OLD ratio** — he was a partner
while those values built up.

His final balance is settled by a lump sum in cash, or by transfer to his **Loan Account** (on which
he is entitled to interest at **6% p.a.** if nothing is agreed), or by an agreed mix paid in
instalments. That Loan Account is a balance-sheet liability until it is discharged.

## 13. Death of a partner

Death is retirement the deceased did not choose, and the accounting is nearly identical — with two
differences. The settlement is made to the **executor** (legal representative), not to the partner;
and death happens on **some date in the middle of the year**, so the deceased is entitled to a share
of the profit earned from the start of the year to that date.

### Profit to the date of death

Two accepted bases, and the deed says which:

<div class="formula">Time basis: Profit to date = Last year's profit × (Months to the date of death ÷ 12), then × his share</div>
<div class="formula">Turnover (sales) basis: Profit to date = Sales up to the date of death × Last year's rate of net profit on sales, then × his share</div>

The turnover basis is the more realistic of the two, and the one examiners prefer when they give you
sales figures for both periods: compute the profit rate from the **previous completed year** (profit
÷ sales × 100) and apply it to the current period's sales. The share of profit to date is debited to
the Profit and Loss **Suspense** Account and credited to the deceased partner's capital account.

### The executor's account

The deceased partner's Capital Account is closed by transfer to **his Executor's Account**.
**Credits:** capital balance · share of reserves and accumulated profits (old ratio) · share of
revaluation profit (old ratio) · share of goodwill (borne by the continuing partners in their gaining
ratio) · share of profit to the date of death · interest on capital to the date of death if the deed
provides for it. **Debits:** drawings to the date of death · interest on drawings · share of
revaluation loss · share of accumulated losses.

Until it is paid, the Executor's Account is a liability of the firm, carrying interest at **6% p.a.**
where nothing else is agreed.

## 14. Limited Liability Partnership (LLP)

An LLP is the hybrid the Limited Liability Partnership Act 2008 created: the internal flexibility of
a partnership with the limited liability of a company. It is a **body corporate**, incorporated by
registration with the Registrar, and a **legal entity separate from its partners**. Its key features:

- **Separate legal entity** — it can own property, sue and be sued in its own name.
- **Perpetual succession** — a change in partners does not affect its existence, rights or
  liabilities.
- **Limited liability** — a partner is not personally liable for the LLP's obligations merely
  because he is a partner; his exposure is limited to his agreed contribution (fraud aside).
- **Minimum two partners**, no upper limit. An individual or a body corporate may be a partner.
- **At least two designated partners**, both individuals, of whom **at least one must be resident in
  India**. Designated partners answer for statutory compliances and filings.
- **Mutual rights governed by the LLP agreement**; where it is silent, the Act's default provisions
  apply — much as the 1932 Act supplies terms for a firm.
- **No mutual agency between partners** — each partner is an agent of the LLP, not of the other
  partners. That is precisely why one partner's misconduct does not sink the others.

### Partnership firm vs LLP

| Point | Partnership firm (Act of 1932) | LLP (Act of 2008) |
|---|---|---|
| Governing law | Indian Partnership Act 1932 | Limited Liability Partnership Act 2008 |
| Legal status | Not a separate legal entity | **Body corporate**, separate legal entity |
| Liability of partners | **Unlimited**, joint and several | **Limited** to the agreed contribution |
| Perpetual succession | No — changes with the partners | **Yes** |
| Registration | Optional (but an unregistered firm cannot sue) | **Compulsory** — it exists only on incorporation |
| Maximum number of partners | Restricted (50, under the Companies Rules) | **No limit** |
| Designated partners | Not applicable | At least two, one resident in India |
| Ownership of property | Held by the partners jointly | Held by the **LLP itself** |
| Agency | Partner is an agent of the firm **and of the other partners** | Partner is an agent of the LLP **only** |
| Annual filings with the Registrar | None | Annual Return and Statement of Account and Solvency |

### LLP accounts

The bookkeeping is reassuringly familiar. Partners bring in **contribution** rather than "capital" —
in cash, in tangible or intangible property, or in other benefits, with its monetary value stated in
the accounts. The LLP prepares the same Profit and Loss Account and shares profits under the LLP
agreement, through an appropriation section that behaves exactly like the one in §4. It must keep
**proper books of account** at its registered office, on the cash or the accrual basis; each year it
files a **Statement of Account and Solvency** and an **Annual Return** with the Registrar; and its
accounts must be **audited** where turnover or contribution crosses the limits prescribed under the
LLP Rules.

## Fully worked examples

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — appropriation account, with the no-deed twist (moderate)">
  <p>
    Anil and Bhavna share profits 3:2 with fixed capitals of ₹6,00,000 and ₹4,00,000. Their deed
    allows interest on capital at 8% p.a., a salary to Anil of ₹12,000 per month, and interest on
    drawings at 10% p.a. On 1 April 2025 Bhavna advanced a loan of ₹2,00,000 to the firm; the deed
    says nothing about interest on it. Anil drew ₹20,000 at the end of every quarter; Bhavna drew
    ₹1,00,000 on 1 October 2025. Profit for the year ended 31 March 2026, before any of the above,
    was ₹5,00,000. Divide the profit. Then redo it assuming there is no deed at all.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — the charge comes off first</div>
    Interest on Bhavna's loan = 2,00,000 × 6% = ₹12,000. The deed is silent, so s.13(d) fixes 6%; it is a <strong>charge</strong>, so it is debited to the P&amp;L Account.<br />
    Net profit carried to the Appropriation Account = 5,00,000 − 12,000 = <strong>₹4,88,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — interest on drawings</div>
    Anil: equal amounts at the END of each quarter → average period 4.5 months.<br />
    80,000 × 10% × 4.5/12 = <strong>₹3,000</strong> (product check: 20,000 × (9 + 6 + 3 + 0) = 3,60,000 ₹-months × 10% ÷ 12 = 3,000)<br />
    Bhavna: 1,00,000 out for 6 months → 1,00,000 × 10% × 6/12 = <strong>₹5,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — the appropriation account</div>
    Credits: net profit 4,88,000 + interest on drawings 8,000 = 4,96,000<br />
    Debits: interest on capital (48,000 + 32,000) = 80,000; Anil's salary 12,000 × 12 = 1,44,000<br />
    Divisible profit = 4,96,000 − 80,000 − 1,44,000 = <strong>₹2,72,000</strong><br />
    Anil 3/5 = ₹1,63,200 · Bhavna 2/5 = ₹1,08,800
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — the twist: no deed</div>
    No salary, no interest on capital, no interest on drawings. But Bhavna's <em>advance</em> still earns 6% — that right comes from the Act, not from the deed.<br />
    Divisible profit = 5,00,000 − 12,000 = 4,88,000, shared <strong>equally</strong>: <strong>₹2,44,000 each</strong>, even though the capitals are 6:4.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — goodwill: super profit and capitalisation on one set of data (moderate-hard)">
  <p>
    A firm's capital employed is ₹20,00,000 and the normal rate of return in this line of business
    is 12%. Profits of the last four years: 2022–23 ₹3,40,000; 2023–24 ₹3,60,000; 2024–25 ₹4,00,000
    (including ₹40,000 profit on sale of a machine); 2025–26 ₹3,50,000 (after charging a fire loss
    of ₹30,000, an event that will not recur). From next year a manager must be paid ₹60,000 a year
    for work a partner had been doing free. Value the goodwill by (a) super profit at 3 years'
    purchase, (b) capitalisation of super profit, and (c) capitalisation of average profit.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — adjusted average profit</div>
    2022–23: 3,40,000<br />
    2023–24: 3,60,000<br />
    2024–25: 4,00,000 − 40,000 (abnormal, non-recurring gain) = 3,60,000<br />
    2025–26: 3,50,000 + 30,000 (abnormal loss added back) = 3,80,000<br />
    Total 14,40,000 ÷ 4 = 3,60,000; less manager's salary 60,000 (a future recurring cost) = <strong>₹3,00,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — super profit</div>
    Normal profit = 20,00,000 × 12% = 2,40,000<br />
    Super profit = 3,00,000 − 2,40,000 = <strong>₹60,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — the three valuations</div>
    (a) Super profit × 3 = 60,000 × 3 = <strong>₹1,80,000</strong><br />
    (b) Capitalisation of super profit = 60,000 ÷ 0.12 = <strong>₹5,00,000</strong><br />
    (c) Capitalisation of average profit = (3,00,000 ÷ 0.12) − 20,00,000 = 25,00,000 − 20,00,000 = <strong>₹5,00,000</strong><br />
    (b) and (c) agree — they always do, because normal profit is itself capital employed × the rate. For contrast, average profit at 3 years' purchase would give 3,00,000 × 3 = ₹9,00,000: the method matters enormously, so read the question twice.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — admission: sacrifice, premium, revaluation (hard)">
  <p>
    A and B share profits 3:2. Their capitals are ₹4,00,000 and ₹3,00,000 and the balance sheet
    shows a General Reserve of ₹1,00,000. C is admitted for a 1/5 share; he brings ₹3,00,000 as
    capital and ₹1,00,000 as premium for goodwill, both in cash, and the premium is retained in the
    business. On revaluation: land is worth ₹90,000 more, stock is written down by ₹20,000, a
    provision of ₹10,000 is made against debtors, and an unrecorded creditor of ₹15,000 comes to
    light. Find the sacrificing ratio, the new ratio, and each partner's capital afterwards.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — sacrificing and new ratios</div>
    Nothing says where C's share comes from, so A and B sacrifice in their old ratio → sacrificing ratio <strong>3:2</strong>.<br />
    A = 3/5 × 4/5 = 12/25 · B = 2/5 × 4/5 = 8/25 · C = 1/5 = 5/25 → new ratio <strong>12 : 8 : 5</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Revaluation Account</div>
    Credit: land 90,000. Debit: stock 20,000 + provision 10,000 + unrecorded creditor 15,000 = 45,000<br />
    Revaluation <strong>profit ₹45,000</strong> → to the OLD partners in the OLD ratio 3:2: A ₹27,000, B ₹18,000. C gets nothing.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — premium and reserve</div>
    Premium ₹1,00,000 in the <strong>sacrificing</strong> ratio 3:2 → A ₹60,000, B ₹40,000.<br />
    General Reserve ₹1,00,000 in the OLD ratio 3:2 → A ₹60,000, B ₹40,000.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — capital accounts</div>
    A: 4,00,000 + 60,000 + 27,000 + 60,000 = <strong>₹5,47,000</strong><br />
    B: 3,00,000 + 40,000 + 18,000 + 40,000 = <strong>₹3,98,000</strong><br />
    C: <strong>₹3,00,000</strong> (the premium he paid is <em>not</em> part of his capital)
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — retirement: gaining ratio and the hidden-goodwill variant (hard)">
  <p>
    X, Y and Z share profits 5:3:2. Y retires, and X and Z agree to share future profits 3:2. The
    goodwill of the firm is valued at ₹3,00,000. (i) Pass the goodwill adjustment. (ii) Then take a
    different version of the same question: the goodwill is <em>not</em> stated, but the partners
    agree that Y will be paid ₹4,20,000 in full settlement, and after revaluation and reserves his
    capital account stands at ₹3,30,000. Find the firm's goodwill.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — gaining ratio</div>
    X: 3/5 − 5/10 = 6/10 − 5/10 = 1/10<br />
    Z: 2/5 − 2/10 = 4/10 − 2/10 = 2/10<br />
    Gaining ratio <strong>1 : 2</strong> — note Z gains twice as much as X even though X has the bigger share.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — goodwill adjustment (part i)</div>
    Y's share of goodwill = 3,00,000 × 3/10 = ₹90,000, borne by the gainers 1:2 → X ₹30,000, Z ₹60,000<br />
    <strong>X's Capital A/c Dr ₹30,000; Z's Capital A/c Dr ₹60,000; To Y's Capital A/c ₹90,000.</strong> No Goodwill Account is opened (AS 26).
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — hidden goodwill (part ii)</div>
    Excess paid over the balance standing to Y's credit = 4,20,000 − 3,30,000 = ₹90,000. That excess <em>is</em> Y's share of goodwill.<br />
    Firm's goodwill = 90,000 × 10/3 = <strong>₹3,00,000</strong> — the same firm, reached from the other end.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — death mid-year: the executor's amount on a turnover basis (hard)">
  <p>
    P, Q and R share profits 2:2:1. R dies on 31 August 2025; the firm closes its books on 31 March.
    R's capital on 1 April 2025 was ₹2,40,000 and he had drawn ₹35,000 up to the date of death. The
    balance sheet showed a General Reserve of ₹1,50,000. Revaluation on the date of death produced a
    profit of ₹75,000, and the firm's goodwill was valued at ₹4,00,000. The deed says R's share of
    the current year's profit is to be computed on the basis of turnover. Sales for the year ended
    31 March 2025 were ₹40,00,000 on which the profit was ₹5,00,000; sales from 1 April 2025 to 31
    August 2025 were ₹12,00,000. Find the amount payable to R's executor.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — profit to the date of death (turnover basis)</div>
    Last year's rate of net profit on sales = 5,00,000 ÷ 40,00,000 = 12.5%<br />
    Profit for 1 Apr – 31 Aug 2025 = 12,00,000 × 12.5% = 1,50,000<br />
    R's share = 1,50,000 × 1/5 = <strong>₹30,000</strong><br />
    <em>(On a time basis it would have been 5,00,000 × 5/12 × 1/5 = ₹41,667 — a different answer, so use the basis the deed names.)</em>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — R's share of goodwill</div>
    4,00,000 × 1/5 = ₹80,000, borne by P and Q in their gaining ratio. Their new ratio is 2:2, their old was 2:2 out of 5, so they gain equally → <strong>₹40,000 each</strong>, debited to their capital accounts. No Goodwill Account is raised.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — R's Executor's Account</div>
    Capital 2,40,000<br />
    + General Reserve 1,50,000 × 1/5 = 30,000<br />
    + Revaluation profit 75,000 × 1/5 = 15,000<br />
    + Goodwill 80,000<br />
    + Profit to date of death 30,000<br />
    − Drawings 35,000<br />
    = <strong>₹3,60,000 payable to R's executor</strong>, carrying interest at 6% p.a. until paid if nothing else is agreed.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

- **Sharing profit in the capital ratio when the deed is silent.** The Act says **equally**, however
  lopsided the capitals. This is the single most common error in the whole chapter.
- **Refusing interest on a partner's loan because "the deed is silent".** Capital earns nothing when
  the deed is silent, but an **advance earns 6% p.a.** — and it is a *charge*, deducted before net
  profit, not an appropriation.
- **Putting rent payable to a partner in the Appropriation Account.** Rent and a partner's loan
  interest are charges; his salary, commission and interest on capital are appropriations.
- **Swapping 6.5 and 5.5 months.** Beginning-of-month drawings are out longer → 6.5. End-of-month →
  5.5. Beginning-of-quarter → 7.5, end-of-quarter → 4.5.
- **Calculating interest on capital on the closing balance** under fluctuating capitals. The closing
  figure already contains this year's profit and drawings; work back to the opening capital first.
- **Sharing the premium for goodwill in the old ratio.** It goes in the **sacrificing** ratio.
  Likewise, the retiring partner's goodwill is borne in the **gaining** ratio, not the old ratio.
- **Giving revaluation profit to the incoming partner.** On admission, revaluation profit or loss and
  all reserves belong to the **old partners in the old ratio**. On retirement and death they belong
  to **all partners including the outgoing one**, in the old ratio.
- **Raising a Goodwill Account.** AS 26 forbids recognising self-generated goodwill. Adjust through
  capital accounts. If goodwill is already sitting in the old balance sheet, write it off among the
  old partners in the old ratio first.
- **Treating the new partner's premium as part of his capital.** It is credited to the sacrificing
  partners, not to him.
- **Getting the guarantee deficiency ratio wrong.** Read whether the deficiency is borne by all the
  others in their mutual ratio, by one named partner, or in a stated ratio — all three appear.
- **Reopening past years for a past adjustment.** One net entry through the capital accounts, based
  on a two-column statement whose totals must agree.
- **Using the time basis when the deed says turnover** (or the reverse) for the profit to the date
  of death. The two give different numbers, and both appear as options.
- **Saying an LLP partner is an agent of the other partners.** He is an agent of the **LLP only** —
  that is the point of the structure.

## One-page revision summary

**Definition (s.4):** relation between persons who agreed to share the profits of a business carried
on by all or any of them acting for all · the clinching test is **mutual agency** · firm has
**unlimited liability**, is not a separate legal person.

**When the deed is silent:** profits and losses **equal** · **no** salary or commission · **no**
interest on capital · **no** interest on drawings · interest on a partner's **advance at 6% p.a.** ·
interest on capital, where allowed, is payable only out of profits · admission needs the consent of
**all** partners.

**Charges (P&amp;L A/c):** interest on partner's loan · rent to a partner · manager's salary.
**Appropriations (Appropriation A/c):** interest on capital · partner's salary and commission ·
transfer to reserve · share of profit.

**Formulas:**

<div class="formula">Divisible profit = Net profit + Interest on drawings − Interest on capital − Partners' salary/commission − Transfer to reserve</div>
<div class="formula">Interest on drawings (product method) = Total ₹-month products × Rate × 1/12</div>
<div class="formula">Average period = (Months left after the first drawing + Months left after the last drawing) ÷ 2</div>
<div class="formula">Goodwill (average profit) = Adjusted average profit × Years' purchase</div>
<div class="formula">Super profit = Adjusted average profit − (Capital employed × Normal rate of return)</div>
<div class="formula">Goodwill (capitalisation of super profit) = Super profit ÷ Normal rate of return</div>
<div class="formula">Goodwill (capitalisation of average profit) = (Average profit ÷ Normal rate) − Capital employed</div>
<div class="formula">Sacrificing ratio = Old ratio − New ratio · Gaining ratio = New ratio − Old ratio</div>
<div class="formula">Hidden goodwill (admission) = (New partner's capital × reciprocal of his share) − Total adjusted capital of all partners</div>
<div class="formula">Profit to date of death (turnover basis) = Sales to date × Last year's net profit rate × His share</div>

**Ratios at a glance:** premium for goodwill → **sacrificing ratio** · retiring/deceased partner's
goodwill → **gaining ratio** · revaluation profit and reserves on admission → **old partners, old
ratio** · on retirement and death → **all partners including the outgoing one, old ratio** ·
guarantee deficiency → agreed ratio, else the remaining partners' mutual ratio.

**Drawings shortcuts:** monthly at beginning 6.5 · monthly at end 5.5 · monthly in middle 6 ·
quarterly at beginning 7.5 · quarterly at end 4.5 · quarterly in middle 6 · amount only, no dates: 6.

**LLP:** body corporate under the LLP Act 2008 · separate legal entity · perpetual succession ·
liability limited to the agreed **contribution** · minimum two partners, no maximum · at least two
**designated partners**, both individuals, one resident in India · a partner is an agent of the LLP
but **not** of the other partners · registration compulsory · annual filings of a Statement of
Account and Solvency and an Annual Return.
