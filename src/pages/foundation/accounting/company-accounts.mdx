Sharma &amp; Sons has run a tile showroom in Indore for thirty years. To open a factory the family needs
₹8 crore — far more than the family can put in, and more than any bank will lend against a proprietor's
own name. So the family does what businesses have done for two centuries: it forms a **company**, splits
the money it needs into small pieces called **shares**, and sells those pieces to hundreds of strangers.
Each stranger risks only the price of his own pieces; nobody has to trust anybody personally.

This chapter is the bookkeeping of that idea. How does a company record money coming in from shareholders
(Units 1 and 2)? What happens when a shareholder does not pay (forfeiture and reissue)? And how does it
record money *borrowed* through **debentures** (Unit 3)? Almost all of it is arithmetic with fixed
patterns — learn six of them (issue at premium, pro-rata allotment, forfeiture, reissue, Table F interest,
loss on issue of debentures) and very little can surprise you.

## 1. What a company is, and the kinds you must know

A company is an **artificial legal person** created by registration under the Companies Act 2013. Six
features follow from that one sentence, and Unit 1 theory questions come from this list:

- **Separate legal entity** — the company owns its property and owes its debts; a shareholder owns shares,
  not the factory.
- **Limited liability** — a member can be asked for the **unpaid amount on his shares and nothing more**;
  on fully paid shares his liability is nil.
- **Perpetual succession** — members die or sell out, the company continues until wound up by law.
- **Transferability** — shares of a public company are freely transferable; a private company restricts
  transfer by its articles.
- **Separate property and capacity to sue** — it contracts, sues and is sued in its own name.
- **Common seal, now optional** — no longer compulsory; the company may act through authorised signatories.

**The kinds you must be able to tell apart:**

| Kind | The defining test |
|---|---|
| **Private company** | Articles restrict transfer of shares, limit members to 200 (joint holders counted as one; employee-members excluded) and prohibit any invitation to the public. Minimum 2 members, 2 directors. |
| **Public company** | Not a private company. Minimum 7 members, 3 directors; no upper limit on members; may invite the public to subscribe. |
| **One Person Company (OPC)** | A private company with a **single member**, who must be a natural person, and who must name a **nominee** to step in on his death or incapacity. |
| **Small company** | A private company below the prescribed paid-up capital and turnover limits (currently ₹4 crore and ₹40 crore). A public company, a holding or subsidiary company, a section 8 company and a company governed by a special Act can **never** be a small company, whatever their size. |
| **Listed company** | A company whose securities are listed on a recognised stock exchange. Listing is about the *securities*, not about size. |

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    "Small company" and "listed company" are traps in opposite directions. A small company is defined by
    <strong>numbers plus exclusions</strong> — memorise the exclusion list, which the examiner tests more
    often than the rupee limits. A listed company is defined by <strong>listing alone</strong>; the label
    follows the securities, not the size.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. The share capital ladder

Five words describe the same money at five stages of its journey; questions live or die on keeping them
straight.

1. **Authorised (nominal / registered) capital** — the ceiling stated in the memorandum. A *permission*,
   not money; it cannot be exceeded without altering the memorandum.
2. **Issued capital** — the part of it actually offered to the public.
3. **Subscribed capital** — the part of the issued capital applied for and allotted.
4. **Called-up capital** — the part of the subscribed capital so far **demanded** from shareholders.
5. **Paid-up capital** — the called-up capital **actually received**, i.e. called-up less calls in arrears.

Two more terms hang off the ladder. **Uncalled capital** = Subscribed − Called-up: money the company can
still ask for. **Reserve capital** is a portion of that *uncalled* capital which the company resolves, by
special resolution, to call up **only on winding up** — not to be confused with **capital reserve**, a
reserve created out of capital profits.

<div class="formula">Paid-up capital = Called-up capital − Calls in arrears (calls in advance are not added)</div>

Money received before a call is made is a **liability**, not paid-up capital, until the call is made.

## 3. Equity and preference shares under section 43

Section 43 says the share capital of a company limited by shares shall be of two kinds only: **equity share
capital** — either with voting rights, or with differential rights as to dividend, voting or otherwise as
prescribed — and **preference share capital**.

**Preference shares** carry two preferential rights: a preference in **dividend** (a fixed amount or rate)
and a preference in **repayment of capital** on winding up. That is the whole definition; the rest are
varieties:

- **Cumulative** — a dividend missed in a lean year is carried forward and must be paid before equity gets
  anything; **non-cumulative** — a year missed is lost. Preference shares are presumed cumulative unless
  the terms say otherwise.
- **Participating** — after equity receives a stated rate, these shares also share in surplus profits (and
  often surplus assets). **Non-participating** is the default.
- **Convertible / non-convertible** — convertible into equity shares on stated terms.
- **Redeemable** — repayable within the period the law allows. A company limited by shares **cannot issue
  irredeemable preference shares**; the mechanics of redemption are an Intermediate topic.

**Equity shares** take the residue: no fixed dividend, no preference, but the votes and the whole of
whatever profit is left after the preference dividend.

## 4. Shares versus debentures

A share buys **ownership**; a debenture buys the company's **promise to repay a loan**. Every difference
below follows from that one line.

| | Share | Debenture |
|---|---|---|
| Status of holder | Owner (member) | Creditor |
| Return | **Dividend** — an appropriation of profit, payable only if there is profit and only if declared | **Interest** — a **charge against profit**, payable whether or not the company earns profit |
| Voting | Equity shares carry voting rights | No voting rights at general meetings |
| Repayment | Capital is not normally returned during the company's life | Repayable on the due date (except perpetual debentures) |
| Security | Unsecured by nature | May be secured by a charge on assets |
| Issue at a discount | **Prohibited** (s.53), except sweat equity | **Permitted** — a debenture is a loan, not capital |
| On winding up | Paid last | Paid before all shareholders |

## 5. Issue of shares: application, allotment and calls

Money comes in instalments, and each instalment gets **two entries** — one when it becomes *due*,
one when it is *received*.

| Stage | Entry |
|---|---|
| Application money received | Bank A/c **Dr** … To Share Application A/c |
| Application money transferred on allotment | Share Application A/c **Dr** … To Share Capital A/c (and To Securities Premium A/c, if any premium is charged on application) |
| Allotment money becomes due | Share Allotment A/c **Dr** … To Share Capital A/c … To Securities Premium A/c |
| Allotment money received | Bank A/c **Dr** … To Share Allotment A/c |
| Call becomes due | Share First Call A/c **Dr** … To Share Capital A/c |
| Call received | Bank A/c **Dr** … To Share First Call A/c |

Three rules govern the instalments:

- **Minimum application money.** Section 39 requires the amount payable on application to be at least
  **5% of the nominal value** of the security (SEBI requires a much higher 25% of the issue price for a
  public issue).
- **Minimum subscription.** No allotment may be made unless the minimum subscription stated in the
  prospectus has been subscribed and that application money received; if it is not received within 30 days
  of the issue of the prospectus, the application money must be **refunded**.
- **Uniformity of calls (s.49).** Calls on shares of the same class must be made on a uniform basis. Table
  F adds that no call exceed 25% of nominal value, that calls be a month apart, and that 14 days' notice
  be given.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Share Application, Allotment and Call accounts are <strong>temporary personal accounts</strong>, opened
    to collect one instalment and closed the moment it is received. A balance left in Share Allotment A/c is
    no mystery — it is the <em>allotment money nobody paid</em>, and it is exactly what you credit when
    those shares are forfeited.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. Securities premium and the ban on issue at a discount

A share of ₹10 sold for ₹14 has a **face value** of ₹10 and a **premium** of ₹4. Only the ₹10 is share
capital; the ₹4 is credited to the **Securities Premium Account**, and section 52 lists the only purposes
it may be applied to:

- issuing **fully paid bonus shares** to members;
- writing off the **preliminary expenses** of the company;
- writing off the **expenses of, commission paid on, or discount allowed on, any issue of shares
  or debentures**;
- providing for the **premium payable on redemption** of redeemable preference shares or
  debentures;
- **buying back** its own shares or other securities under section 68.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Securities premium is <strong>never available for dividend</strong>. Any option that pays a dividend
    out of it, or uses it to write off a trading or revenue loss, is wrong — those are the two distractors
    the examiner plants.
  </p>
</Callout>

Going the other way is barred. **Section 53: a company shall not issue shares at a discount**, and a share
so issued is **void**. Two narrow doors exist: **sweat equity shares** under section 54 (to directors or
employees for know-how or value addition), and shares issued to creditors on conversion of debt under a
statutory resolution plan or debt-restructuring scheme. Debentures are unaffected — they may freely be
issued at a discount.

## 7. Oversubscription and pro-rata allotment

When 90,000 shares are applied for and only 60,000 are on offer, the company must dispose of the extra
applications. It has three tools, and a question usually mixes them: **reject** some applications outright
(their money is **refunded** in full); **allot in full** to some applicants; and **allot pro-rata** to the
rest, where the **excess application money is not refunded** but **adjusted against allotment** (and, if
the articles permit and it still exceeds allotment, against the calls).

<div class="formula">Pro-rata ratio = Shares allotted to the group ÷ Shares applied for by the group</div>
<div class="formula">Shares applied for by a member = Shares allotted to him × (Shares applied for by the group ÷ Shares allotted to the group)</div>
<div class="formula">Excess application money of a member = (Shares applied − Shares allotted) × Application money per share</div>
<div class="formula">Allotment money still due from a member = (Shares allotted × Allotment per share) − His excess application money</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The classic slip: computing the amount unpaid on allotment on the shares <strong>applied for</strong>
    instead of the shares <strong>allotted</strong>, or forgetting that his excess application money is
    already sitting against allotment. Both wrong figures will be among the four options.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. Calls in arrears and calls in advance

**Calls in arrears** — money called but not received. It is shown in the balance sheet as a **deduction
from called-up capital**. Under **Table F** the directors may charge interest on arrears at a rate **not
exceeding 10% per annum**, and may waive it wholly or in part.

**Calls in advance** — money received before the call is made; section 50 allows a company to accept it if
the articles authorise. Three consequences follow: it is a **liability** shown separately and **not** added
to paid-up capital until the call falls due; it carries **no voting rights and no dividend**; and under
**Table F** the company may pay interest on it at a rate **not exceeding 12% per annum**, payable **even if
there is no profit**, because it is interest on a debt.

<div class="formula">Interest = Amount × Rate × Months ÷ 12 — from the due date to the date of payment (arrears), or from the date of receipt to the date the call falls due (advance)</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Remember the pair as <strong>"10 in, 12 out"</strong>: the company <em>charges</em> 10% on money owed to
    it and <em>pays</em> 12% on money lent to it early. Both are Table F <strong>maximums</strong>, not
    fixed rates — a question that states its own rate overrides Table F, and a question silent on the rate
    is asking for the Table F ceiling.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Shares issued for consideration other than cash

Shares need not be paid for in money. **To vendors**, for a business or asset bought: record the assets and
liabilities taken over first — Sundry Assets A/c **Dr** … To Sundry Liabilities A/c … To Vendor A/c — then
settle the vendor, Vendor A/c **Dr** … To Share Capital A/c … To Securities Premium A/c. **To promoters**,
for services, know-how or formation costs: Goodwill A/c (or Incorporation Expenses A/c) **Dr** … To Share
Capital A/c.

<div class="formula">Number of shares to be issued = Purchase consideration ÷ Issue price per share (face value + premium)</div>

Dividing by the **face value** when a premium is stated is the standard trap: ₹1,00,000 ÷ 10 gives 10,000
shares, but at an issue price of ₹12.50 the right answer is 8,000 shares.

## 10. Forfeiture of shares

If a shareholder does not pay a call, the company may — after the notice its articles require — **forfeit**
his shares. Forfeiture cancels the shares and confiscates what he has already paid.

**Shares issued at par.** Debit Share Capital with the amount **called up** on those shares, credit the
instalments **not received**, and credit the balance (what he did pay) to **Share Forfeiture A/c**:

| Particulars | Dr | Cr |
|---|---|---|
| Share Capital A/c (shares × amount called up) | ✔ | |
| To Share Allotment / Calls A/c (amounts unpaid) | | ✔ |
| To Share Forfeiture A/c (amount actually received) | | ✔ |

**Shares issued at a premium — the one rule that decides the question.** If the **premium has already been
received**, it is **never cancelled**: leave the Securities Premium Account alone, because section 52
protects premium once received; only the capital instalments come back. If the **premium was called but not
received**, the **Securities Premium Account is debited** with that unreceived premium, alongside Share
Capital.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Debiting Securities Premium with the <em>whole</em> premium on the forfeited shares — including the part
    already banked — is the commonest error in this chapter. Ask one question only: <strong>was the premium
    received?</strong> Received premium stays; unreceived premium is reversed.
  </p>
</Callout>

The credit balance in **Share Forfeiture A/c** is not profit yet. Until those shares are reissued it is
shown in the balance sheet as an **addition to subscribed capital**.

## 11. Reissue of forfeited shares and capital reserve

Forfeited shares may be reissued at par, at a premium, or at a discount. The reissue entry credits Share
Capital with the amount **credited as paid up**, debits Bank with what the new buyer pays, and puts the
shortfall to **Share Forfeiture A/c**:

| Particulars | Dr | Cr |
|---|---|---|
| Bank A/c (reissue proceeds) | ✔ | |
| Share Forfeiture A/c (discount allowed on reissue) | ✔ | |
| To Share Capital A/c (amount credited as paid up) | | ✔ |

Two limits and one transfer. **The discount on reissue cannot exceed the amount forfeited on those very
shares** — the company may give away the confiscated money, never more. If the shares are reissued **at a
premium**, the excess is credited to Securities Premium; it is a fresh premium, not a recovery. And after
reissue, the amount forfeited on the **reissued** shares, less the discount allowed on them, is a **capital
profit** transferred to **Capital Reserve**:

<div class="formula">Capital reserve = Amount forfeited on the shares reissued − Discount allowed on their reissue</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    When only some of the forfeited shares are reissued, apportion the forfeited amount <strong>per
    share</strong> first. Only the reissued shares' portion reaches capital reserve; the rest stays in Share
    Forfeiture A/c and continues to be shown with subscribed capital.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 12. Debentures: kinds and terms of issue

A **debenture** is an instrument of a company evidencing a **debt**, whether or not it creates a charge on
the company's assets. Debentures carry **no voting rights**. They are classified four ways: **by security**
— secured (a charge on assets) or unsecured / naked; **by tenure** — redeemable on a date, or perpetual /
irredeemable; **by convertibility** — fully, partly or non-convertible into equity shares; and **by records
and priority** — registered (transferable through the register) or bearer (by delivery), first or second by
order of repayment.

Because a debenture is a loan, a company may issue it **at par, at a premium or at a discount**, and may
promise to redeem it **at par or at a premium**. That gives six combinations, and questions are built by
picking one:

| Issued | Redeemed | What the entry needs |
|---|---|---|
| At par | At par | Nothing extra |
| At discount | At par | Discount on Issue of Debentures (debit) |
| At premium | At par | Securities Premium (credit) |
| At par | At premium | Loss on Issue of Debentures (debit) + Premium on Redemption (credit) |
| At discount | At premium | Loss on Issue of Debentures = discount + redemption premium |
| At premium | At premium | Securities Premium credited **and** Loss on Issue debited with the redemption premium |

Discount on issue of debentures is a **capital loss**, not an expense of the year. It is written off over
the life of the debentures — most conveniently against the Securities Premium Account, if one exists, and
otherwise against the Statement of Profit and Loss.

## 13. Loss on issue when debentures are redeemable at a premium

When a debenture is issued below face value **and** promises to repay above face value, the company suffers
at both ends. Both losses are recognised **at the time of issue**, in one account:

<div class="formula">Loss on issue of debentures = Discount on issue + Premium payable on redemption</div>

The full entry, for debentures issued at a discount and redeemable at a premium:

| Particulars | Dr | Cr |
|---|---|---|
| Debenture Application &amp; Allotment A/c (money received) | ✔ | |
| Loss on Issue of Debentures A/c (discount + redemption premium) | ✔ | |
| To Debentures A/c (**face value**) | | ✔ |
| To Premium on Redemption of Debentures A/c | | ✔ |

Two points examiners test. **Debentures A/c is always credited with the face value**, never the issue
price — the whole purpose of the discount and premium accounts is to keep the liability at face value. And
**Premium on Redemption of Debentures A/c is a liability**, not an expense: the company will actually pay
it years later, so it sits under long-term liabilities until redemption.

## 14. Debentures as collateral security, and debenture interest

A lender may want more comfort than a promise, so the company issues debentures to it as **collateral
(secondary) security**: the lender holds them and returns them when the loan is repaid, and they become
live only on default. There are **two accepted presentations**:

- **Method 1 — no entry.** Nothing is recorded, because no liability has been created. The fact is
  disclosed as a **note** under the loan: "Bank loan ₹5,00,000, secured by the issue of ₹7,00,000
  debentures as collateral security."
- **Method 2 — entry passed.** Debenture Suspense A/c **Dr** … To Debentures A/c. The debentures appear at
  face value and **Debenture Suspense A/c is shown as a deduction from them**, so the net effect on the
  balance sheet total is nil.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Under method 2 the two accounts must be shown <strong>together and netted off</strong>. Showing
    Debenture Suspense as an asset (the older presentation) inflates both sides of the balance sheet by the
    collateral amount — that is the distractor to avoid.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Interest on debentures** is calculated on the **face (nominal) value** at the coupon rate, never on the
issue price, and it is a **charge against profit** — payable even in a loss year. Tax is deducted at source,
so the entry is: Debenture Interest A/c **Dr** … To Debentureholders A/c (net) … To TDS Payable A/c.

## Fully worked examples

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — issue at a premium, in instalments (easy)">
  <p>
    Sunrise Ceramics Ltd issues 50,000 equity shares of ₹10 each at a premium of ₹4 per share,
    payable: on application ₹5 (including ₹1 premium), on allotment ₹6 (including ₹3 premium), and
    on first and final call ₹3. The issue is fully subscribed and all money is received. Show the
    amounts at each stage and the two credit balances created.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — split each instalment into capital and premium</div>
    Application ₹5 = capital ₹4 + premium ₹1 · Allotment ₹6 = capital ₹3 + premium ₹3 · Call ₹3 = capital ₹3<br />
    Check: capital 4 + 3 + 3 = ₹10 (face value) ✔ · premium 1 + 3 = ₹4 ✔
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — money at each stage (50,000 shares)</div>
    On application: 50,000 × 5 = ₹2,50,000 → Share Capital ₹2,00,000 + Securities Premium ₹50,000<br />
    On allotment: 50,000 × 6 = ₹3,00,000 → Share Capital ₹1,50,000 + Securities Premium ₹1,50,000<br />
    On call: 50,000 × 3 = ₹1,50,000 → Share Capital ₹1,50,000<br />
    Total received = <strong>₹7,00,000</strong> = 50,000 × ₹14 ✔
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — the two balances</div>
    Share Capital A/c = 50,000 × ₹10 = <strong>₹5,00,000</strong> · Securities Premium A/c = 50,000 × ₹4 =
    <strong>₹2,00,000</strong> (usable only for the five section 52 purposes)
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — oversubscription and pro-rata allotment (moderate)">
  <p>
    Meghdoot Steels Ltd offers 60,000 equity shares of ₹10 each at a premium of ₹2, payable: on
    application ₹3, on allotment ₹5 (including the ₹2 premium), on first and final call ₹4.
    Applications are received for 90,000 shares and the directors allot pro-rata to all applicants,
    adjusting the excess application money against allotment. Mr Xavier, who applied for 1,200
    shares, fails to pay the allotment money. Find (a) the ratio, (b) the cash received on
    allotment from all shareholders, and (c) the amount unpaid by Mr Xavier.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — ratio, and where the application money goes</div>
    Pro-rata ratio = 60,000 allotted ÷ 90,000 applied = <strong>2 : 3</strong> — 2 shares for every 3 applied for<br />
    Received on application: 90,000 × 3 = ₹2,70,000<br />
    Retained as application money on 60,000 allotted shares: 60,000 × 3 = ₹1,80,000<br />
    Excess carried to allotment = 2,70,000 − 1,80,000 = <strong>₹90,000</strong> (nothing is
    refunded — no application was rejected)
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — cash due and received on allotment</div>
    Allotment due = 60,000 × 5 = ₹3,00,000<br />
    Less excess application money already held = ₹90,000<br />
    Net receivable on allotment = ₹2,10,000<br />
    Less Mr Xavier's unpaid amount (working note 3) = ₹2,800<br />
    Cash actually received on allotment = <strong>₹2,07,200</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Mr Xavier</div>
    Shares allotted = 1,200 × 2/3 = <strong>800 shares</strong><br />
    Excess application money = (1,200 − 800) × 3 = ₹1,200<br />
    Allotment due on 800 shares = 800 × 5 = ₹4,000<br />
    Amount unpaid = 4,000 − 1,200 = <strong>₹2,800</strong><br />
    (Computing on 1,200 shares, or forgetting the ₹1,200 already held, gives the two wrong answers
    the examiner offers.)
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — forfeiture at a premium and reissue at a discount (hard)">
  <p>
    Nandi Auto Ltd issued shares of ₹10 each at ₹13, payable: on application ₹4 (including ₹1
    premium), on allotment ₹5 (including ₹2 premium), on first and final call ₹4. Ravi, who held
    500 shares, paid the application money and nothing more; his shares were forfeited. Later, 300
    of them were reissued at ₹8 per share as fully paid. Pass the forfeiture and reissue entries
    and find the capital reserve.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — what Ravi paid, split</div>
    Received on application: 500 × ₹4 = ₹2,000, of which premium 500 × ₹1 = ₹500 and capital ₹1,500<br />
    Unpaid: allotment 500 × ₹5 = ₹2,500 (of which premium ₹1,000) and call 500 × ₹4 = ₹2,000
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — forfeiture entry</div>
    Share Capital A/c Dr ₹5,000 (500 × ₹10 called up)<br />
    Securities Premium A/c Dr ₹1,000 (premium called on allotment but <em>not received</em>)<br />
    &nbsp;&nbsp;To Share Allotment A/c ₹2,500<br />
    &nbsp;&nbsp;To Share First and Final Call A/c ₹2,000<br />
    &nbsp;&nbsp;To Share Forfeiture A/c <strong>₹1,500</strong><br />
    The ₹500 premium <em>already received</em> is untouched — it stays in Securities Premium A/c.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — reissue of 300 shares at ₹8, fully paid</div>
    Bank A/c Dr 300 × 8 = ₹2,400<br />
    Share Forfeiture A/c Dr 300 × 2 = ₹600 (discount on reissue)<br />
    &nbsp;&nbsp;To Share Capital A/c 300 × 10 = ₹3,000<br />
    Permitted maximum discount = forfeited amount on these 300 shares = ₹900, so ₹600 is within the
    limit ✔
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — capital reserve</div>
    Forfeited amount per share = 1,500 ÷ 500 = ₹3<br />
    On the 300 reissued shares = 300 × 3 = ₹900<br />
    Less discount allowed on reissue = ₹600<br />
    Capital reserve = <strong>₹300</strong><br />
    Share Forfeiture A/c still carries 200 × ₹3 = <strong>₹600</strong>, shown with subscribed
    capital until those shares are reissued.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — calls in arrears and calls in advance under Table F (moderate)">
  <p>
    Kaveri Textiles Ltd, which has adopted Table F, made a first call of ₹3 per share due on
    1 September 2025. Anand, holding 2,000 shares, paid it only on 1 January 2026. Bhavna, holding
    3,000 shares, paid the final call of ₹2 per share on 1 October 2025, although that call was made
    due only on 1 April 2026. Compute the interest in each case and state how the two amounts appear
    in the balance sheet on 31 December 2025.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Anand: calls in arrears at 10% p.a.</div>
    Amount in arrears = 2,000 × ₹3 = ₹6,000<br />
    Period 1 Sep 2025 to 1 Jan 2026 = 4 months<br />
    Interest = 6,000 × 10/100 × 4/12 = <strong>₹200</strong>, receivable by the company
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Bhavna: calls in advance at 12% p.a.</div>
    Amount in advance = 3,000 × ₹2 = ₹6,000<br />
    Period 1 Oct 2025 to 1 Apr 2026 = 6 months<br />
    Interest = 6,000 × 12/100 × 6/12 = <strong>₹360</strong>, payable by the company even if it
    earns no profit
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — presentation on 31 December 2025</div>
    Anand's ₹6,000 is deducted from called-up capital to give paid-up capital.<br />
    Bhavna's ₹6,000 is a <strong>liability</strong>, shown separately as "Calls in advance"; it is
    not added to paid-up capital, carries no vote and earns no dividend.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — debentures at a discount redeemable at a premium, plus collateral (hard)">
  <p>
    Godavari Cements Ltd issues 4,000 12% debentures of ₹100 each at ₹96, redeemable after five
    years at ₹105. On the same day it takes a bank loan of ₹5,00,000 and issues ₹7,00,000 of
    debentures to the bank as collateral security. Show the issue entry, the loss on issue, the
    annual interest, and both collateral presentations.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — the two losses</div>
    Discount on issue = (100 − 96) × 4,000 = ₹16,000<br />
    Premium payable on redemption = (105 − 100) × 4,000 = ₹20,000<br />
    Loss on issue of debentures = 16,000 + 20,000 = <strong>₹36,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — the issue entries</div>
    Bank A/c Dr ₹3,84,000 (4,000 × ₹96) … To Debenture Application &amp; Allotment A/c ₹3,84,000<br />
    Debenture Application &amp; Allotment A/c Dr ₹3,84,000<br />
    Loss on Issue of Debentures A/c Dr ₹36,000<br />
    &nbsp;&nbsp;To 12% Debentures A/c ₹4,00,000 (<strong>face value</strong>)<br />
    &nbsp;&nbsp;To Premium on Redemption of Debentures A/c ₹20,000<br />
    Both sides total ₹4,20,000 ✔
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — annual interest</div>
    Interest = 12% × ₹4,00,000 (face value, not ₹3,84,000) = <strong>₹48,000 per year</strong>, a
    charge against profit, paid net of tax deducted at source.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — the collateral debentures</div>
    <strong>Method 1:</strong> no entry. Under the bank loan of ₹5,00,000, a note records that
    ₹7,00,000 debentures have been issued as collateral security.<br />
    <strong>Method 2:</strong> Debenture Suspense A/c Dr ₹7,00,000 … To 12% Debentures A/c
    ₹7,00,000. In the balance sheet the ₹7,00,000 debentures are shown with ₹7,00,000 Debenture
    Suspense <em>deducted from them</em> — net effect nil. Either way, the liability the company
    actually owes the bank is <strong>₹5,00,000</strong>.
  </div>
</Callout>

## 15. What this chapter deliberately leaves out

Three topics look as though they belong here and do not, at Foundation level: **redemption of preference
shares**, **redemption of debentures** (including sinking-fund and Debenture Redemption Reserve mechanics),
and **bonus and rights issues**. All three are Intermediate topics. You are still expected to know, from
Units 1 and 3, that redeemable preference shares and redeemable debentures exist and that securities
premium may be used to provide for the premium payable on redemption.

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

- **Cancelling premium already received on forfeiture.** Debit Securities Premium only with premium
  **called but not received**; money already banked is protected by section 52.
- **Working allotment arrears on shares applied for.** The amount due is on the shares **allotted**, and
  his excess application money is already adjusted against it.
- **Refunding money in a pro-rata question.** If no application was rejected, nothing is refunded.
- **Discount on reissue exceeding the forfeited amount** — the cap is the amount forfeited **on those very
  shares**, computed per share when only some are reissued.
- **Sending the whole forfeiture balance to capital reserve.** Only the reissued shares' portion, net of the
  reissue discount, is transferred.
- **Adding calls in advance to paid-up capital.** It is a liability until the call is made: interest yes,
  dividend and vote no.
- **Swapping the two Table F rates.** 10% on arrears (company receives), 12% on advance (company pays), both
  ceilings rather than fixed rates.
- **Crediting Debentures A/c with the issue price**, or **calculating debenture interest on the issue
  price**. Both use the **face value**.
- **Showing Debenture Suspense as an asset.** Net it against the debentures issued as collateral.
- **Treating discount on issue of shares as merely irregular.** Section 53 prohibits it and the share so
  issued is **void**; only sweat equity and a statutory debt conversion escape.

## One-page revision summary

**The ladder:** Authorised → Issued → Subscribed → Called-up → Paid-up · Uncalled = Subscribed − Called-up ·
Reserve capital is callable only on winding up (not the same as capital reserve).

**Statute:** s.43 — only two kinds of share capital, equity and preference · s.52 — securities premium usable
only for bonus shares, preliminary expenses, issue expenses/commission/discount, premium on redemption and
buy-back; **never for dividend** · s.53 — no issue of shares at a discount, such a share is void, exceptions
sweat equity (s.54) and statutory debt conversion · s.49 — calls on a uniform basis · s.50 — calls in advance
if the articles permit.

**Table F:** interest on calls in arrears **up to 10% p.a.**; on calls in advance **up to 12% p.a.**; calls
not exceeding 25% of nominal value, a month apart, on 14 days' notice.

**Formulas:**

<div class="formula">Paid-up capital = Called-up capital − Calls in arrears</div>
<div class="formula">Shares applied by a member = Shares allotted × (Total applied ÷ Total allotted)</div>
<div class="formula">Allotment due from a member = (Shares allotted × Allotment per share) − Excess application money</div>
<div class="formula">Amount forfeited per share = Total received on those shares, excluding premium received</div>
<div class="formula">Maximum discount on reissue = Amount forfeited on the shares being reissued</div>
<div class="formula">Capital reserve = Forfeited amount on shares reissued − Discount allowed on their reissue</div>
<div class="formula">Loss on issue of debentures = Discount on issue + Premium payable on redemption</div>
<div class="formula">Debenture interest = Coupon rate × Face value (never the issue price)</div>

**Rules that answer theory MCQs:** forfeiture cancels shares and confiscates money paid · premium
received is never reversed, premium unreceived is · share forfeiture balance is added to subscribed
capital until reissue · calls in advance is a liability, no vote, no dividend, interest payable
even in a loss year · dividend is an appropriation, debenture interest is a charge · debentures may
be issued at a discount, shares may not · Debentures A/c always at face value · Debenture Suspense
is deducted from debentures, never shown as an asset · redemption and bonus issues belong to
Intermediate.
