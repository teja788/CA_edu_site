Meera runs a small saree shop in Surat. One evening she asks three simple questions: how much
did I really earn this month, how much does the shop owe, and how much of it is truly mine?
Accounting exists to answer exactly these questions — in one shared language, by one set of
rules, so that anyone reading the answer can trust it. This chapter is that rulebook: what
accounting is, the ground rules it follows, and the one equation every rule protects.

In the exam this chapter is a scoring machine: every attempt carries a cluster of quick MCQs
from here — name the concept, spot the AS 1 assumption, classify capital vs revenue, or apply
the accounting equation. The traps live in near-twin terms (accrual vs realisation, provision
vs contingent liability, policy vs estimate), so read the micro-examples closely.

## 1. What accounting really is: the process

Accounting is the process of **identifying** money-measurable transactions and events,
**recording** them, **classifying** and **summarising** them, and then **analysing,
interpreting and communicating** the results to the people who need them. The classic
definition — "the art of recording, classifying and summarising … and interpreting the results
thereof" — came from the AICPA (the American institute), and the SM opens with the same idea.

The eight steps, in order:

1. **Identifying** — pick out only transactions and events of a *financial character*.
   Meera buying sarees is in; her hiring a very charming salesman is not (no money value yet).
2. **Measuring** — express what you identified in rupees, the common unit.
3. **Recording** — write it chronologically in the books of original entry (journal and its
   subsidiary books).
4. **Classifying** — group entries of one kind in one place: the **ledger** (all cash entries
   in the Cash account, all of Ram's dealings in Ram's account).
5. **Summarising** — condense the ledger into statements users can digest: the trial balance,
   then the Profit &amp; Loss Account and the Balance Sheet.
6. **Analysing** — establish relationships between the figures (profit to sales, current
   assets to current liabilities) so the mass of numbers starts talking.
7. **Interpreting** — explain what those relationships *mean*: can the shop pay its dues?
   Is it earning enough on what the owner put in?
8. **Communicating** — send the analysed, interpreted results to users through accounting
   reports.

| Stage | Where it happens |
|---|---|
| Recording | Journal and subsidiary books |
| Classifying | Ledger |
| Summarising | Trial balance → P&amp;L Account and Balance Sheet |

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Three favourite one-markers: the <strong>first</strong> step of the process is
    <strong>identifying</strong> (not recording); classification happens in the
    <strong>ledger</strong>; and <em>analysing</em> establishes the relationships while
    <em>interpreting</em> explains their significance — two different steps.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. Book-keeping, accounting and the three subfields

**Book-keeping** is the routine, mechanical part — identifying, measuring, recording and
classifying. **Accounting** starts where book-keeping ends: it summarises, analyses,
interprets and communicates. **Accountancy** is the whole body of systematic knowledge the
other two rest on.

| Point | Book-keeping | Accounting |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Recording and classifying | Also summarising, analysing, interpreting, communicating |
| Stage | Primary (base) stage | Secondary — begins where book-keeping ends |
| Objective | Maintain systematic records | Show the results and state of the business to users |
| Nature of work | Routine, clerical | Analytical; needs judgement and skill |

Accounting itself has grown subfields:

- **Financial accounting** — records transactions and prepares the P&amp;L and Balance Sheet
  for a period; largely historical; the statements travel to *outside* users.
- **Cost accounting** — ascertains the cost of products, services and activities, and helps
  control that cost.
- **Management accounting** — reworks financial and cost data into whatever form managers
  need for planning and decisions; internal, future-facing, and bound by no prescribed format.
- The SM also names **social responsibility accounting** (reporting the social costs and
  benefits of the business) and **human resource accounting** (valuing people as a resource).

## 3. Who reads the accounts: users of financial statements

Users split into **internal** — the management (and owners who run the business day to day) —
and **external**, who must rely on the published statements:

- **Investors** (present and potential) — is my money safe, and what is it earning?
- **Lenders and banks** — will interest and principal arrive on time?
- **Suppliers and trade creditors** — can the firm clear my bill in the short run?
- **Employees and unions** — is the employer stable and profitable enough for salaries, bonus
  and retirement dues?
- **Customers** — will this supplier still exist when I need service and spares?
- **Government and its agencies** — taxes, subsidies, statistics, regulation.
- **The public** — jobs, local suppliers, the firm's footprint in the community.

## 4. Concepts, principles and conventions: the ground rules

The SM itself notes that the words *concept*, *principle* and *convention* are used loosely
and interchangeably — no marks turn on the label; the marks turn on knowing each rule and
spotting it in a one-line story.

- **Entity concept** — the business is separate from its owner. Capital the owner brings in is
  a *liability of the business toward the owner*, and money the owner takes for personal use is
  **drawings**, never a business expense. Meera's home rent paid from the shop's cash box is
  drawings.
- **Money measurement** — record only what money can measure. A ₹75,000 sewing machine enters
  the books; a brilliant, loyal production manager does not. Limitation: the rupee's own value
  changes over time, and the books ignore that.
- **Going concern** — assume the business will continue for the foreseeable future. This is why
  machines sit at cost less depreciation instead of scrap-sale value, and why costs can be
  spread over future years at all.
- **Cost concept** — an asset is recorded at its acquisition cost, and that cost is the base
  for all later accounting. Land bought for ₹10,00,000 stays at ₹10,00,000 in the books even
  when the market says ₹45,00,000.
- **Dual aspect** — every transaction has two effects: what comes in and where it came from.
  This keeps <strong>Assets = Liabilities + Capital</strong> permanently true and is the seed
  of double-entry book-keeping (§7).
- **Accrual** — recognise revenue when *earned* and expense when *incurred*, not when cash
  moves. March salaries paid in April still belong to March's year, as outstanding salary.
- **Periodicity** — chop the indefinite life of the business into equal accounting periods
  (normally a year) so performance can be measured, compared and taxed on time.
- **Matching** — set the period's revenues against the expenses incurred *to earn them*. Goods
  still unsold at year end are carried forward as closing stock so their cost meets next year's
  sale, not this year's.
- **Realisation** — record revenue only when it is actually realised: goods delivered, legal
  right to receive established. A confirmed order in March for May delivery produces **no**
  March revenue. Profits are never anticipated.
- **Consistency** — use the same accounting methods period after period, so figures compare.
  A change is allowed when a statute or an accounting standard requires it, or when the new
  method presents the picture more appropriately — with disclosure.
- **Conservatism (prudence)** — anticipate no profit, but provide for all probable losses.
  Inventory at lower of cost and net realisable value, and the provision for doubtful debts,
  are its two classic faces.
- **Materiality** — items significant enough to influence a user's decisions deserve separate,
  careful treatment; trifles do not. A ₹450 stapler that will serve for years is simply
  expensed. Materiality is *relative* — ₹50,000 is material to a kirana shop, a rounding error
  to Infosys.
- **Full disclosure** — the statements should reveal all material information honestly, on the
  face or in the notes, so users are informed, not misled.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Three abuse-of-the-rule traps. <strong>Conservatism</strong> does not permit deliberate
    understatement or secret reserves — over-providing distorts the true and fair view.
    <strong>Consistency</strong> does not mean a method can never change — it means changes
    need good reason and disclosure. <strong>Materiality</strong> is judged by size <em>and
    nature</em> relative to the business — there is no all-India cutoff figure.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. The three fundamental accounting assumptions (AS 1)

Out of the whole crowd in §4, AS 1 elevates exactly **three** to the rank of *fundamental
accounting assumptions*:

1. **Going concern**
2. **Consistency**
3. **Accrual**

The disclosure rule is beautifully asymmetric: if these three are followed, **no specific
disclosure is required** — readers assume them by default. If any one is **not** followed,
**that fact must be disclosed**. A firm keeping its books on cash basis must therefore say so,
because accrual — a fundamental assumption — has been dropped.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The MCQ trap is always a swap: options slip <em>prudence</em>, <em>matching</em> or
    <em>materiality</em> into the list of three. Hold the line: going concern, consistency,
    accrual — nothing else. Prudence, substance over form and materiality belong to a
    different AS 1 list: the considerations for <strong>selecting accounting policies</strong> (§10).
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. Qualitative characteristics of financial statements

What makes financial statements *useful*? Four principal qualities:

- **Understandability** — presentable to a user with reasonable knowledge of business, who is
  willing to study the statements with diligence. Complexity is no excuse to omit relevant
  matter.
- **Relevance** — the information helps users evaluate past, present or future events, or
  confirms or corrects their earlier evaluations. Materiality works as its threshold: omitting
  or misstating an item is a relevance problem when it could sway decisions.
- **Reliability** — free from material error and bias; faithfully represents what it claims to
  represent; substance over legal form; neutral; prudent; complete.
- **Comparability** — usable against the same firm's earlier years and against other firms.
  Consistency of methods and full disclosure of accounting policies are what make this
  possible.

Together they aim the statements at one target the SM keeps repeating: a **true and fair
view** of the financial position and performance.

## 7. The accounting equation

The dual aspect concept compressed into five symbols:

<div class="formula">Assets = Liabilities + Capital</div>
<div class="formula">Capital = Assets − Liabilities</div>

Everything the business *owns* was financed by someone — outsiders (liabilities) or the owner
(capital). Every transaction touches at least two of these, and the equation never stops
balancing. Watch five standard moves:

| Transaction | Effect on the equation |
|---|---|
| Owner brings in ₹3,00,000 cash | Assets ↑ 3,00,000 · Capital ↑ 3,00,000 |
| Buy furniture ₹80,000 for cash | One asset ↑, another ↓ — totals unchanged |
| Buy goods ₹1,20,000 on credit | Assets ↑ 1,20,000 · Liabilities ↑ 1,20,000 |
| Pay a creditor ₹25,000 | Assets ↓ 25,000 · Liabilities ↓ 25,000 |
| Pay rent ₹20,000 | Assets ↓ 20,000 · Capital ↓ 20,000 (expense) |

Profits and losses land on capital: incomes push it up, expenses and losses pull it down,
drawings pull it down at *cost* to the business. Across a year:

<div class="formula">Closing capital = Opening capital + Additional capital + Profit − Drawings</div>

Turn it around and the equation finds missing figures: profit = closing capital − opening
capital − additional capital + drawings. Half the numericals in this chapter are this one
formula wearing different clothes.

## 8. Capital vs revenue: expenditure and receipts

Two questions decide **expenditure**:

1. Does the benefit last beyond the current accounting period?
2. Does it *acquire* a fixed asset, bring one to working condition, or *increase* its earning
   capacity — or does it merely *run or maintain* what already exists?

Acquire, install, extend, improve → **capital expenditure** (balance sheet, then depreciated).
Run, maintain, restore → **revenue expenditure** (this year's P&amp;L). The classic battery:

| Item | Classification | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Price of a new machine | Capital | Acquires a fixed asset |
| Carriage and installation on that machine | Capital | Cost of bringing it to working condition |
| Overhaul of a second-hand machine before first use | Capital | Needed to make it usable at all |
| Repairs after months of use (even if heavy) | Revenue | Maintains existing capacity |
| Annual whitewashing of the shop | Revenue | Routine upkeep |
| Adding a new room to the workshop | Capital | Extends capacity |
| Legal fees to purchase land | Capital | Part of acquisition cost |
| Wages and salaries for the year | Revenue | Cost of running the period |

**Receipts** follow the same instinct. **Capital receipts** — capital brought in, loans
raised, sale proceeds of a fixed asset — are not income; they hit the balance sheet.
**Revenue receipts** — sale of goods, fees, commission, interest earned — belong to the
P&amp;L. Insurance claims split the same way: a claim for a destroyed *machine* is a capital
receipt; a claim for destroyed *stock* is a revenue receipt, set against the trading loss.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    "Heavy" is not a classification test. A ₹5,00,000 repair that only restores an old
    machine's normal working is still <strong>revenue</strong> expenditure; a ₹30,000 attachment
    that permanently raises output is <strong>capital</strong>. Judge the <em>nature of the
    benefit</em>, never the size of the bill.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Deferred revenue expenditure, provisions and contingent liabilities

**Deferred revenue expenditure (DRE)** is revenue in nature but so large and so
forward-reaching that charging it to one year would distort results — the SM's example is a
massive one-time launch campaign. Treatment per the SM: write it off over the years of
expected benefit; the unwritten-off balance sits on the asset side as a *fictitious asset*
(no real value behind it).

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Know the boundary lines. DRE is not a <em>prepaid expense</em> — prepaid rent buys a
    definite future service; DRE has no such enforceable claim. And under the modern AS 26
    view most such spending is expensed at once — Foundation follows the SM's classic
    treatment, so answer exam questions the SM's way.
  </p>
</Callout>

A **provision** is a liability that exists today but whose *amount* must be estimated —
outflow probable, estimate reliable — so it is **recognised in the accounts** (provision for
doubtful debts, for warranty claims). A **contingent liability** is only a *possible*
obligation — its existence hangs on an uncertain future event, or the outflow is not probable
or not reliably measurable — so it is **not recorded**, only **disclosed in the notes**.
Bills discounted with the bank that may bounce, and a pending court case the firm may lose,
are the two exam staples.

## 10. Accounting policies, estimates and measurement bases

**Accounting policies** are the specific accounting principles a firm adopts *and* the methods
it uses to apply them: which depreciation method, which inventory valuation formula, how to
translate foreign amounts. Different firms may validly choose differently — which is exactly
why AS 1 makes them disclose the choice in one place.

A policy may be **changed** only when a statute requires it, an accounting standard requires
it, or the change would give a **more appropriate presentation** of the statements.

AS 1 names three **considerations that govern selection** of policies, all serving the true
and fair view:

- **Prudence** — do not anticipate profits; provide for all known liabilities and probable
  losses.
- **Substance over form** — account for the economic reality, not just the legal costume. An
  asset bought on hire purchase appears in the buyer's books though legal title has not yet
  passed.
- **Materiality** — disclose whatever could influence a user's decisions.

**Accounting estimates** are approximations made because exact measurement is impossible:
useful life of a machine, the doubtful-debts percentage, expected warranty claims. Choosing
the WDV *method* is a policy; deciding the machine will last *eight years* is an estimate.
Estimates are revised when facts change — prospectively, and a revision is not an error.

Money amounts themselves can be measured on four **bases**:

| Basis | The asset is carried at… |
|---|---|
| Historical cost | What was paid when it was acquired (the everyday base) |
| Current cost | What acquiring it *today* would cost |
| Realisable (settlement) value | What selling it in an orderly way would fetch today |
| Present value | Today's discounted value of its future net cash inflows |

## Fully worked examples

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — building the accounting equation (easy)">
  <p>
    On 1 April 2026 Ananya starts Sunrise Stationery with cash ₹3,00,000. She then (i) buys a
    display counter for ₹80,000 cash, (ii) buys notebooks on credit from Bharat Paper Mart for
    ₹1,20,000, and (iii) sells notebooks costing ₹40,000 for ₹55,000 cash. Show the equation.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Assets, one by one</div>
    Cash = 3,00,000 − 80,000 + 55,000 = 2,75,000<br />
    Furniture (counter) = 80,000 · Stock = 1,20,000 − 40,000 = 80,000<br />
    Total assets = <strong>₹4,35,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — The other side</div>
    Liabilities = creditor (Bharat Paper Mart) = 1,20,000<br />
    Capital = 3,00,000 + profit (55,000 − 40,000) = 3,15,000<br />
    Check: 1,20,000 + 3,15,000 = <strong>₹4,35,000</strong> — the equation balances.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — capital vs revenue expenditure battery (moderate)">
  <p>
    Malhotra Furniture Works spends: new lathe ₹2,50,000; carriage on the lathe ₹10,000;
    installation ₹15,000; annual servicing contract ₹12,000; repainting the factory gate
    ₹5,000; extending the workshop by one room ₹90,000. Split capital vs revenue.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Capital expenditure</div>
    Lathe 2,50,000 + carriage 10,000 + installation 15,000 (bringing to working condition)
    + workshop extension 90,000 (adds capacity) = <strong>₹3,65,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Revenue expenditure</div>
    Servicing contract 12,000 + gate repainting 5,000 (maintenance, benefit within the year)
    = <strong>₹17,000</strong> to the P&amp;L.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — capital vs revenue receipts (moderate)">
  <p>
    Desai Traders receives during the year: sale of goods ₹6,50,000; sale of an old delivery
    scooter ₹25,000; bank loan ₹2,00,000; insurance claim for stock lost in a fire ₹45,000;
    fresh capital from the owner ₹1,00,000. Split the receipts.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Revenue receipts</div>
    Sale of goods 6,50,000 + insurance claim for <em>stock</em> 45,000 (replaces a trading
    loss) = <strong>₹6,95,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Capital receipts</div>
    Scooter sale 25,000 (fixed asset) + bank loan 2,00,000 (liability created) + fresh capital
    1,00,000 (owner's contribution) = <strong>₹3,25,000</strong> — none of this is income.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — finding profit through the equation (moderate-hard)">
  <p>
    On 31 March 2026 Nikhil's business shows: machinery ₹2,40,000; stock ₹1,10,000; debtors
    ₹70,000; cash ₹30,000; creditors ₹95,000; bank loan ₹60,000. Opening capital was
    ₹2,45,000, drawings during the year ₹36,000, no fresh capital. Find the year's profit.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Closing capital from the equation</div>
    Assets = 2,40,000 + 1,10,000 + 70,000 + 30,000 = 4,50,000<br />
    Liabilities = 95,000 + 60,000 = 1,55,000<br />
    Closing capital = 4,50,000 − 1,55,000 = <strong>₹2,95,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Work back to profit</div>
    Profit = Closing capital − Opening capital − Fresh capital + Drawings<br />
    = 2,95,000 − 2,45,000 − 0 + 36,000 = <strong>₹86,000</strong>
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

- **The list of three.** AS 1's fundamental assumptions are going concern, consistency,
  accrual. Options smuggling in prudence, matching or materiality are wrong — those three are
  the *policy-selection considerations*.
- **Disclosure direction reversed.** Followed → nothing to disclose; not followed → disclose.
  Options demanding disclosure of assumptions that *are* followed have it backwards.
- **Accrual vs realisation.** Accrual times *expenses and incomes* (recognise when incurred or
  earned); realisation stops *revenue* being recorded before the sale happens. An advance
  order recorded as a sale violates realisation.
- **Drawings of goods at selling price.** Withdrawals are recorded at **cost** — the owner
  cannot earn a profit by selling to himself (realisation again).
- **"Heavy" repairs capitalised.** Size never decides. In-use repairs that restore capacity
  are revenue regardless of amount; pre-use overhaul of a second-hand asset is capital.
- **Sale of a fixed asset in the P&amp;L.** The sale *proceeds* are a capital receipt. Only a
  profit or loss versus book value ever touches the P&amp;L — never the whole receipt.
- **Provision vs contingent liability.** Probable + estimable = provision, recognised in the
  accounts. Merely possible = contingent liability, disclosed in notes only. Bills discounted
  live in the notes.
- **Policy vs estimate.** The WDV *method* is a policy; the *eight-year life* is an estimate.
  Swapping these two words is the whole trick in several MCQs.
- **Equation slips.** A credit purchase raises assets *and* liabilities (totals grow); paying
  a creditor shrinks both; expenses reduce *capital*, not liabilities.

## One-page revision summary

**Process:** identify → measure → record (journal) → classify (ledger) → summarise (trial
balance, final accounts) → analyse → interpret → communicate. Book-keeping = the first four,
routine; accounting begins where book-keeping ends. Subfields: financial (external,
historical), cost (ascertain and control cost), management (internal decisions, no format).

**Concepts in one line each:** entity — owner ≠ business, capital is an internal liability ·
money measurement — record only the measurable · going concern — continuity assumed · cost —
record at acquisition cost · dual aspect — two effects, always · accrual — earned/incurred,
not received/paid · periodicity — yearly slices · matching — expenses meet their revenues ·
realisation — no revenue before sale · consistency — same methods, or disclose the change ·
prudence — no anticipated profits, all probable losses · materiality — significance is
relative · full disclosure — all material facts, face or notes.

**AS 1:** fundamental assumptions = going concern, consistency, accrual; disclose only
non-observance. Policies = principles + methods of applying them; change on statute, standard
or better presentation; selection weighs prudence, substance over form, materiality.
Qualitative characteristics: understandability, relevance, reliability, comparability.
Measurement bases: historical cost, current cost, realisable value, present value.

**Formulas:**

<div class="formula">Assets = Liabilities + Capital</div>
<div class="formula">Capital = Assets − Liabilities</div>
<div class="formula">Closing capital = Opening capital + Additional capital + Profit − Drawings</div>

**Capital vs revenue in one breath:** acquire, install, extend, improve → capital; run,
maintain, restore → revenue; fixed-asset sale proceeds, loans, owner's money → capital
receipts; sales, fees, interest, stock-loss insurance claims → revenue receipts; DRE = big
revenue spend spread over its benefit years (SM treatment); contingent liabilities are
disclosed, never recorded.
