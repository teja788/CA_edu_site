A quality inspector at a garment unit in Tiruppur pulls ten shirts at random from every batch of a
thousand. Long experience says about 4% of the unit's output has a stitching fault. The inspector
cannot examine all thousand shirts, yet she still wants to say, before the batch ships, how likely
it is that her sample of ten throws up no faulty shirt at all, or exactly one, or three or more. She
is not measuring anything new. She is reading a **pattern** that the number of faulty shirts in a
sample of ten must follow, given the 4% rate — a pattern fixed in advance by the mathematics, not by
this particular batch.

That pattern is a **theoretical distribution**. It is a probability model written down from a few
assumptions, and it tells you the chance of each possible value before you collect a single
observation. This chapter builds the three that Paper 3 tests: the **binomial**, for counting
successes in a fixed number of trials; the **Poisson**, for counting rare events over a stretch of
time or space; and the **normal**, the bell-shaped curve that measurements of size, weight and marks
tend to follow. Learn to recognise which situation feeds which distribution, and most of the work is
substituting into one formula.

Paper 3 is fully objective, so you will not derive these results. You will be handed the parameters
and asked for one probability, one mean, one variance or one area, quickly and exactly. This chapter
takes each distribution in turn, shows how every number is computed from the parameters in the stem,
and keeps to standard values so that no probability table is ever needed to answer a question.

## 1. What a theoretical distribution is

A **random variable** is a rule that attaches a number to each outcome of an experiment — the number
of heads in five tosses, the number of accidents on a road in a day, the weight of a bag of cement.
A **probability distribution** lists every value the variable can take together with the probability
of each. When those probabilities come from actual data collected by counting, the distribution is
**observed** (or empirical). When they come instead from a mathematical law derived from assumptions,
the distribution is **theoretical** (or expected).

- A **discrete** distribution attaches probability to separate, countable values — 0, 1, 2, 3 and so
  on. The binomial and the Poisson are discrete: you cannot have 2.5 defective shirts.
- A **continuous** distribution spreads probability over an interval of the number line, so the
  variable can take any value in a range. The normal is continuous: a weight can be 49.7 kg or
  49.73 kg or anything between.

Two rules hold for every probability distribution, and an option that breaks either is wrong on
sight. Each probability lies between 0 and 1, and the probabilities of all the values add up to
exactly 1.

<div class="formula">Each P(X = value) is at least 0 and at most 1   ·   the sum of P over all values = 1</div>

A theoretical distribution is described by a small number of **parameters** — constants that fix the
whole shape. The binomial needs two (n and p), the Poisson needs one (m), and the normal needs two
(the mean and the standard deviation). Give the parameters and the entire distribution is
determined, which is exactly why an objective question can hand you the parameters and expect a
single, definite number back.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The purpose of fitting a theoretical distribution to real data is to <strong>replace a bulky
    table of observed frequencies with a couple of parameters</strong>. Once you know the data behave
    like a binomial with a certain p, or a Poisson with a certain m, you can state the probability of
    any value — including values you never actually observed — without collecting more data. That is
    the whole practical payoff, and it is why examiners like these three distributions.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. The binomial distribution

The binomial answers one question: in **n** independent trials, each of which either succeeds or
fails, how many successes occur? "Success" is just the label for the outcome you are counting; it
need not be good news, and counting faulty shirts is a perfectly ordinary use. Four assumptions
define the model, and every one of them must hold:

1. There is a **fixed number of trials**, n, decided before the experiment.
2. Each trial has exactly **two outcomes**, success or failure (a Bernoulli trial).
3. The probability of success, **p**, is **constant** from trial to trial. The probability of failure
   is q = 1 − p, so p + q = 1.
4. The trials are **independent** — the result of one does not change the chances of another.

Sampling ten shirts from a thousand, tossing a coin twenty times, firing eight shots at a target:
each fits the four assumptions. Drawing cards without replacement does **not**, because p changes as
the pack shrinks — that breaks assumption 3.

If X is the number of successes in n trials, its probability is given by the **binomial law**:

<div class="formula">P(X = r) = nCr p^r q^(n−r),   for r = 0, 1, 2, …, n,   where q = 1 − p</div>

Here nCr is the number of ways to choose which r of the n trials are the successes, nCr = n! ÷ [r!
(n − r)!]. The p^r is the chance those r trials all succeed, and q^(n−r) is the chance the other
n − r all fail. Multiply and add over the ways, and you have the formula. The n + 1 probabilities for
r = 0 up to r = n are the successive terms of the expansion of (q + p)^n, which is why they must add
to (q + p)^n = 1.

**Worked illustration.** A fair coin is tossed 6 times. What is the probability of exactly 4 heads?
Here n = 6, p = 1/2, q = 1/2, r = 4.

- 6C4 = 15
- P(X = 4) = 15 × (1/2)^4 × (1/2)^2 = 15 × (1/2)^6 = 15 ÷ 64 = **0.2344**

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The exponents must add to n. In P(X = r) = nCr p^r q^(n−r), the power of p is r and the power of q
    is n − r, and r + (n − r) = n every time. Writing q^r or p^(n−r) swaps the two and is the
    commonest slip in the whole chapter. A quick check: the two exponents together must always equal
    the number of trials.
  </p>
</Callout>

Two shapes of question recur. "**At least one**" is answered through its complement, because working
out P(X = 1) + P(X = 2) + … is slow, while the opposite event is a single term:

<div class="formula">P(at least one success) = 1 − P(X = 0) = 1 − q^n</div>

And "**at most one**" or "**fewer than two**" is the sum of just the first one or two terms,
P(X = 0) + P(X = 1). Read the boundary words carefully — "more than 2" means r = 3, 4, …, while "at
least 2" includes r = 2 itself.

## 3. Mean, variance and mode of the binomial

You rarely need the whole distribution. The three summary numbers are asked far more often than any
single probability, and each is a one-line formula.

<div class="formula">Mean = n p   ·   Variance = n p q   ·   Standard deviation = √(n p q)</div>

The mean is where the distribution balances — in n trials with success chance p, you expect np
successes, which is plain common sense. The variance measures the spread. Because q is a proper
fraction (it is below 1), npq is always **smaller than** np, so a binomial variance is **always less
than its mean**. That single fact separates the binomial from the Poisson, where the two are equal,
and it is a favourite one-line examiner test.

**Worked illustration.** For n = 20 and p = 0.3: mean = 20 × 0.3 = **6**, variance = 20 × 0.3 × 0.7 =
**4.2**, standard deviation = √4.2 = **2.0494**. The variance 4.2 is below the mean 6, as it must be.

Given the mean and the variance you can recover the parameters, which is a standard reverse question.
Divide the variance by the mean to get q, since npq ÷ np = q; then p = 1 − q and n = mean ÷ p.

<div class="formula">q = variance ÷ mean   ·   p = 1 − q   ·   n = mean ÷ p</div>

For a binomial with mean 4 and variance 2.4: q = 2.4 ÷ 4 = 0.6, so p = 0.4 and n = 4 ÷ 0.4 = **10**.

The **mode** — the most probable number of successes — is found without listing every probability.
Compute (n + 1)p. If it is not a whole number, the mode is the whole-number part (the largest integer
below it). If (n + 1)p is itself a whole number, the distribution has two modes, at (n + 1)p and at
(n + 1)p − 1.

<div class="formula">Mode = the integer part of (n + 1)p, when (n + 1)p is not a whole number</div>

For n = 10 and p = 0.4, (n + 1)p = 11 × 0.4 = 4.4, so the mode is **4**. For n = 9 and p = 0.5,
(n + 1)p = 5 exactly, so there are two modes, 5 and 4.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Three facts about the shape are worth carrying into the hall. The binomial is
    <strong>symmetric</strong> only when p = 0.5; it leans to the right when p is below 0.5 and to the
    left when p is above 0.5. Its mean np and its mode are usually close but need not be equal. And it
    is a <strong>discrete</strong> distribution with exactly n + 1 possible values, from 0 successes
    up to n. If an option offers a fractional number of successes, it is wrong.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Fitting a binomial distribution

To **fit** a binomial to observed data means to find the binomial that best matches it, then compute
the frequencies that model predicts. The recipe is short. The number of trials n is the largest count
possible (five coins give n = 5). The probability p is estimated from the mean of the data, because
mean = np, so p = (mean) ÷ n. The **expected frequency** of exactly r successes, out of N repetitions
of the whole experiment, is N times the binomial probability:

<div class="formula">Expected frequency of r successes = N × nCr p^r q^(n−r)</div>

**Worked illustration.** Five fair coins are tossed 320 times. How many of the 320 tosses should show
exactly 2 heads? Here n = 5, p = 1/2, N = 320, r = 2.

- 5C2 = 10, so P(X = 2) = 10 × (1/2)^5 = 10 ÷ 32 = 0.3125
- Expected frequency = 320 × 0.3125 = **100**

The full set of expected frequencies for r = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 comes out as 10, 50, 100, 100, 50, 10,
which add back to 320 — a useful check, since the expected frequencies must total N.

## 5. The Poisson distribution

The Poisson distribution counts how many times a **rare event** happens over a fixed stretch of time,
length, area or volume: misprints on a page, accidents at a junction in a week, calls arriving at an
exchange in a minute, faulty items in a large consignment. There is no natural upper limit — a page
could in principle carry any number of misprints — so the count runs 0, 1, 2, 3, … without stopping.

The distribution arises as a **limiting case of the binomial**, when the number of trials n grows
very large while the success probability p becomes very small, in such a way that the mean np settles
at a finite figure written **m** (some texts call it λ). In that limit the binomial formula collapses
to a much simpler one that needs only the single parameter m:

<div class="formula">P(X = x) = e^(−m) m^x ÷ x!,   for x = 0, 1, 2, 3, …,   where m = the mean</div>

Here e = 2.71828 is the base of natural logarithms, m is the average number of occurrences in the
interval, and x! is the factorial of x (with 0! = 1). The probabilities again add to 1, because the
series e^(−m)[1 + m + m²/2! + m³/3! + …] is e^(−m) × e^m = 1.

**Worked illustration.** The number of misprints on a page follows a Poisson distribution with mean
m = 2. The probability of exactly 3 misprints on a page is:

- e^(−2) = 0.1353
- P(X = 3) = e^(−2) × 2^3 ÷ 3! = 0.1353 × 8 ÷ 6 = 0.1353 × 1.3333 = **0.1804**

The two shapes from the binomial reappear. P(X = 0) is simply e^(−m), because m^0 = 1 and 0! = 1, so
"**no occurrence**" is one exponential. And "**at least one**" is its complement:

<div class="formula">P(X = 0) = e^(−m)   ·   P(at least one) = 1 − e^(−m)</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The Poisson mean m is the average count <strong>for the interval the question asks about</strong>.
    If accidents happen at 2 per week and the question asks about a fortnight, m becomes 4, not 2. If
    misprints run at 3 per page and you want the chance for two pages, m is 6. Scale the mean to the
    interval <em>before</em> putting it in the formula, or every probability comes out wrong.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. Mean, variance and the additive property of Poisson

The Poisson has a property that no other distribution in this chapter shares, and it is tested almost
every attempt: its **mean and its variance are equal**, both equal to m.

<div class="formula">Mean = m   ·   Variance = m   ·   Standard deviation = √m</div>

So a Poisson variable with mean 9 has variance 9 and standard deviation 3. Turned around, if a
distribution is known to be Poisson and you are told its variance, you have been told its mean; if
mean and variance are quoted as unequal, the distribution cannot be Poisson. Contrast the binomial,
whose variance npq is strictly below its mean np — the two never match unless there is nothing to
count.

The **additive (reproductive) property** says that if two independent Poisson variables are added,
the total is again Poisson, with a mean equal to the sum of the two means.

<div class="formula">If X is Poisson(m₁) and Y is Poisson(m₂), independent, then X + Y is Poisson(m₁ + m₂)</div>

If line A of a switchboard takes calls at a Poisson rate of 3 per minute and line B at 2 per minute,
independently, the two lines together take calls at a Poisson rate of 5 per minute, and the chance of
no call at all in a minute across both is e^(−5) = 0.0067.

The **mode** of a Poisson follows the same rule as the binomial's, using m in place of (n + 1)p. When
m is not a whole number the mode is its integer part; when m is a whole number the distribution has
two modes, m and m − 1. A convenient way to build the whole distribution is the **recurrence
relation**, each probability from the one before it:

<div class="formula">P(X = x + 1) = P(X = x) × m ÷ (x + 1)</div>

## 7. Fitting a Poisson distribution

Fitting a Poisson is even shorter than fitting a binomial, because there is only one parameter to
estimate. The mean m is read straight off the data as the average count, m = (sum of f × x) ÷ (sum of
f), where f is the observed frequency of each value x. The **expected frequency** of exactly x
occurrences, over N repetitions, is N times the Poisson probability:

<div class="formula">Expected frequency of x occurrences = N × e^(−m) m^x ÷ x!</div>

**Worked illustration.** A Poisson distribution with mean m = 1 is fitted to N = 200 observations. The
expected frequency of exactly 1 occurrence is:

- e^(−1) = 0.3679
- P(X = 1) = e^(−1) × 1^1 ÷ 1! = 0.3679
- Expected frequency = 200 × 0.3679 = **73.58**, that is about 74

In practice you build the whole table with the recurrence of section 6: start from N × e^(−m) for
x = 0, then multiply each figure by m ÷ (x + 1) to reach the next. It saves recomputing the
exponential every line.

## 8. The normal distribution

The **normal distribution** is the continuous, bell-shaped curve that heights, weights, examination
marks and measurement errors follow closely. Unlike the two discrete distributions, it spreads
probability over the whole number line, and it is described by two parameters: the mean μ (the Greek
letter mu) and the standard deviation σ (sigma). Its curve is given by a formula you never have to
evaluate by hand at Foundation level, but should recognise:

<div class="formula">f(x) = 1 ÷ (σ √(2π)) × e^(−(x − μ)² ÷ (2σ²))</div>

What matters is the list of properties, because every examinable fact is one of them:

- The curve is **bell-shaped and perfectly symmetric** about the mean. The left half is the mirror
  image of the right.
- Because of that symmetry, the **mean, the median and the mode all coincide** at the centre, μ. This
  is the property most often asked as a one-liner.
- The **total area under the curve is 1**, and by symmetry exactly **half, 0.5, lies on each side** of
  the mean. Area under the curve is read as probability.
- The curve is **asymptotic** to the horizontal axis: it approaches the axis on both sides but never
  touches it, so in theory the variable can take any value, however extreme.
- It has its **points of inflexion at μ − σ and μ + σ**, one standard deviation on each side of the
  mean, where the curve changes from bending down to bending up.
- The two tails are identical, so the **skewness is zero** and the coefficient of **kurtosis is 3**
  (the curve is mesokurtic).

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A few fixed relationships for the normal curve are worth memorising, because they turn up as
    direct one-mark questions. The <strong>mean deviation is about 0.7979σ</strong> (roughly four
    fifths of σ), the <strong>quartile deviation is 0.6745σ</strong> (roughly two thirds), so the
    quartiles sit at μ ± 0.6745σ. The ratio quartile deviation : mean deviation : standard deviation
    is close to <strong>10 : 12 : 15</strong>. You are far more likely to be asked to recognise these
    than to use them in arithmetic.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. The standard normal variable and z-scores

Every normal distribution, whatever its mean and spread, can be rescaled to one fixed reference curve
— the **standard normal distribution**, which has mean 0 and standard deviation 1. The rescaling
subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation, and the result is the **standard normal
variable**, written Z:

<div class="formula">z = (x − μ) ÷ σ</div>

The z-score says how many standard deviations a value x lies above the mean (z positive) or below it
(z negative). A value equal to the mean has z = 0; a value one standard deviation above the mean has
z = 1; a value half a standard deviation below has z = −0.5. Because the shift-and-scale does not
distort areas, the probability that x falls in any range equals the probability that Z falls in the
matching range of z-scores — and that single reference curve serves every normal distribution ever
set.

**Worked illustration.** Marks are normally distributed with mean μ = 50 and standard deviation
σ = 10. A mark of 68 corresponds to:

- z = (68 − 50) ÷ 10 = 18 ÷ 10 = **1.8**

So 68 lies 1.8 standard deviations above the mean. Running the formula backwards, a z-score can be
turned into a raw value with x = μ + zσ. With μ = 100 and σ = 15, the value two standard deviations
above the mean is x = 100 + 2 × 15 = **130**.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Divide by the standard deviation, never by the variance. In z = (x − μ) ÷ σ the denominator is σ,
    so if a question gives you the variance you must take its square root first. A variance of 64
    means σ = 8, and it is 8 that goes under the line. Using 64 shrinks every z-score eightfold and
    lands you on the wrong option.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. Areas under the normal curve

Since area under the normal curve is probability, the examinable skill is turning a range of x-values
into a z-range and reading off the area. The full table of z-areas is not needed at Foundation level,
because questions are built around a handful of standard z-values whose areas are worth knowing by
heart. The key figures, each the area from the mean (z = 0) out to the stated z, are:

| z | Area from 0 to z | Read as |
|---|---|---|
| 1.00 | 0.3413 | one standard deviation |
| 1.96 | 0.4750 | the 95% marker |
| 2.00 | 0.4772 | two standard deviations |
| 2.58 | 0.4950 | the 99% marker |
| 3.00 | 0.4987 | three standard deviations |

From these, any one-sided or two-sided area follows by adding or subtracting, remembering that the
whole area is 1 and each half is 0.5:

- **P(Z greater than 1)** = 0.5 − 0.3413 = **0.1587** (the area beyond one standard deviation in one
  tail).
- **P(Z less than 2)** = 0.5 + 0.4772 = **0.9772**.
- **P(−1 less than Z less than 2)** = 0.3413 + 0.4772 = **0.8185** (areas on opposite sides of the
  mean are added).

Doubling the one-side figures gives the **empirical rule**, the most quoted fact about the normal
curve: about **68.27%** of the values lie within one standard deviation of the mean, about **95.45%**
within two, and about **99.73%** within three.

<div class="formula">μ ± 1σ covers ≈ 68.27%   ·   μ ± 2σ covers ≈ 95.45%   ·   μ ± 3σ covers ≈ 99.73%</div>

Two further markers matter because they set the central 95% and 99% exactly. The **central 95%** of a
normal distribution lies between μ − 1.96σ and μ + 1.96σ, leaving 2.5% in each tail. The **central
99%** lies between μ − 2.58σ and μ + 2.58σ, leaving 0.5% in each tail.

<div class="formula">Central 95%: μ ± 1.96σ   ·   Central 99%: μ ± 2.58σ</div>

**Worked illustration.** Weights are normal with mean 500 g and standard deviation 100 g. The middle
95% of the packets weigh between 500 − 1.96 × 100 = **304 g** and 500 + 1.96 × 100 = **696 g**. To
count how many of 10,000 packets lie within one standard deviation of the mean, multiply the area by
the number: 10,000 × 0.6826 = **6,826 packets**, taking the within-one-σ area as 2 × 0.3413.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Keep the two-sided and one-sided readings apart. "Within one standard deviation" is a
    <strong>two-sided</strong> band and covers 2 × 0.3413 = 0.6826. "More than one standard deviation
    above the mean" is a <strong>one-sided</strong> tail and covers 0.5 − 0.3413 = 0.1587. The same
    0.3413 feeds both, but the first doubles it around the mean while the second subtracts it from a
    half. Decide which picture the question draws before you compute.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 11. Choosing the right distribution

Half the marks in this chapter go to picking the correct model, so fix the signals now.

- Use the **binomial** when there is a **fixed number of independent trials**, each a success or a
  failure with a **constant** probability, and you count the successes: heads in 10 tosses, defectives
  in a sample of 20, correct answers in a 15-question guess. Both n and p are known, and n is small
  enough to state.
- Use the **Poisson** when you count how often a **rare event** happens over an interval of time or
  space with **no fixed upper limit** and only the **average rate** given: accidents per week,
  customers per minute, flaws per metre of cloth. There is a mean but no n and no p, or n is huge and
  p tiny.
- Use the **normal** when the variable is a **continuous measurement** — length, weight, marks, time —
  that clusters symmetrically around an average, and the question talks of a **mean and a standard
  deviation** and asks for a proportion or a percentage in some range.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The three are linked, not separate. The <strong>Poisson is the limiting form of the binomial</strong>
    when n is large and p is small (a working rule of thumb: n at least 20 and p at most 0.05), with
    m = np. And the <strong>normal is the limiting form of the binomial</strong> when n is large and p
    is not too close to 0 or 1. So a binomial with a big n can be approximated by a Poisson or a
    normal, which is why the same physical situation can sometimes be handled by more than one model.
  </p>
</Callout>

<div class="formula">Binomial (n large, p small) → Poisson, m = np   ·   Binomial (n large, p moderate) → Normal, μ = np, σ = √(npq)</div>

## 12. Answering under negative marking

Paper 3 is entirely objective. A correct answer earns 1 mark, a wrong answer costs 0.25 mark, and a
blank scores zero. That arithmetic should shape how you treat a distribution question you cannot
finish.

Guessing blindly among four options has an expected value of (1 ÷ 4)(+1) + (3 ÷ 4)(−0.25) =
**+0.0625** marks — just barely positive, and nearly worthless. The value of a guess comes almost
entirely from the options you can eliminate first, and this chapter hands you cheap eliminations.
A binomial variance quoted **above** its mean is impossible. A Poisson whose mean and variance
**differ** is impossible. A probability **above 1**, a **negative** probability, or a **fractional
number of successes** is impossible. A normal area **above 1** or a claim that **more than half** the
curve lies beyond the mean is impossible. Ruling out even two options lifts the expected value of a
guess to **+0.375**.

The practical rule: eliminate what the properties forbid, commit to the surviving option, and move on.
Leave a question blank only when nothing can be ruled out and the minute is better spent elsewhere.
There is no penalty for a blank, so never leave a question you have narrowed to two.

## Fully worked examples

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — a single binomial probability (easy)">
  <p>
    A fair coin is tossed 5 times. Find the probability of getting exactly 3 heads.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Identify the parameters</div>
    A toss succeeds (heads) with probability p = 1/2, fails with q = 1/2, and there are n = 5
    independent trials. We want r = 3.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Apply the binomial law</div>
    5C3 = 10<br />
    P(X = 3) = 10 × (1/2)^3 × (1/2)^2 = 10 × (1/2)^5 = 10 ÷ 32 = <strong>0.3125</strong><br />
    The exponents 3 and 2 add to 5, the number of trials — the built-in check.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — mean, variance and standard deviation of a binomial (easy)">
  <p>
    A machine produces items of which 20% are defective. A sample of 100 items is drawn. Find the
    mean, the variance and the standard deviation of the number of defectives.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Set up n, p and q</div>
    n = 100, p = 0.20 (defective is the "success" we count), q = 0.80.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Substitute</div>
    Mean = n p = 100 × 0.20 = <strong>20</strong><br />
    Variance = n p q = 100 × 0.20 × 0.80 = <strong>16</strong><br />
    Standard deviation = √16 = <strong>4</strong><br />
    The variance 16 is below the mean 20, as a binomial variance always must be.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — at least one, through the complement (moderate)">
  <p>
    The probability that a component fails a test is 0.1. Five components are tested independently.
    Find the probability that at least one of them fails.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Recognise the complement</div>
    "At least one failure" is the opposite of "no failures at all". Computing P(X = 0) is one term;
    computing P(X = 1) + … + P(X = 5) is five. Use the complement.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Compute</div>
    n = 5, p = 0.1, q = 0.9<br />
    P(X = 0) = q^n = (0.9)^5 = 0.59049<br />
    P(at least one) = 1 − 0.59049 = <strong>0.40951</strong>
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — fitting a binomial and finding expected frequencies (moderate)">
  <p>
    Five coins are tossed together 320 times. Assuming the coins are fair, find the expected number of
    tosses showing 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 heads.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Parameters of the fitted binomial</div>
    n = 5, p = 1/2, q = 1/2, N = 320. Every probability is 5Cr ÷ 32.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Probabilities from the binomial coefficients</div>
    5Cr for r = 0…5 is 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1, over a denominator of 32.<br />
    Probabilities: 1/32, 5/32, 10/32, 10/32, 5/32, 1/32.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Multiply by N = 320</div>
    Expected frequencies = 320 × probability:<br />
    r = 0: 10 · r = 1: 50 · r = 2: 100 · r = 3: 100 · r = 4: 50 · r = 5: 10<br />
    They add to <strong>320</strong>, which confirms the fit is complete.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — a single Poisson probability (easy)">
  <p>
    The number of accidents at a road junction in a week follows a Poisson distribution with mean 2.
    Find the probability of exactly 3 accidents in a week. Take e^(−2) = 0.1353.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Parameters</div>
    m = 2 accidents per week; x = 3; the interval asked about is one week, so m needs no scaling.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Apply the Poisson law</div>
    P(X = 3) = e^(−2) × 2^3 ÷ 3! = 0.1353 × 8 ÷ 6<br />
    = 0.1353 × 1.3333 = <strong>0.1804</strong>
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 6 — the additive property and 'at least one' (moderate)">
  <p>
    A switchboard receives calls on two independent lines. Line A averages 3 calls a minute and line B
    averages 2 calls a minute, each Poisson. Find (a) the probability of no call at all in a given
    minute and (b) the probability of at least one call. Take e^(−5) = 0.006738.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Add the means</div>
    By the additive property, the two lines together are Poisson with mean m = 3 + 2 = 5 calls a
    minute.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (a) No call</div>
    P(X = 0) = e^(−5) = <strong>0.006738</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (b) At least one call</div>
    P(at least one) = 1 − e^(−5) = 1 − 0.006738 = <strong>0.993262</strong><br />
    Almost certain, which fits an exchange taking five calls a minute on average.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 7 — fitting a Poisson with the recurrence relation (moderate)">
  <p>
    Over 200 days the total number of accidents on a stretch of road was 100. Fit a Poisson
    distribution and find the expected number of days with 0, 1 and 2 accidents. Take e^(−0.5) =
    0.6065.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Estimate the mean</div>
    m = total accidents ÷ number of days = 100 ÷ 200 = <strong>0.5</strong> accidents per day.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Start the table at x = 0</div>
    Expected days with 0 accidents = 200 × e^(−0.5) = 200 × 0.6065 = <strong>121.30</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Step up with the recurrence P(x+1) = P(x) × m ÷ (x+1)</div>
    Days with 1 accident = 121.30 × 0.5 ÷ 1 = <strong>60.65</strong><br />
    Days with 2 accidents = 60.65 × 0.5 ÷ 2 = <strong>15.16</strong><br />
    Each figure is the one above it times m ÷ (x + 1), so the exponential is computed only once.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 8 — z-scores and the area between two values (moderate)">
  <p>
    The weights of bags of cement are normal with mean 50 kg and standard deviation 5 kg. Find the
    percentage of bags weighing between 45 kg and 60 kg. Take the area from 0 to 1 as 0.3413 and from
    0 to 2 as 0.4772.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Convert both limits to z-scores</div>
    z for 45 = (45 − 50) ÷ 5 = −1<br />
    z for 60 = (60 − 50) ÷ 5 = +2
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Add areas on opposite sides of the mean</div>
    The range runs from one standard deviation below the mean to two above, so the areas add:<br />
    Area = 0.3413 + 0.4772 = 0.8185
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Express as a percentage</div>
    0.8185 × 100 = <strong>81.85%</strong> of the bags weigh between 45 kg and 60 kg.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 9 — the empirical rule and the central 95% (moderate-hard)">
  <p>
    The marks of 1,000 students are normal with mean 60 and standard deviation 15. (a) How many
    students score between 30 and 90? (b) Between which two marks do the middle 95% of students lie?
    Take the within-two-σ area as 0.9545 and z = 1.96 for the central 95%.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) Recognise μ ± 2σ</div>
    30 = 60 − 2 × 15 and 90 = 60 + 2 × 15, so the range is exactly the mean ± two standard deviations.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Multiply the area by the number of students</div>
    Number = 1,000 × 0.9545 = <strong>954.5</strong>, so about <strong>955 students</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (b) The central 95% uses z = 1.96</div>
    Lower = 60 − 1.96 × 15 = 60 − 29.4 = <strong>30.6</strong><br />
    Upper = 60 + 1.96 × 15 = 60 + 29.4 = <strong>89.4</strong><br />
    The middle 95% of students score between about 30.6 and 89.4 marks.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 10 — the Poisson as a limit of the binomial (hard)">
  <p>
    In a large consignment, 0.2% of articles are defective. A sample of 1,000 articles is drawn. Using
    the Poisson approximation, find the probability that the sample contains exactly 3 defective
    articles. Take e^(−2) = 0.1353.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Why Poisson fits here</div>
    This is a binomial with n = 1,000 and p = 0.002 — n very large and p very small. The Poisson is
    the right approximation, with m = n p.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Find the mean</div>
    m = n p = 1,000 × 0.002 = <strong>2</strong> defective articles on average.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Apply the Poisson law</div>
    P(X = 3) = e^(−2) × 2^3 ÷ 3! = 0.1353 × 8 ÷ 6 = <strong>0.1804</strong><br />
    Computing the exact binomial 1000C3 (0.002)^3 (0.998)^997 gives almost the same figure, which is
    what "limiting case" means in practice — the hard binomial is replaced by an easy Poisson.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes and examiner traps

- **Swapping the exponents in the binomial.** In nCr p^r q^(n−r) the power of p is the number of
  successes r and the power of q is n − r. The two powers must add to n; if they do not, they have
  been swapped.
- **Leaving a probability as a percentage or above 1.** Every P(X = r) is a number between 0 and 1.
  An answer bigger than 1, or negative, is a signal of a wrong formula, not a valid option.
- **Forgetting the complement for "at least one".** P(at least one) = 1 − P(none) = 1 − q^n for the
  binomial and 1 − e^(−m) for the Poisson. Summing the long way wastes time and invites slips.
- **Quoting a binomial variance above its mean.** npq is always below np because q is below 1. A
  variance larger than the mean cannot be binomial.
- **Treating the Poisson mean and variance as different.** For a Poisson both equal m. If a question
  says the mean is one figure and the variance another, the distribution is not Poisson.
- **Not scaling the Poisson mean to the interval.** A rate of 2 per week becomes m = 4 for a
  fortnight and m = 6 for three weeks. Match m to the interval the question asks about.
- **Dividing by the variance instead of the standard deviation in a z-score.** z = (x − μ) ÷ σ. If
  the variance is given, take its square root first.
- **Confusing a two-sided band with a one-sided tail.** "Within one σ" covers 2 × 0.3413 = 0.6826;
  "more than one σ above the mean" covers 0.5 − 0.3413 = 0.1587. The same 0.3413, two different
  pictures.
- **Forgetting that half the normal curve lies each side of the mean.** The total area is 1 and the
  mean, median and mode all sit at the centre, so exactly 0.5 lies above the mean and 0.5 below.
- **Mixing up the empirical percentages.** μ ± 1σ ≈ 68.27%, μ ± 2σ ≈ 95.45%, μ ± 3σ ≈ 99.73%, while
  the central 95% and 99% use z = 1.96 and z = 2.58. Keep the σ-multiples and the 95/99 markers
  apart.
- **Choosing the wrong distribution.** A fixed n with a known p is binomial; a rare event with only a
  mean rate is Poisson; a continuous measurement with a mean and a standard deviation is normal.

## One-page revision summary

**Binomial** (parameters n and p, with q = 1 − p; discrete, values 0 to n)

<div class="formula">P(X = r) = nCr p^r q^(n−r) · Mean = n p · Variance = n p q (always below the mean) · SD = √(n p q)</div>
<div class="formula">P(at least one) = 1 − q^n · Mode = integer part of (n + 1)p · Recover parameters: q = variance ÷ mean, p = 1 − q, n = mean ÷ p</div>
<div class="formula">Fitting: expected frequency of r = N × nCr p^r q^(n−r), with p = mean ÷ n</div>

**Poisson** (single parameter m; discrete, values 0, 1, 2, … with no upper limit)

<div class="formula">P(X = x) = e^(−m) m^x ÷ x! · Mean = Variance = m · SD = √m · P(X = 0) = e^(−m) · P(at least one) = 1 − e^(−m)</div>
<div class="formula">Additive: Poisson(m₁) + Poisson(m₂) = Poisson(m₁ + m₂) · Recurrence: P(x + 1) = P(x) × m ÷ (x + 1) · Limit of the binomial when n large, p small, m = n p</div>

**Normal** (parameters μ and σ; continuous, symmetric bell)

<div class="formula">Mean = median = mode = μ · Total area = 1, with 0.5 each side · Points of inflexion at μ ± σ · Skewness 0, kurtosis 3</div>
<div class="formula">Standard normal variable z = (x − μ) ÷ σ (mean 0, SD 1) · Raw value from z: x = μ + z σ</div>
<div class="formula">Area 0 to 1 = 0.3413 · 0 to 1.96 = 0.4750 · 0 to 2 = 0.4772 · 0 to 2.58 = 0.4950 · 0 to 3 = 0.4987</div>
<div class="formula">Empirical rule: μ ± 1σ ≈ 68.27% · μ ± 2σ ≈ 95.45% · μ ± 3σ ≈ 99.73% · Central 95% = μ ± 1.96σ · Central 99% = μ ± 2.58σ</div>

**Choosing the model.** Fixed independent trials with constant p, count successes → **binomial**.
Rare events over an interval, only a mean rate → **Poisson**. Continuous measurement clustering
around a mean → **normal**.

**Conventions used on this page.** e = 2.71828, with e^(−1) = 0.3679, e^(−2) = 0.1353, e^(−3) =
0.0498. Normal areas use the standard z-values in the table above; where a question needs an area it
is either one of these or is stated in the stem, so no external table is required. Probabilities are
rounded to four decimal places and percentages to two.
