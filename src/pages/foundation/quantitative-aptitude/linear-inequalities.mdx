A small furniture unit at Hubballi makes two things: stacking chairs and study tables. The carpentry
shed can give 120 hours a week and the polishing bay 80 hours. Nobody in the workshop is asking
"what is the exact number of chairs?" — the honest question is **how many combinations of chairs and
tables can this week actually deliver?** Not one answer. A whole region of answers, with an edge
running around it.

That is the difference between an equation and an inequality, and it is the whole of this chapter.
An equation pins a variable to a point. An inequality fences it into a range. Real business runs on
fences: budgets that must not be exceeded, minimum orders that must be met, machine hours that run
out, godowns that fill up. In the Foundation paper this chapter is worth a steady handful of marks,
and almost all of them go to three skills — flipping the sign correctly when you divide by a
negative number, shading the right side of a boundary line, and reading the corner points of a
feasible region off the right pair of constraints.

## 1. What an inequality is

An **equation** says two expressions are equal. An **inequality** says one is bigger or smaller than
the other. Compare:

| Statement | Kind | What satisfies it |
|---|---|---|
| `2x + 6 = 20` | Linear equation | Exactly one value, `x = 7` |
| `2x + 6 < 20` | Linear inequality | Every value below 7 — infinitely many |
| `2x + 6 ≤ 20` | Linear inequality | Every value up to and including 7 |

An inequality is called **linear** when every variable in it appears to the first power only, with no
products of variables, no squares, no variable inside a root or a denominator. So `3x − 4y ≥ 12` is
linear; `x² ≤ 9`, `xy ≤ 20` and `3/x < 5` are not.

The set of all values that make the inequality true is its **solution set**. For an equation the
solution set is usually a single number. For a linear inequality in one variable it is an interval
of the number line; for a linear inequality in two variables it is half of the plane. That is the
one idea to carry through every section that follows:

<div class="formula">Equation → a point · Inequality in one variable → an interval · Inequality in two variables → a half-plane</div>

An inequality is called **strict** when it uses `<` or `>` and the boundary value is excluded, and
**non-strict** (or "weak") when it uses `≤` or `≥` and the boundary value is included. That one word
in the question — "at most", "not more than", "less than" — decides whether the endpoint belongs to
your answer, and examiners test it deliberately.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A single inequality is neither true nor false on its own; it becomes true or false only once you
    put a number into it. <code>2x + 6 &lt; 20</code> is true at <code>x = 3</code> and false at
    <code>x = 9</code>. "Solving" it means finding every <em>x</em> that makes it true, not finding
    the one that makes it true.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. The four symbols, strict and non-strict

| Symbol | Read as | Boundary included? | English phrases that produce it |
|---|---|---|---|
| `<` | is less than | No | less than · below · under · fewer than · short of |
| `>` | is greater than | No | more than · above · exceeds · over |
| `≤` | is less than or equal to | Yes | at most · not more than · maximum of · up to · cannot exceed · available |
| `≥` | is greater than or equal to | Yes | at least · not less than · minimum of · no fewer than · required |

Two conventions worth fixing now, because every later section depends on them.

**Direction is a property of the statement, not of the numbers.** `5 > 3` and `3 < 5` say the same
thing. You may read an inequality from either end, so `−4 ≤ x` and `x ≥ −4` are the same solution
set. Students who mechanically keep the variable on the left lose marks when the question presents
it on the right.

**A double inequality is two inequalities in one line.** `−3 ≤ x < 7` means `x ≥ −3` **and** `x < 7`
at the same time. Note the mixed ends: −3 belongs to the set, 7 does not. Both halves must hold, so
a double inequality is always an "and", never an "or".

## 3. The rules of operation on inequalities

You may do to an inequality almost everything you do to an equation. Four rules cover the syllabus.

**Rule 1 — Add or subtract the same quantity on both sides. The sign does not change.**
If `a < b`, then `a + c < b + c` and `a − c < b − c`, whatever the sign of *c*. Adding 5 to both
pans of a balance that was already tilted leaves it tilted the same way. This is why transposing a
term across the sign is safe.

**Rule 2 — Multiply or divide both sides by the same POSITIVE quantity. The sign does not change.**
If `a < b` and `c > 0`, then `ac < bc` and `a/c < b/c`. Scaling both sides up by a positive factor
preserves which one is larger.

**Rule 3 — Multiply or divide both sides by the same NEGATIVE quantity. The sign REVERSES.**
If `a < b` and `c < 0`, then `ac > bc` and `a/c > b/c`. This is the rule the whole chapter turns on,
and section 4 is devoted to it.

**Rule 4 — Two inequalities of the same direction may be added.**
If `a < b` and `c < d`, then `a + c < b + d`. Useful when a question gives ranges for two quantities
and asks for the range of their total. Note carefully what is **not** allowed: you may not
**subtract** two inequalities of the same direction, and you may not multiply two inequalities
together unless every quantity involved is known to be positive.

| Operation on `−2x + 5 ≤ 11` | Result | Sign |
|---|---|---|
| Subtract 5 from both sides | `−2x ≤ 6` | unchanged |
| Divide both sides by 2 (positive) | `−x ≤ 3` | unchanged |
| Divide both sides by −2 (negative) | `x ≥ −3` | **reversed** |
| Multiply both sides by −1 (negative) | `2x − 5 ≥ −11` | **reversed** |

Two further habits protect marks. First, **never cross-multiply by an expression whose sign you do
not know** — if a question gives `12/(x − 2) > 3` you cannot multiply by `(x − 2)` without knowing
whether it is positive or negative, and at Foundation level such a question is out of scope, so
check that the inequality really is linear before you start. Second, **taking reciprocals of both
sides also reverses the direction** when both sides carry the same sign: `2 < 5` gives `1/2 > 1/5`.

## 4. The sign reversal: multiplying or dividing by a negative

Rule 3 deserves its own section because it is the single most common source of a wrong answer in
this chapter, and because the wrong answer it produces is always sitting there as an option.

Why does the direction flip? Look at a true statement and multiply it by `−1`:

<div class="formula">3 &lt; 8 is true → multiply by −1 → −3 and −8 → but −3 &gt; −8, not −3 &lt; −8</div>

Multiplying by a negative number reflects both values through zero. Reflection reverses order: the
number that was further to the right lands further to the left. The relation therefore has to turn
around for the statement to stay true.

Three places where the flip is needed and students miss it:

1. **Dividing by a negative coefficient.** `−5x > 20` divides by `−5` to give `x < −4`, not
   `x > −4`.
2. **Multiplying a whole inequality through by −1 to tidy it.** Turning `−x + 3y ≤ 6` into
   `x − 3y ≥ −6` requires the flip. Writing `x − 3y ≤ −6` describes the opposite half-plane, and in
   a graphical question you will then shade the wrong side and read the wrong corner points.
3. **Inside a double inequality.** `−4 ≤ −2x ≤ 10` divided by `−2` gives `2 ≥ x ≥ −5`, which is
   normally rewritten from the small end as `−5 ≤ x ≤ 2`. Both ends flip together, and the two ends
   swap places.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The safe habit: <strong>collect the variable on whichever side leaves it with a positive
    coefficient.</strong> Faced with <code>7 − 3x &gt; 1</code>, do not divide by −3. Add
    <code>3x</code> to both sides and take 1 across instead: <code>6 &gt; 3x</code>, so
    <code>x &lt; 2</code>. No negative division, no flip to remember, no lost mark. Use the flip when
    you must; avoid needing it when you can.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A 10-second check that catches almost every sign error: <strong>test one number</strong>. If you
    solved <code>−5x &gt; 20</code> and got <code>x &lt; −4</code>, try <code>x = −10</code>. Then
    <code>−5 × −10 = 50</code>, and <code>50 &gt; 20</code> is true, so the answer holds. Try
    <code>x = 0</code>: <code>0 &gt; 20</code> is false, as the answer predicts. Two substitutions,
    and the direction is settled beyond argument.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Solving a linear inequality in one variable

The method is the method you already use for a linear equation, with one extra thing to watch. Four
steps:

1. **Clear fractions** by multiplying every term by the LCM of the denominators. The LCM is a
   positive number, so no flip.
2. **Remove brackets** and simplify each side.
3. **Collect the variable terms on one side and the constants on the other**, by adding or
   subtracting. No flip at this stage either.
4. **Divide by the coefficient of the variable.** Flip the sign here if, and only if, that
   coefficient is negative.

Worked through once, in full:

<div class="formula">Solve 3(x − 2) + 4 ≤ 5x + 10</div>

Step 2 gives `3x − 6 + 4 ≤ 5x + 10`, that is `3x − 2 ≤ 5x + 10`. Step 3: take `3x` to the right and
10 to the left, `−12 ≤ 2x`. Step 4: divide by 2, a positive number, so `−6 ≤ x`, which is
`x ≥ −6`. The solution set is every real number from −6 upwards, with −6 itself included because the
original sign was non-strict.

Now the same problem with a negative coefficient left in place:

<div class="formula">Solve 8 − 2(x + 1) &gt; 3x + 16</div>

Removing brackets: `8 − 2x − 2 > 3x + 16`, so `6 − 2x > 3x + 16`. Collect: `6 − 16 > 3x + 2x`, so
`−10 > 5x`, and dividing by the positive 5 gives `−2 > x`, that is `x < −2`. Notice that by moving
`−2x` to the right instead of moving `3x` to the left, the division was by a positive number and no
flip was needed.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    When you clear fractions, multiply <strong>every</strong> term, including the ones that have no
    denominator. In <code>x/3 − 2 &lt; (x + 4)/5</code> the LCM is 15, and the middle term becomes
    <code>−30</code>, not <code>−2</code>. Dropping the multiplier on the whole-number term is the
    commonest arithmetic slip in this section, and it produces an answer that looks perfectly
    reasonable.
  </p>
</Callout>

If the variable disappears completely, the inequality is still telling you something. `2x + 5 < 2x + 9`
reduces to `5 < 9`, which is true whatever *x* is, so the solution set is **all real numbers**. And
`2x + 5 > 2x + 9` reduces to `5 > 9`, which is never true, so the solution set is **empty**. Neither
case is a mistake in your working; both are legitimate answers.

## 6. Three ways to write the answer

Once you have solved the inequality you must present the solution set. Foundation questions ask for
any of three forms, and options are often built by translating the correct answer into the wrong
form.

**Set-builder form** names the universe explicitly: `{x : x ∈ R, x ≥ −6}`. State the universe,
because it changes the answer. If the question says *x* is a natural number, the solution set of
`2x < 9` is `{1, 2, 3, 4}` and not the interval `(−∞, 4.5)`.

**The number line** shows the same thing as a picture. Since this page carries no drawings, describe
one in words the way an examiner's answer key does:

| Solution | Circle at the endpoint | Shading runs |
|---|---|---|
| `x < 6` | Open (hollow) circle at 6 | To the left, without end |
| `x ≤ 6` | Closed (solid) circle at 6 | To the left, without end |
| `x > −2` | Open circle at −2 | To the right, without end |
| `−3 ≤ x < 7` | Closed at −3, open at 7 | Between them only |

**Interval notation** is the compact form. A **square bracket** includes the endpoint; a **round
bracket** excludes it. Infinity always takes a round bracket, because infinity is not a number that
can belong to a set.

| Inequality | Interval | Name |
|---|---|---|
| `a < x < b` | `(a, b)` | Open interval |
| `a ≤ x ≤ b` | `[a, b]` | Closed interval |
| `a ≤ x < b` | `[a, b)` | Half-open (left-closed) |
| `x ≥ a` | `[a, ∞)` | Unbounded above |
| `x < b` | `(−∞, b)` | Unbounded below |

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Read the bracket back into English before you tick an option. <code>[2, 9)</code> says "2 is in,
    9 is out". If the question asked for values "more than 2 and at most 9", the correct interval is
    <code>(2, 9]</code> — the same two numbers, the brackets the other way round. A distractor that
    differs only in bracket type is a bracket question, not an arithmetic question.
  </p>
</Callout>

Practise moving between the three forms until it is automatic. Every row below is the same fact
written three ways:

| Inequality | Set-builder (over the reals) | Interval | Number line |
|---|---|---|---|
| `x > 3` | `{x : x ∈ R, x > 3}` | `(3, ∞)` | Open circle at 3, shaded right |
| `x ≤ −1` | `{x : x ∈ R, x ≤ −1}` | `(−∞, −1]` | Solid circle at −1, shaded left |
| `0 ≤ x ≤ 5` | `{x : x ∈ R, 0 ≤ x ≤ 5}` | `[0, 5]` | Solid circles at 0 and 5, shaded between |
| `−2 < x ≤ 4` | `{x : x ∈ R, −2 < x ≤ 4}` | `(−2, 4]` | Open at −2, solid at 4, shaded between |
| No solution | `∅` | — | Nothing shaded |
| All reals | `R` | `(−∞, ∞)` | The whole line shaded |

The universe matters more than students expect, and a question states it deliberately. Solve
`3x − 4 < 11`. Over the reals the answer is `x < 5`, interval `(−∞, 5)`. Over the **natural
numbers** the same inequality has the solution set `{1, 2, 3, 4}`. Over the **whole numbers** it is
`{0, 1, 2, 3, 4}`, five members rather than four. Over the **integers** it is `{…, 3, 4}`, an
infinite set with no lower end. One inequality, four different correct answers, chosen by a phrase
in the stem.

## 7. Double inequalities

A double inequality traps a variable between two bounds in one line: `−5 ≤ 3x + 4 < 19`. It is
solved exactly like a single inequality, except that **every operation is performed on all three
parts at once**.

Take `−5 ≤ 3x + 4 < 19`. Subtract 4 from all three parts: `−9 ≤ 3x < 15`. Divide all three by 3, a
positive number: `−3 ≤ x < 5`. In interval form, `[−3, 5)`.

When the middle coefficient is negative, both signs flip and the two ends change places. Take
`7 ≤ 5 − 2x ≤ 13`. Subtract 5: `2 ≤ −2x ≤ 8`. Divide by −2 and reverse both signs:
`−1 ≥ x ≥ −4`. Rewrite from the smaller end, as convention requires: `−4 ≤ x ≤ −1`, interval
`[−4, −1]`.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Flipping only the sign you happen to be looking at turns <code>2 ≤ −2x ≤ 8</code> into
    <code>−1 ≥ x ≤ −4</code>, which is not a statement at all. Either flip both, or split the double
    inequality into its two halves, solve them separately, and put the answers back together. The
    split always works and costs one extra line.
  </p>
</Callout>

A double inequality can also be **empty**. `9 < 2x + 1 < 5` requires *x* to be more than 4 and less
than 2 at the same time; no number does that, so the solution set is `∅`. Whenever the left bound
ends up above the right bound, stop and say so.

## 8. Simultaneous linear inequalities in one variable

Two or more separate inequalities in the same variable must hold **together**. Solve each one on its
own, then take the **intersection** — the overlap — of the solution sets.

<div class="formula">Solution of a system = the values common to every inequality in it</div>

Solve `3x − 5 ≤ 7` and `2x + 3 > 5` together. The first gives `3x ≤ 12`, so `x ≤ 4`. The second
gives `2x > 2`, so `x > 1`. The values that satisfy both are those greater than 1 and at most 4:
`1 < x ≤ 4`, interval `(1, 4]`.

Laying the overlap out as a table is the fastest way to avoid an error:

| Inequality | Solved | Interval |
|---|---|---|
| `3x − 5 ≤ 7` | `x ≤ 4` | `(−∞, 4]` |
| `2x + 3 > 5` | `x > 1` | `(1, ∞)` |
| **Both together** | `1 < x ≤ 4` | `(1, 4]` |

Three outcomes are possible and all three appear in question banks:

- **An interval**, as above, when the two ranges overlap partly.
- **One of the two ranges**, when one range sits entirely inside the other. `x ≤ 4` together with
  `x ≤ 9` is simply `x ≤ 4`; the weaker condition adds nothing.
- **The empty set**, when the ranges do not meet at all. `x > 6` together with `x < 2` has no
  solution.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The endpoint needs its own decision. <code>x ≤ 4</code> with <code>x &lt; 4</code> gives
    <code>x &lt; 4</code> — the <strong>strict</strong> condition wins at a shared endpoint, because
    a value must satisfy every inequality, and 4 fails the strict one. Students who write
    <code>x ≤ 4</code> here have taken the union instead of the intersection.
  </p>
</Callout>

Where the variable is restricted to integers, finish by listing. `−2 < x ≤ 3` over the integers is
`{−1, 0, 1, 2, 3}` — five values, and miscounting the endpoints is exactly what a "how many integer
solutions" question is testing.

## 9. Turning a business sentence into an inequality

Most marks in this chapter are not lost in the algebra; they are lost in the translation. Work in
four steps: **name the variables, state their units, write one inequality per limited resource, and
add the non-negativity conditions.**

| Business sentence | Inequality | The word that decides the sign |
|---|---|---|
| The budget for machines is ₹ 2,40,000 | `15000x + 20000y ≤ 240000` | "budget" — a ceiling |
| The carpentry shed offers 120 hours a week | `2x + 3y ≤ 120` | "offers" — availability, a ceiling |
| The contract requires at least 40 units of A | `x ≥ 40` | "at least" — a floor |
| The mix must contain not more than 30% cement | `c ≤ 0.30(c + s)` | "not more than" — a ceiling |
| The lorry carries a maximum of 6 tonnes | `0.5x + 0.8y ≤ 6` | "maximum" — a ceiling |
| The blend needs a minimum of 90 units of protein | `12x + 8y ≥ 90` | "minimum" — a floor |
| Production of B cannot exceed twice that of A | `y ≤ 2x` | "cannot exceed" — a ceiling |
| At least a third of the output must be A | `x ≥ (1/3)(x + y)`, i.e. `2x ≥ y` | "at least" — a floor |

Two translation traps recur.

**A "resource available" line is always `≤`, however the sentence is phrased.** "The shed has 120
hours" does not mean the shed must be worked for 120 hours. It means it cannot be worked for more.

**A ratio condition must be cleared of its fraction before it is a usable constraint.** "At least a
third of the output must be chairs" is `x ≥ (x + y)/3`. Multiply by 3: `3x ≥ x + y`, so `2x ≥ y`,
or `2x − y ≥ 0`. Only in this last form can you find its intercepts and draw it.

A short drill, worked in full. A bakery makes cakes and biscuit packs. Each cake needs 300 g of
flour and 20 minutes of oven time; each biscuit pack needs 200 g of flour and 12 minutes. The
bakery has 60 kg of flour and the oven runs for 8 hours. It has a standing order for at least 40
biscuit packs, and it will not make more cakes than biscuit packs.

| Restriction | Raw form | Tidied constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Flour, 60 kg = 60,000 g | `300x + 200y ≤ 60000` | `3x + 2y ≤ 600` |
| Oven, 8 hours = 480 minutes | `20x + 12y ≤ 480` | `5x + 3y ≤ 120` |
| Standing order | at least 40 packs | `y ≥ 40` |
| Product mix | cakes not more than packs | `x ≤ y`, i.e. `x − y ≤ 0` |
| Non-negativity | counts of items | `x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0` |

Both capacity lines were converted before the coefficients were written — kilograms to grams, hours
to minutes — and both were then divided by a common factor to make the intercepts readable. Neither
step changes which points satisfy the constraint.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Read the stem twice for the two silent words. "The bakery <em>has</em> 60 kg of flour" is a
    ceiling even though no comparison word appears. "It <em>will not</em> make more cakes than
    packs" is a ceiling on <em>x</em> relative to <em>y</em>, not on <em>x</em> alone. Constraints
    that compare two variables have no intercept on one axis in the usual sense — <code>x = y</code>
    passes through the origin — so plan to test <code>(1, 0)</code> or <code>(0, 1)</code> on them.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. A linear inequality in two variables, and its boundary line

`3x + 4y ≤ 24` is a linear inequality in two variables. Its solution is not a set of numbers but a
set of **ordered pairs** — every point `(x, y)` that satisfies it. Drawn on graph paper, those
points fill exactly half of the plane.

The construction has two parts, and they are independent of each other.

**Part 1 — draw the boundary line.** Replace the inequality sign with `=` and plot the resulting
line. The quickest plot is by **intercepts**: put `y = 0` to get the point where the line crosses
the *x*-axis, then put `x = 0` for the *y*-axis. For `3x + 4y = 24` that gives `(8, 0)` and
`(0, 6)`. Join them and extend.

**Part 2 — decide whether the line itself belongs to the solution.** This is where the strict and
non-strict distinction earns its keep:

| Sign in the inequality | Boundary line drawn as | Points on the line are |
|---|---|---|
| `≤` or `≥` | **Solid** (unbroken) | Part of the solution |
| `<` or `>` | **Dashed** (broken) | **Not** part of the solution |

So `3x + 4y ≤ 24` gets a solid line, and `3x + 4y < 24` the same line drawn dashed. The two regions
differ only in their edge, but in a corner-point question that edge is the whole answer: if the
boundary is excluded, the vertices on it are not attainable, and a "maximum production" reading of
such a region has no exact answer at all.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    If a boundary line has an intercept that is not a whole number, plot two convenient points
    instead of forcing the intercepts. For <code>3x + 7y = 42</code> the intercepts are
    <code>(14, 0)</code> and <code>(0, 6)</code>, both clean; for <code>5x + 3y = 22</code> they are
    <code>(4.4, 0)</code> and <code>(0, 7.33)</code>, so plot <code>(2, 4)</code> and
    <code>(−1, 9)</code> instead. Accuracy of the plot decides accuracy of the corner points.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 11. Which side to shade: the test point method

A line cuts the plane in two. One side satisfies the inequality, the other does not, and there is no
way to tell which by looking. The reliable method is to **test one point that is not on the line**.

1. Pick a test point off the boundary. Use `(0, 0)` whenever the line does not pass through the
   origin, because the arithmetic is trivial.
2. Substitute it into the original inequality.
3. If the statement comes out **true**, shade the side of the line that contains the test point. If
   it comes out **false**, shade the other side.

Set out as a table — the format used everywhere below, and the format to reproduce in an exam:

| Inequality | Boundary | Intercepts | Line | Test point | Substitution | True? | Shade |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `3x + 4y ≤ 24` | `3x + 4y = 24` | `(8, 0)`, `(0, 6)` | Solid | `(0, 0)` | `0 ≤ 24` | Yes | Origin side (below-left) |
| `3x + 4y ≥ 24` | `3x + 4y = 24` | `(8, 0)`, `(0, 6)` | Solid | `(0, 0)` | `0 ≥ 24` | No | Far side (above-right) |
| `2x − y > 4` | `2x − y = 4` | `(2, 0)`, `(0, −4)` | Dashed | `(0, 0)` | `0 > 4` | No | Far side (below-right) |
| `y ≤ 2x` | `y = 2x` | Passes through origin | Solid | `(1, 0)` | `0 ≤ 2` | Yes | Side containing `(1, 0)` |

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Two failures of the test-point method, both avoidable. First, <strong>using the origin when the
    boundary passes through it</strong>: <code>y ≤ 2x</code> at <code>(0, 0)</code> gives
    <code>0 ≤ 0</code>, which is true but tells you nothing, because the origin lies on the line.
    Pick <code>(1, 0)</code> or <code>(0, 1)</code> instead. Second, <strong>testing the rearranged
    inequality while remembering the original picture</strong>: if you turned <code>−x + 2y ≥ 4</code>
    into <code>x − 2y ≤ −4</code>, test the version you are actually holding, and test it honestly.
  </p>
</Callout>

There is a shortcut worth knowing but not worth trusting. Once the inequality is written with a
**positive** coefficient of *y* and *y* alone on the left — `y ≤ mx + c` — the solution is the
region **below** the line, and `y ≥ mx + c` the region above. It is quick, but it depends entirely
on that coefficient of *y* being positive, and rearranging to get there is one more chance to lose a
sign. The test point costs five seconds and never lies.

The same substitution answers the other common question type: **does a given point lie in the
region?** Put the pair into every inequality in turn. It belongs only if all of them hold, and one
failure is enough to exclude it. Take `3x + 4y ≤ 24` with `x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0`:

| Point | `3x + 4y` | `≤ 24`? | `x ≥ 0`? | `y ≥ 0`? | In the region? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `(2, 3)` | `6 + 12 = 18` | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes, strictly inside |
| `(8, 0)` | `24 + 0 = 24` | Yes, with equality | Yes | Yes | Yes, on the boundary |
| `(4, 4)` | `12 + 16 = 28` | No | Yes | Yes | No |
| `(−1, 5)` | `−3 + 20 = 17` | Yes | **No** | Yes | No — non-negativity fails |

The last row is the one that catches people. The point satisfies the interesting constraint and
fails a constraint that looked like a formality.

## 12. Systems of inequalities and the feasible region

A business rarely has one constraint. Put several linear inequalities together and the solution is
the set of points satisfying **every one of them at once** — the **common region**, or, in the
language of business problems, the **feasible region**.

<div class="formula">Feasible region = intersection of all the half-planes = the set of all workable production plans</div>

The procedure never changes:

1. Convert each inequality to its boundary line and mark the two intercepts.
2. Draw each line, solid or dashed as the sign requires.
3. Shade the correct half-plane for each, using a test point.
4. The region covered by **all** the shadings is the feasible region. Everything else is out.

Return to the Hubballi workshop. Let *x* be the number of chairs and *y* the number of tables made
in a week. A chair takes 2 hours of carpentry and 2 hours of polishing; a table, being larger but
plainer, takes 3 hours of carpentry and 1 hour of polishing. Carpentry has 120 hours available,
polishing 80.

<div class="formula">Carpentry: 2x + 3y ≤ 120 · Polishing: 2x + y ≤ 80 · x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0</div>

| Constraint | Boundary | x-intercept | y-intercept | Line | Test `(0, 0)` | True? | Shade |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `2x + 3y ≤ 120` | `2x + 3y = 120` | `(60, 0)` | `(0, 40)` | Solid | `0 ≤ 120` | Yes | Towards origin |
| `2x + y ≤ 80` | `2x + y = 80` | `(40, 0)` | `(0, 80)` | Solid | `0 ≤ 80` | Yes | Towards origin |
| `x ≥ 0` | `x = 0` (the y-axis) | — | — | Solid | `(1, 1)`: `1 ≥ 0` | Yes | Right of the axis |
| `y ≥ 0` | `y = 0` (the x-axis) | — | — | Solid | `(1, 1)`: `1 ≥ 0` | Yes | Above the axis |

The four shadings overlap in a four-sided region in the first quadrant. Its corners turn out to be
`(0, 0)`, `(40, 0)`, `(30, 20)` and `(0, 40)` — section 15 shows how those are computed, and worked
example 5 does it in full.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A practical habit: shade <strong>lightly</strong>, or hatch each half-plane in a different
    direction, and then outline the overlap firmly. Students who shade heavily cannot see the common
    region at all by the fourth constraint, and end up guessing the boundary. Some candidates prefer
    to shade the <em>rejected</em> side of each line instead; the region left completely unshaded is
    then the feasible region. Either convention is acceptable if you state which one you used.
  </p>
</Callout>

A system can also have **no feasible region at all**. `x + y ≤ 10` together with `x + y ≥ 25`, both
with `x, y ≥ 0`, asks for points that are simultaneously inside and outside; the half-planes do not
overlap, the feasible region is empty, and the problem as framed cannot be met. That is a perfectly
proper answer to a question, and it is the right answer whenever a minimum requirement exceeds the
available capacity.

Set that empty case out as a table, because the working is what earns the mark, not the conclusion:

| Constraint | Boundary | Intercepts | Test `(0, 0)` | Shade |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `x + y ≤ 10` | `x + y = 10` | `(10, 0)`, `(0, 10)` | `0 ≤ 10` true | Towards the origin |
| `x + y ≥ 25` | `x + y = 25` | `(25, 0)`, `(0, 25)` | `0 ≥ 25` false | Away from the origin |

The two boundary lines are **parallel**, and the shadings point in opposite directions, so nothing
lies in both. Whenever two constraints have proportional coefficients — the same left-hand side, or
one a multiple of the other — check them against each other first. Either one of them is redundant,
because it is implied by the other, or the pair is contradictory and the region is empty. There is
no third possibility.

The mirror image is a **redundant constraint**, which is far more common and much easier to miss.
`x + y ≤ 10` together with `x + y ≤ 30` and `x, y ≥ 0` is just `x + y ≤ 10`; the second line is
drawn, shaded and then found to lie entirely outside the region. A redundant constraint costs
nothing except the time spent on it, and its intercepts are a favourite source of wrong corner
points.

## 13. The non-negativity constraints

Almost every business formulation ends with the line `x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0`. It looks like a formality. It
is not: it is a constraint like any other, it is often the constraint that supplies a corner point,
and leaving it out changes the answer.

Why it is always there: the variables count real things. Chairs, kilograms, lorry loads, rupees
invested, hours booked. **A negative number of chairs has no meaning**, so the mathematics must be
told what the workshop already knows. Without it, `2x + 3y ≤ 120` alone would happily allow
`x = 200, y = −100`.

What it does to the picture: `x ≥ 0` keeps you to the right of the *y*-axis and `y ≥ 0` above the
*x*-axis, so together they confine the whole feasible region to the **first quadrant**. Both axes
become boundary lines of the region, always solid, because the constraint is non-strict.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Two errors follow from treating non-negativity as decoration. The first is <strong>losing corner
    points</strong>: in the workshop above, <code>(40, 0)</code> comes from the polishing line
    meeting <code>y = 0</code>, and <code>(0, 40)</code> from the carpentry line meeting
    <code>x = 0</code>. Ignore the axes and you find only <code>(30, 20)</code>. The second is
    <strong>calling a region bounded when only the axes closed it</strong>. Check whether the
    non-negativity lines are doing the work before you describe the region.
  </p>
</Callout>

Note also what non-negativity does **not** say. `x ≥ 0` permits `x` to be zero — making no chairs
at all is a feasible plan, and the origin is usually a corner point of a "resource ceiling" region.
And `x ≥ 0` says nothing about whole numbers. If the answer must be a whole number of chairs, the
question has to say so; see section 15 and the reviewer's note in the citations file.

## 14. Bounded and unbounded regions

A feasible region is **bounded** if it can be enclosed inside a circle of some finite radius — you
can walk all the way round its edge and come back. It is **unbounded** if it runs away to infinity
in at least one direction, so that one or more of its sides never closes.

The pattern is easy to recognise once you know it:

| Constraint pattern | Typical region | Why |
|---|---|---|
| All `≤` with positive coefficients, plus `x, y ≥ 0` | **Bounded** | Ceilings above, axes below; the region is boxed in |
| All `≥` with positive coefficients, plus `x, y ≥ 0` | **Unbounded** | Floors push you away from the origin, nothing caps you |
| A mixture of `≤` and `≥` | Either — check | Depends on whether the ceilings close every direction |

The workshop region of section 12 is bounded: carpentry and polishing cap it from above, the axes
close it from below, and it has four corners.

Now a minimum-requirement problem. A poultry farm blends two feeds. Each kilogram of feed X supplies
1 unit of protein and 3 units of minerals; each kilogram of feed Y supplies 2 units of protein and 1
unit of minerals. The daily ration must supply at least 20 units of protein and at least 15 units of
minerals.

<div class="formula">Protein: x + 2y ≥ 20 · Minerals: 3x + y ≥ 15 · x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0</div>

| Constraint | Boundary | x-intercept | y-intercept | Line | Test `(0, 0)` | True? | Shade |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `x + 2y ≥ 20` | `x + 2y = 20` | `(20, 0)` | `(0, 10)` | Solid | `0 ≥ 20` | No | Away from origin |
| `3x + y ≥ 15` | `3x + y = 15` | `(5, 0)` | `(0, 15)` | Solid | `0 ≥ 15` | No | Away from origin |

The common region lies above and to the right of both lines and is **unbounded**: buying more of
both feeds is always allowed, only buying too little is not. Its corner points are `(0, 15)`,
`(2, 9)` and `(20, 0)` — three corners, and the region simply continues upward and rightward past
the first and the last.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    An unbounded region is <strong>not</strong> an empty region, and "no solution" is the wrong
    answer to a question about one. The farm above has infinitely many workable rations. Unbounded
    means the region has no outer edge in some direction; empty means no point satisfies the system
    at all. They are opposite conditions, and a distractor pairing them is checking whether you have
    read the constraint signs.
  </p>
</Callout>

Unboundedness has one real consequence at Foundation level. On a bounded region every corner point
exists and can be listed. On an unbounded region the corners still exist and are still listed, but
the region contains points arbitrarily far out, so a question asking for a *largest* feasible value
has no answer there. That is why cost-minimising blends give unbounded regions and revenue-capping
capacity problems give bounded ones.

How to settle the question in an exam, without drawing to scale. Ask: **is every direction of travel
from a point inside the region eventually blocked?** Take any feasible point and imagine walking
along each axis and along the diagonal away from the origin. If some direction never leaves the
region, the region is unbounded.

| System | Walk right (`x` increasing) | Walk up (`y` increasing) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| `2x + 3y ≤ 120`, `2x + y ≤ 80`, `x, y ≥ 0` | Blocked by both ceilings | Blocked by both ceilings | **Bounded** |
| `x + 2y ≥ 20`, `3x + y ≥ 15`, `x, y ≥ 0` | Never blocked | Never blocked | **Unbounded** |
| `x + y ≥ 8`, `x + 2y ≤ 20`, `x, y ≥ 0` | Blocked at `x = 20` on the second line | Blocked at `y = 10` | **Bounded** |
| `x ≥ 2`, `y ≥ 3`, `x + y ≤ 12` | Blocked | Blocked | **Bounded** |
| `y ≥ 3`, `x ≥ 0` | Never blocked to the right | Never blocked upward | **Unbounded** |

The third row is the case worth studying, because it mixes the two signs and still comes out
bounded: the `≤` constraint caps both variables even though a `≥` constraint is present. So the
table of patterns in this section is a guide, not a rule. When the signs are mixed, walk the
directions.

## 15. Corner points, and how to read the vertices

A **corner point** (vertex, or extreme point) is a point where two boundary lines of the feasible
region cross **and which satisfies every constraint**. Both halves matter. Lines cross in many
places; only some of those crossings are corners of the region.

The method that never goes wrong:

1. **List every boundary line**, including `x = 0` and `y = 0`.
2. **Take the lines two at a time** and solve each pair simultaneously. With four lines there are
   six pairs; with five lines, ten.
3. **Test each intersection against all the constraints.** Keep it only if it satisfies every one.
   Discard the rest — they are crossings outside the region.
4. **Keep the answers exact.** A vertex is frequently a fraction, and rounding it early is how
   corner-point questions are lost.

Worked on a region with a fractional vertex. A dairy at Belagavi bottles two packs, and the plant
gives `x + y ≤ 12` (filling-line hours) and `x + 4y ≤ 32` (cold-store space), with `x, y ≥ 0`.

| Pair of lines solved | Intersection | Feasible? | Test that decides it |
|---|---|---|---|
| `x = 0` and `y = 0` | `(0, 0)` | Yes | `0 ≤ 12` and `0 ≤ 32` |
| `y = 0` and `x + y = 12` | `(12, 0)` | Yes | `12 + 0 = 12 ≤ 32` |
| `y = 0` and `x + 4y = 32` | `(32, 0)` | **No** | `32 + 0 = 32 > 12`, filling line breached |
| `x + y = 12` and `x + 4y = 32` | `(16/3, 20/3)` | Yes | Both hold with equality |
| `x = 0` and `x + 4y = 32` | `(0, 8)` | Yes | `0 + 8 = 8 ≤ 12` |
| `x = 0` and `x + y = 12` | `(0, 12)` | **No** | `0 + 48 = 48 > 32`, cold store breached |

Four crossings survive, so the region has four corners: `(0, 0)`, `(12, 0)`, `(16/3, 20/3)` and
`(0, 8)`. Two crossings were rejected, and each rejected point is exactly the distractor an option
list will offer.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Keep <code>(16/3, 20/3)</code> as a fraction. Written as <code>(5.33, 6.67)</code> it fails the
    equality test on both lines, and any later arithmetic inherits the error. Only round at the very
    last step, and only if the question asks you to.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 16. A full construction, start to finish

One problem, every step, in the order an examiner expects to see them.

A garment unit at Tiruppur makes shirts and trousers, counted in dozens. A dozen shirts needs 3
hours of cutting and 1 hour of stitching; a dozen trousers needs 2 hours of cutting and 2 hours of
stitching. Cutting has 60 hours and stitching 40 hours in the week. The buyer will not take more
than 16 dozen shirts. Set up the system and find the feasible region and its corner points.

**Step 1 — name the variables.** Let *x* be dozens of shirts and *y* dozens of trousers made in the
week.

**Step 2 — one inequality per restriction.**

<div class="formula">Cutting: 3x + 2y ≤ 60 · Stitching: x + 2y ≤ 40 · Buyer's limit: x ≤ 16 · x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0</div>

**Step 3 — intercepts, line type and side, in one table.**

| Constraint | Boundary | x-intercept | y-intercept | Line | Test point | Substitution | Shade |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `3x + 2y ≤ 60` | `3x + 2y = 60` | `(20, 0)` | `(0, 30)` | Solid | `(0, 0)` | `0 ≤ 60` true | Towards origin |
| `x + 2y ≤ 40` | `x + 2y = 40` | `(40, 0)` | `(0, 20)` | Solid | `(0, 0)` | `0 ≤ 40` true | Towards origin |
| `x ≤ 16` | `x = 16` (vertical) | `(16, 0)` | none | Solid | `(0, 0)` | `0 ≤ 16` true | Left of the line |
| `x ≥ 0` | `x = 0` | — | — | Solid | `(1, 1)` | `1 ≥ 0` true | Right of the axis |
| `y ≥ 0` | `y = 0` | — | — | Solid | `(1, 1)` | `1 ≥ 0` true | Above the axis |

**Step 4 — solve the pairs and filter by feasibility.**

| Pair solved | Point | Kept? | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| `x = 0`, `y = 0` | `(0, 0)` | Yes | Satisfies all three ceilings |
| `y = 0`, `x = 16` | `(16, 0)` | Yes | Cutting 48 ≤ 60; stitching 16 ≤ 40 |
| `y = 0`, `3x + 2y = 60` | `(20, 0)` | No | `20 > 16`, buyer's limit breached |
| `x = 16`, `3x + 2y = 60` | `(16, 6)` | Yes | Stitching `16 + 12 = 28 ≤ 40` |
| `x = 16`, `x + 2y = 40` | `(16, 12)` | No | Cutting `48 + 24 = 72 > 60` |
| `3x + 2y = 60`, `x + 2y = 40` | `(10, 15)` | Yes | `x = 10 ≤ 16` |
| `x = 0`, `x + 2y = 40` | `(0, 20)` | Yes | Cutting `0 + 40 = 40 ≤ 60` |
| `x = 0`, `3x + 2y = 60` | `(0, 30)` | No | Stitching `0 + 60 = 60 > 40` |

**Step 5 — state the answer.** The feasible region is the five-sided figure with corner points
`(0, 0)`, `(16, 0)`, `(16, 6)`, `(10, 15)` and `(0, 20)`. It is **bounded**, because the three
ceilings and the two axes close it on every side. Every point inside or on the edge of that figure
is a production plan the week can actually deliver.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Notice that three of the eight crossings were discarded, and that two of them — <code>(20, 0)</code>
    and <code>(0, 30)</code> — are intercepts you had already plotted. An intercept is not
    automatically a corner point. It is a corner point only when it survives every other constraint.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 17. The constraint families that keep coming back

Questions change their story; the arithmetic recycles. Five families cover almost everything asked.

**Machine or labour hours.** Per-unit time × units, summed over products, cannot exceed the hours
available. `2x + 3y ≤ 120`. Watch for a plant that runs two shifts, or a month stated in days: the
right-hand side has to be converted to hours before you use it.

**Raw material or component limits.** Per-unit consumption × units, summed, cannot exceed the stock
on hand. A firm with 900 kg of steel, using 5 kg per bracket and 3 kg per clamp, gets
`5x + 3y ≤ 900`. Where two products share a scarce component, that shared line is usually the one
that binds.

**Budget or investment ceilings.** Price × quantity, summed, cannot exceed the money available.
With machines at ₹ 15,000 and ₹ 20,000 and a budget of ₹ 2,40,000, `15000x + 20000y ≤ 240000`, which
tidies to `3x + 4y ≤ 48`. Dividing through by the common factor before plotting makes the intercepts
readable and changes nothing.

**Blending, diet and quality minimums.** Nutrient or ingredient content × quantity, summed, must
reach at least the specified level. `12x + 8y ≥ 90`. These are the `≥` family, and they are what
produce unbounded regions.

**Transport and storage capacity.** Weight or volume per unit × units cannot exceed what the vehicle
or godown holds. A lorry rated at 6 tonnes carrying crates of 0.5 t and 0.8 t gives
`0.5x + 0.8y ≤ 6`, or `5x + 8y ≤ 60` once cleared of decimals.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Units are where these go wrong. If the machine time is given in minutes per unit and the
    availability in hours, one side must be converted — 40 minutes a unit against 60 hours a week is
    <code>40x ≤ 3600</code> in minutes, not <code>40x ≤ 60</code>. Similarly a budget in lakhs
    against prices in rupees. Write the unit next to every coefficient before you form the
    inequality, and the error cannot happen.
  </p>
</Callout>

Two more patterns sit on top of these families and are worth naming, because they look unfamiliar
the first time.

**A market or contract limit on one product alone.** "The buyer will not lift more than 16 dozen
shirts" is `x ≤ 16`, a **vertical** line. "At least 40 packs must be made" is `y ≥ 40`, a
**horizontal** line. Neither has two intercepts, and both are easy to plot; the only care needed is
which side to shade, and the origin settles it.

**A proportion between two products.** "Output of B must be at least half the output of A" is
`y ≥ x/2`, that is `2y − x ≥ 0`. Such a line always passes through the origin, so the test point
must be somewhere else. Take `(4, 0)`: `2(0) − 4 = −4`, and `−4 ≥ 0` is false, so shade the side
away from `(4, 0)` — the upper-left side of the line.

| Family | Sign | Right-hand side is | Effect on the region |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hours, material, budget, capacity | `≤` | What is available | Caps the region; pushes it towards the origin |
| Diet, blend, contract minimum | `≥` | What is required | Pushes the region away from the origin; often unbounded |
| Market ceiling on one product | `≤` | A number | A vertical or horizontal cut |
| Contract floor on one product | `≥` | A number | A vertical or horizontal cut |
| Proportion between products | either | Zero, after clearing | A line through the origin |

## 18. Negative marking and when to guess

Paper 3 is fully objective, and a wrong answer costs 0.25 marks while a blank costs nothing. That
changes the arithmetic of guessing, and it is worth working out once, calmly, rather than deciding
under time pressure.

A pure guess among four options is right one time in four. Over four such guesses you expect
`1 × 1 = 1` mark from the one you get right and `3 × 0.25 = 0.75` marks lost on the three you get
wrong, a net gain of 0.25 marks. So blind guessing is very slightly positive, and not worth much.
What changes the picture is elimination. Rule out one option and you are guessing among three: the
expected value rises to about 0.33 marks per guess. Rule out two and it is 0.5 marks per guess —
genuinely worth doing.

This chapter is unusually well suited to elimination, because its wrong options come from named
errors rather than from random numbers. If you know the boundary is non-strict, every option with a
round bracket at that endpoint goes. If you know the sign reverses, the option that kept the
original direction goes. If a corner point must satisfy both binding constraints, substitute and
discard the ones that fail. Two eliminations turn a coin-toss into a favourable guess, and on a
numerical question you can often verify the survivor outright by substitution — at which point it is
not a guess at all.

    The 9 became 108 and the 3 became 36 — the whole-number terms are multiplied too.

    <strong>x ≤ 63/10</strong><br />
    Solution set <code>{'{'}x : x ∈ R, x ≤ 6.3{'}'}</code> · interval <strong>(−∞, 6.3]</strong> ·

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — a double inequality with a negative middle term (moderate)">
  <p>
    Solve <code>−8 ≤ 5 − 3x &lt; 13</code>. How many integers satisfy it?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Subtract 5 from all three parts</div>
    <code>−8 − 5 ≤ −3x &lt; 13 − 5</code>, that is <code>−13 ≤ −3x &lt; 8</code><br />
    Subtraction never changes a sign, so both symbols still point the same way.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Divide all three parts by −3, reversing both signs</div>
    <code>13/3 ≥ x &gt; −8/3</code><br />
    Rewrite from the smaller end, which is the standard presentation:
    <strong>−8/3 &lt; x ≤ 13/3</strong><br />
    Interval <strong>(−8/3, 13/3]</strong>, that is approximately <code>(−2.67, 4.33]</code>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Count the integers</div>
    The smallest integer above −2.67 is −2; the largest integer not exceeding 4.33 is 4.<br />
    Integers: −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 → <strong>7 values</strong> (count is 4 − (−2) + 1 = 7).
  </div>
  <p>
    The bracket types came from opposite ends of the original line, and they swapped when the signs
    reversed. The strict end started on the right (<code>&lt; 13</code>) and finished on the left.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — simultaneous inequalities in one variable (moderate)">
  <p>
    A wholesaler will accept an order of <em>x</em> cartons only if it satisfies both
    <code>5x − 3 ≤ 3x + 11</code> and <code>2(3x − 1) &gt; 4x + 6</code>. Find the permitted order
    sizes, and the number of whole cartons possible.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Solve the first inequality</div>
    <code>5x − 3 ≤ 3x + 11</code> → <code>5x − 3x ≤ 11 + 3</code> → <code>2x ≤ 14</code> →
    <strong>x ≤ 7</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Solve the second inequality</div>
    <code>6x − 2 &gt; 4x + 6</code> → <code>2x &gt; 8</code> → <strong>x &gt; 4</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Intersect, then count</div>
    Both must hold: <strong>4 &lt; x ≤ 7</strong>, interval <strong>(4, 7]</strong>.<br />
    Cartons are whole units, so <code>x ∈ {'{'}5, 6, 7{'}'}</code> — <strong>3 possible order
    sizes</strong>. Four is excluded because the second inequality is strict; seven is included
    because the first is not.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — translating a business brief into a system (moderate)">
  <p>
    A workshop at Nashik plans to buy lathes and drilling machines. A lathe costs ₹ 15,000 and
    occupies 4 sq m of floor; a drilling machine costs ₹ 20,000 and occupies 3 sq m. The capital
    sanctioned is ₹ 2,40,000 and the shed offers 60 sq m of usable floor. The production plan needs
    at least 3 lathes. Write the system of inequalities, and test whether the purchase of 8 lathes
    with 5 drilling machines, and of 12 lathes with 4 drilling machines, is feasible.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Name the variables and their units</div>
    Let <em>x</em> = number of lathes bought and <em>y</em> = number of drilling machines bought.
    Both are counts of machines.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — One inequality per restriction</div>
    Capital: <code>15000x + 20000y ≤ 2,40,000</code>, which divides by 5,000 to
    <strong>3x + 4y ≤ 48</strong><br />
    Floor: <code>4x + 3y ≤ 60</code><br />
    Plan requirement: <strong>x ≥ 3</strong> ("at least", so non-strict)<br />
    Non-negativity: <code>x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0</code> — here <code>x ≥ 3</code> already implies
    <code>x ≥ 0</code>, but <code>y ≥ 0</code> is still needed.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Test the two proposed purchases</div>
    <strong>8 lathes, 5 drills:</strong> capital <code>3(8) + 4(5) = 44 ≤ 48</code> ✓ · floor
    <code>4(8) + 3(5) = 47 ≤ 60</code> ✓ · <code>x = 8 ≥ 3</code> ✓ → <strong>feasible</strong>
    (actual spend ₹ 2,20,000).<br />
    <strong>12 lathes, 4 drills:</strong> capital <code>3(12) + 4(4) = 52 &gt; 48</code> ✗ →
    <strong>not feasible</strong>; the spend of ₹ 2,60,000 exceeds the sanction by ₹ 20,000, even
    though the floor at 60 sq m is exactly met and the lathe requirement is satisfied. One failed
    constraint is enough.
  </div>
  <p>
    Dividing the capital line by 5,000 changed nothing about which points satisfy it, and it turned
    the intercepts into the readable pair <code>(16, 0)</code> and <code>(0, 12)</code>. Note also
    that machines come in whole numbers, so the practical answers here are the <em>lattice</em>
    points of the region; the region itself is still drawn as a continuous area.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — the feasible region and its corner points (moderate)">
  <p>
    The Hubballi workshop of section 12 makes <em>x</em> chairs and <em>y</em> tables a week. A chair
    takes 2 hours of carpentry and 2 hours of polishing; a table takes 3 hours of carpentry and 1
    hour of polishing. Carpentry offers 120 hours and polishing 80. Draw the feasible region and
    list its corner points.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — The system</div>
    <code>2x + 3y ≤ 120</code> (carpentry) · <code>2x + y ≤ 80</code> (polishing) ·
    <code>x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0</code>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Construction table</div>
    <code>2x + 3y = 120</code>: intercepts <code>(60, 0)</code> and <code>(0, 40)</code>; solid line;
    test <code>(0, 0)</code> gives <code>0 ≤ 120</code>, true → shade towards the origin.<br />
    <code>2x + y = 80</code>: intercepts <code>(40, 0)</code> and <code>(0, 80)</code>; solid line;
    test <code>(0, 0)</code> gives <code>0 ≤ 80</code>, true → shade towards the origin.<br />
    Both axes solid, region confined to the first quadrant.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Solve the pairs, then filter</div>
    <code>x = 0, y = 0</code> → <code>(0, 0)</code>: feasible.<br />
    <code>y = 0</code> with polishing → <code>(40, 0)</code>: carpentry <code>80 ≤ 120</code> ✓
    feasible.<br />
    <code>y = 0</code> with carpentry → <code>(60, 0)</code>: polishing <code>120 &gt; 80</code> ✗
    rejected.<br />
    Carpentry ∩ polishing: subtract <code>2x + y = 80</code> from <code>2x + 3y = 120</code> to get
    <code>2y = 40</code>, so <code>y = 20</code> and then <code>2x = 60</code>, <code>x = 30</code> →
    <strong>(30, 20)</strong>: feasible, both binding.<br />
    <code>x = 0</code> with carpentry → <code>(0, 40)</code>: polishing <code>40 ≤ 80</code> ✓
    feasible.<br />
    <code>x = 0</code> with polishing → <code>(0, 80)</code>: carpentry <code>240 &gt; 120</code> ✗
    rejected.
  </div>
  <p>
    Corner points: <strong>(0, 0), (40, 0), (30, 20), (0, 40)</strong>. The region is
    <strong>bounded</strong>. At <code>(30, 20)</code> both departments are fully used —
    <code>2(30) + 3(20) = 120</code> carpentry hours and <code>2(30) + 20 = 80</code> polishing hours
    — which is why that vertex is the one a planner looks at first.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 6 — a corner point that is not a whole number (hard)">
  <p>
    A dairy at Belagavi fills toned milk and curd, measured in thousands of litres a day, <em>x</em>
    and <em>y</em>. The filling line allows <code>x + y ≤ 12</code> and the cold store allows
    <code>x + 4y ≤ 32</code>, with <code>x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0</code>. Find the corner points exactly.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Intercepts and sides</div>
    <code>x + y = 12</code>: <code>(12, 0)</code> and <code>(0, 12)</code>; solid; test
    <code>(0, 0)</code> → <code>0 ≤ 12</code> true → shade towards the origin.<br />
    <code>x + 4y = 32</code>: <code>(32, 0)</code> and <code>(0, 8)</code>; solid; test
    <code>(0, 0)</code> → <code>0 ≤ 32</code> true → shade towards the origin.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — The intersection of the two slanting lines</div>
    Subtract <code>x + y = 12</code> from <code>x + 4y = 32</code>: <code>3y = 20</code>, so
    <code>y = 20/3</code>.<br />
    Then <code>x = 12 − 20/3 = 36/3 − 20/3 = 16/3</code>.<br />
    Vertex = <strong>(16/3, 20/3)</strong>. Check both: <code>16/3 + 20/3 = 36/3 = 12</code> ✓ and
    <code>16/3 + 80/3 = 96/3 = 32</code> ✓.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Filter the remaining crossings</div>
    <code>(0, 0)</code>: feasible.<br />
    <code>(12, 0)</code> from <code>y = 0</code> and the filling line: cold store
    <code>12 ≤ 32</code> ✓ feasible.<br />
    <code>(32, 0)</code> from <code>y = 0</code> and the cold store: filling line
    <code>32 &gt; 12</code> ✗ rejected.<br />
    <code>(0, 8)</code> from <code>x = 0</code> and the cold store: filling line
    <code>8 ≤ 12</code> ✓ feasible.<br />
    <code>(0, 12)</code> from <code>x = 0</code> and the filling line: cold store
    <code>48 &gt; 32</code> ✗ rejected.
  </div>
  <p>
    Corner points: <strong>(0, 0), (12, 0), (16/3, 20/3), (0, 8)</strong> — a bounded, four-sided
    region. Rounded to <code>(5.33, 6.67)</code> the fourth vertex satisfies neither line exactly, so
    an examiner expecting a substitution check would mark it wrong. Keep the thirds.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 7 — an unbounded region (moderate-hard)">
  <p>
    A poultry farm blends feed X and feed Y, in kilograms a day. Each kilogram of X supplies 1 unit
    of protein and 3 units of minerals; each kilogram of Y supplies 2 units of protein and 1 unit of
    minerals. The ration must supply at least 20 units of protein and at least 15 units of minerals.
    Describe the feasible region and give its corner points.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — The system, and the shading</div>
    <code>x + 2y ≥ 20</code> · <code>3x + y ≥ 15</code> · <code>x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0</code><br />
    <code>x + 2y = 20</code>: <code>(20, 0)</code>, <code>(0, 10)</code>; test <code>(0, 0)</code> →
    <code>0 ≥ 20</code> false → shade <em>away</em> from the origin.<br />
    <code>3x + y = 15</code>: <code>(5, 0)</code>, <code>(0, 15)</code>; test <code>(0, 0)</code> →
    <code>0 ≥ 15</code> false → shade <em>away</em> from the origin.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — The crossing of the two lines</div>
    From <code>3x + y = 15</code>, <code>y = 15 − 3x</code>. Substitute into
    <code>x + 2y = 20</code>:<br />
    <code>x + 30 − 6x = 20</code> → <code>−5x = −10</code> → <code>x = 2</code>, so
    <code>y = 15 − 6 = 9</code> → <strong>(2, 9)</strong>, feasible on both.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — The two axis corners</div>
    On <code>x = 0</code>: the protein line gives <code>(0, 10)</code>, but minerals
    <code>0 + 10 = 10 &lt; 15</code> ✗. The mineral line gives <code>(0, 15)</code>, and protein
    <code>0 + 30 = 30 ≥ 20</code> ✓ → <strong>(0, 15)</strong> is the corner.<br />
    On <code>y = 0</code>: the mineral line gives <code>(5, 0)</code>, but protein
    <code>5 &lt; 20</code> ✗. The protein line gives <code>(20, 0)</code>, and minerals
    <code>60 ≥ 15</code> ✓ → <strong>(20, 0)</strong> is the corner.
  </div>
  <p>
    Corner points: <strong>(0, 15), (2, 9), (20, 0)</strong>. The region is <strong>unbounded</strong>
    — feeding more of both is always permitted, so it extends without limit upward and to the right.
    It is emphatically not empty: <code>(10, 10)</code>, for instance, supplies 30 units of protein
    and 40 of minerals and lies well inside it.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 8 — a ratio constraint and a floor together (hard)">
  <p>
    A trust has ₹ 6,00,000 to place between a bond scheme and an equity scheme. It need not invest
    the whole sum. At least twice as much must go into bonds as into equity, and at least ₹ 1,00,000
    must go into equity. Taking <em>x</em> and <em>y</em> as the amounts in bonds and equity in
    thousands of rupees, set up the system and find the corner points.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Translate, clearing the ratio</div>
    Funds: <code>x + y ≤ 600</code> ("need not invest the whole sum", so a ceiling, not an
    equality).<br />
    Ratio: "at least twice as much in bonds as in equity" → <code>x ≥ 2y</code>, i.e.
    <code>x − 2y ≥ 0</code>.<br />
    Floor on equity: <code>y ≥ 100</code>. Non-negativity: <code>x ≥ 0</code> (and
    <code>y ≥ 100</code> already covers <code>y ≥ 0</code>).
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Boundary lines and sides</div>
    <code>x + y = 600</code>: <code>(600, 0)</code>, <code>(0, 600)</code>; test <code>(0, 0)</code>
    → true → shade towards the origin.<br />
    <code>x = 2y</code> passes through the origin, so test <code>(600, 0)</code>:
    <code>600 ≥ 0</code> true → shade the side containing <code>(600, 0)</code>, below the line.<br />
    <code>y = 100</code>: horizontal; test <code>(0, 0)</code> → <code>0 ≥ 100</code> false → shade
    above it.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Solve the three pairs</div>
    <code>y = 100</code> with <code>x = 2y</code> → <code>x = 200</code> → <strong>(200, 100)</strong>;
    funds <code>300 ≤ 600</code> ✓.<br />
    <code>y = 100</code> with <code>x + y = 600</code> → <code>x = 500</code> →
    <strong>(500, 100)</strong>; ratio <code>500 ≥ 200</code> ✓.<br />
    <code>x = 2y</code> with <code>x + y = 600</code> → <code>3y = 600</code>, <code>y = 200</code>,
    <code>x = 400</code> → <strong>(400, 200)</strong>; equity <code>200 ≥ 100</code> ✓.
  </div>
  <p>
    Corner points: <strong>(200, 100), (500, 100), (400, 200)</strong> — a bounded triangle. In
    rupees, the three plans are bonds ₹ 2,00,000 with equity ₹ 1,00,000; bonds ₹ 5,00,000 with equity
    ₹ 1,00,000; and bonds ₹ 4,00,000 with equity ₹ 2,00,000. The first leaves ₹ 3,00,000 uninvested,
    which the wording permits. Had the brief said the whole sum <em>must</em> be placed, the funds
    line would be an equation, the region would collapse to the segment joining
    <code>(500, 100)</code> and <code>(400, 200)</code>, and <code>(200, 100)</code> would disappear.
  </p>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

- **Not reversing the sign on a negative multiplier or divisor.** `−5x > 20` becomes `x < −4`, never
  `x > −4`. Collect the variable on the side that leaves it positive and the problem disappears.
- **Reversing the sign when only adding or subtracting.** The flip belongs to multiplication and
  division alone. Transposing a term across the inequality never changes its direction.
- **Flipping one end of a double inequality and not the other.** `2 ≤ −2x ≤ 8` becomes
  `−4 ≤ x ≤ −1`; both symbols turn, and the two bounds change places.
- **Treating a strict inequality as inclusive, or the reverse.** "Less than 500" is `< 500`; "not
  more than 500" is `≤ 500`. It decides whether the endpoint is in the answer, whether the interval
  takes a square or a round bracket, and whether the boundary line is solid or dashed.
- **Round bracket around a finite endpoint that belongs to the set.** `[2, 9)` and `(2, 9]` contain
  different numbers. Infinity, however, always takes a round bracket.
- **Shading the wrong half-plane.** Test a point, substitute honestly, and shade towards the test
  point only when the statement came out true. Never use `(0, 0)` when the line passes through it.
- **Multiplying an inequality by −1 to tidy it and then shading as before.** `−x + 2y ≤ 6` and
  `x − 2y ≤ −6` are opposite half-planes; only `x − 2y ≥ −6` is the same one.
- **Assuming every intercept is a corner point.** An intercept is a corner only if it satisfies every
  other constraint too. Solve the pairs, then filter.
- **Reading a vertex off the wrong pair of constraints.** In a five-line system there are ten
  crossings and often only four or five corners. The pair that is *binding* at a corner is the pair
  that meets there with equality; check both.
- **Rounding a fractional vertex early.** `(16/3, 20/3)` is exact; `(5.33, 6.67)` satisfies neither
  boundary equation.
- **Calling an unbounded region "no solution".** Unbounded means no outer edge; empty means no point
  at all. A system of `≥` constraints with non-negativity is usually unbounded and always has
  solutions.
- **Dropping the non-negativity constraints.** They are what confine the region to the first
  quadrant, and they supply the corner points that sit on the axes.
- **Not clearing a ratio condition.** "At least a third must be product A" is `3x ≥ x + y`, that is
  `2x − y ≥ 0`. Until it is in that form it has no intercepts and cannot be drawn.
- **Mismatched units in a constraint.** Minutes against hours, rupees against lakhs, kilograms
  against tonnes. Convert before you write the coefficient, not after.
- **Forgetting that whole units may be required.** A vertex of `(16/3, 20/3)` is a correct corner of
  the region, but 16/3 machines cannot be bought. Answer what was asked: the corner point, or the
  workable whole-number plan.

## One-page revision summary

**The four rules:**

<div class="formula">Add or subtract the same quantity on both sides → direction unchanged</div>
<div class="formula">Multiply or divide by a POSITIVE quantity → direction unchanged</div>
<div class="formula">Multiply or divide by a NEGATIVE quantity → direction REVERSES</div>
<div class="formula">Two inequalities of the same direction may be added, never subtracted</div>

**Signs and their words:** `<` less than, below, under · `>` more than, exceeds, above ·
`≤` at most, not more than, available, up to · `≥` at least, not less than, minimum, required.
Strict signs exclude the boundary; non-strict signs include it.

**Presenting the answer:** solution set `{x : x ∈ R, x ≤ 6}` · number line with an open circle for a
strict bound and a closed circle for a non-strict one · interval with `[ ]` for an included endpoint
and `( )` for an excluded one, infinity always `( )`.

**Systems in one variable:** solve each inequality separately, then take the **intersection**. At a
shared endpoint the strict condition wins. Non-overlapping ranges give the empty set.

**Two variables, in order:** replace the sign with `=` · plot by intercepts (`y = 0` then `x = 0`) ·
**solid** line for `≤`/`≥`, **dashed** for `<`/`>` · test a point off the line, usually `(0, 0)` ·
shade towards the test point if the statement is **true**, away if **false**.

<div class="formula">Feasible region = the overlap of all the shaded half-planes, confined to the first quadrant by x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0</div>

**Corner points:** list every boundary line including both axes · solve the lines in pairs · keep
only the intersections that satisfy **every** constraint · keep fractions exact.

**Bounded or unbounded:** all `≤` with positive coefficients plus non-negativity → bounded; all `≥`
with positive coefficients plus non-negativity → unbounded. Unbounded is not empty. Empty means the
half-planes never overlap.

**Constraint families:** machine or labour hours (`≤`) · raw material stock (`≤`) · budget or
investment ceiling (`≤`, and divide through by the common factor) · blending, diet or quality
minimum (`≥`) · transport or storage capacity (`≤`) · ratio conditions, cleared of fractions before
plotting.

**Marking:** one mark for a correct answer, 0.25 deducted for a wrong one, nothing for a blank. Two
eliminations make a guess clearly worthwhile; a substitution check often removes the guess entirely.
