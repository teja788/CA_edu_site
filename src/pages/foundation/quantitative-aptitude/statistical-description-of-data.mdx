A milk co-operative in Anand collects the day's yield from four thousand members. By evening the
society must know one thing above all: how much milk came in, and how it was spread across the small,
medium and large suppliers. The raw register — four thousand entries, litre by litre — answers
nothing on its own. Nobody can read four thousand numbers and see a pattern. Yet group those same
numbers into a few bands, count how many members fall in each, and the picture appears at once: most
members bring between two and six litres, a handful bring twenty or more, and the society can plan
its tankers accordingly.

That move — from a heap of figures to a short, readable summary — is the whole of this chapter. It
is the first, quiet half of statistics: not the averages and correlations that come later, but the
work of collecting the numbers honestly, arranging them so a human can see them, and drawing them so
a reader grasps them in a glance. Get this half wrong and every average built on top is an average
of rubbish.

Paper 3 is fully objective, so you will not be asked to design a survey or draw a chart by hand. You
will be asked to name the scale a variable is measured on, to pick the right sampling method, to
compute a class width or a mid-value, to read a cumulative frequency, to convert a share into a
pie-chart angle, or to locate a median on an ogive. This chapter takes each of those in turn, and
shows the small computation behind every one, so that a definition you half-remember never costs you
a mark.

## 1. What statistics means

The word **statistics** carries two senses, and the examiner tests both.

- In the **plural** sense, statistics are the numerical facts themselves — the yields, the marks,
  the prices. A single such figure is a *datum*; many are *data*.
- In the **singular** sense, statistics is the **science** of collecting, organising, presenting,
  analysing and interpreting numerical data so that sound decisions can be taken under uncertainty.

This chapter lives almost entirely in the first three verbs of that definition — collecting,
organising and presenting. Analysing and interpreting (averages, dispersion, correlation,
probability) are the chapters that follow.

Fix three more words now, because every later idea reuses them.

- A **variable** is a characteristic that changes from one item to the next — height, marks, the
  district a person lives in.
- A **variable is discrete** when it can take only separate, countable values (the number of
  children in a family: 0, 1, 2, …, never 2.4), and **continuous** when it can take any value in a
  range (height, weight, time), limited only by the precision of the instrument.
- An **attribute** is a characteristic that is not numerical at all — sex, religion, grade of a
  product. Attributes are counted, not measured.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Statistics deals with <strong>aggregates</strong>, not single items. The height of one student is
    a fact of anatomy; the heights of a whole class, arranged and summarised, are a fact of
    statistics. A question that turns on "the study of a single figure" is testing whether you know
    that statistics is a science of the mass, not of the individual.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. Primary and secondary data

Data are classified by **who collected them and for what purpose**.

- **Primary data** are collected by the investigator, first-hand, for the specific enquiry in hand.
  A researcher who goes door to door with her own questionnaire is gathering primary data. They are
  original, tailored to the need, and expensive in time and money.
- **Secondary data** are data already collected by someone else, for some other purpose, and reused.
  The Census of India tables, RBI bulletins, a company's published accounts, a newspaper's price
  series — all are secondary in the hands of a new user.

The single test is not the number itself but the **relationship of the collector to the enquiry**.
The very same figures are primary to the body that first collected them and secondary to everyone
who later borrows them. The Census is primary data to the Registrar General who conducts it, and
secondary data to a student who quotes it in a project.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    "Primary data are always more accurate" is a half-truth the examiner exploits. Primary data are
    fitted to the enquiry and their limitations are known to the collector, which is their strength.
    But badly collected primary data are worse than well-collected secondary data. The right caution
    with secondary data is different: before reuse, check <strong>who</strong> collected them,
    <strong>how</strong>, <strong>when</strong>, and whether the definitions match your own — a
    "worker" in one survey may exclude the part-timers your enquiry needs.
  </p>
</Callout>

Data are gathered by several methods — direct personal interview, indirect oral investigation,
mailed questionnaire, schedules sent through enumerators, and information from local correspondents.
Each trades cost against coverage and reliability, and the method is chosen to fit the enquiry, the
budget and the literacy of the respondents.

## 3. The four scales of measurement

Before a single figure is collected, ask what kind of scale the variable sits on. The scale fixes
which arithmetic is meaningful, and the examiner's favourite trap is a calculation the scale does not
permit. Four scales climb from least to most informative.

<div class="formula">Nominal → Ordinal → Interval → Ratio (each level adds one new permitted operation)</div>

- **Nominal scale** — labels only, with no order. Numbers, if used, are mere codes. Jersey numbers,
  PIN codes, a code of 1 for male and 2 for female. You may say two items are the same or different;
  you may **not** say one is greater. The average of two PIN codes is meaningless.
- **Ordinal scale** — labels that carry a **rank**, but with unequal or unknown gaps between ranks.
  Positions in a race (first, second, third), a satisfaction rating (poor, fair, good, excellent),
  merit grades. You may order the items, but the difference between first and second need not equal
  the difference between second and third, so subtraction is not meaningful.
- **Interval scale** — ordered, with **equal and meaningful gaps**, but **no true zero**. The classic
  case is temperature in Celsius or Fahrenheit: 30 °C is as far above 20 °C as 20 °C is above 10 °C,
  so differences are real, but 0 °C does not mean "no temperature", so 40 °C is **not** twice as hot
  as 20 °C. Ratios are meaningless; differences are fine.
- **Ratio scale** — ordered, equal gaps, **and a true, absolute zero** that means "none of the
  quantity". Height, weight, income, age, distance, the number of children. Zero income means no
  income; ₹ 40,000 really is twice ₹ 20,000. Every arithmetic operation is permitted.

| Scale | Order? | Equal intervals? | True zero? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominal | No | No | No | Blood group, PIN code, sex code |
| Ordinal | Yes | No | No | Merit rank, satisfaction rating |
| Interval | Yes | Yes | No | Temperature in °C, calendar year |
| Ratio | Yes | Yes | Yes | Height, weight, income, age |

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The two questions that separate the scales are: <strong>can I rank the values?</strong> (nominal
    says no, the rest say yes) and <strong>is zero a real "nothing"?</strong> (only the ratio scale
    says yes). Temperature in Celsius is the standard exam trap: it looks like a ratio scale but its
    zero is a convention, not an absence, so it is <em>interval</em>. Temperature in Kelvin, whose
    zero is absolute, is a ratio scale — the same quantity, a different scale, because the zero
    changed.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Census, samples and methods of sampling

To learn about a **population** — every item of interest — you have two routes.

- A **census** (complete enumeration) examines **every** unit. It is exhaustive and, in principle,
  most accurate, but it is costly, slow, and impossible where testing destroys the item (you cannot
  test every matchstick by striking it).
- A **sample survey** examines a **part** of the population, chosen to represent the whole, and infers
  the population's features from it. It is cheaper, faster, and often *more* accurate in practice,
  because the smaller task can be done with better-trained staff and closer supervision.

A **sample** is the subset actually studied; the **sampling frame** is the list of all units from
which the sample is drawn. Good sampling is **random** — every unit has a known, non-zero chance of
selection — because only randomness lets you measure the reliability of the result and keeps the
investigator's bias out of the choice.

The examinable methods of random sampling:

- **Simple random sampling** — every unit has an **equal** chance, and every possible sample of the
  chosen size is equally likely. Done by lottery or by random numbers, with replacement or without.
  It is the benchmark against which the others are judged.
- **Systematic sampling** — order the frame, choose one random start within the first interval, then
  take every k-th unit thereafter. The **sampling interval** is the key number:

<div class="formula">Sampling interval k = Population size N ÷ Sample size n</div>

  From 2,400 members choosing 60, k = 2,400 ÷ 60 = 40: pick a random start between 1 and 40, then
  every 40th member. Quick and even, but it fails if the list has a hidden cycle of length k.
- **Stratified sampling** — divide the population into **strata** (groups that are internally
  similar — men and women, urban and rural, the three size-bands of supplier), then sample **within
  each stratum**. Under **proportional allocation** the sample is split in the same proportions as
  the strata:

<div class="formula">Units drawn from a stratum = (Stratum size ÷ Population size) × Total sample size</div>

  It guarantees every group is represented, and it sharpens accuracy when the strata differ from one
  another but are uniform inside.
- **Cluster sampling** — divide the population into **clusters** (usually geographic — villages,
  blocks, schools), pick some clusters at random, and study **every** unit in the chosen clusters. It
  is cheap when the population is spread out, because the fieldwork concentrates in a few places.
- **Multistage sampling** — sampling in stages: pick states, then districts within them, then
  villages within those, then households within those. It is how large national surveys are actually
  run.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Stratified and cluster sampling are constantly confused. In <strong>stratified</strong> sampling
    you sample <em>from every group</em> and the groups are meant to be <em>internally similar</em>;
    in <strong>cluster</strong> sampling you take <em>only some groups</em> but study them
    <em>completely</em>, and the clusters are meant to be <em>internally varied</em>, each a small
    picture of the whole. Same word "group", opposite handling. If the question says "every stratum
    is represented", it is stratified; if it says "a few areas are chosen and fully surveyed", it is
    cluster.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Sampling and non-sampling error

No survey is perfect, and its errors come in two families that behave very differently.

- **Sampling error** arises simply because a sample is a *part*, not the whole: a different sample
  would have given a slightly different answer. It exists **only** in sample surveys, never in a
  complete census. It is not a mistake — it is the price of not measuring everyone — and it
  **shrinks as the sample size grows** and can be estimated from the sample itself.
- **Non-sampling error** arises from **faults in the process** — a wrong measuring instrument, an
  ambiguous question, a lying or forgetful respondent, an enumerator's recording slip, mistakes in
  editing and tabulation. It afflicts **both** censuses and samples, and — this is the sting — it can
  actually be **larger in a census**, because the huge scale of a complete count strains supervision
  and invites more processing mistakes.

| | Sampling error | Non-sampling error |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Studying a part, not the whole | Faults in measurement and processing |
| Present in a census? | No | Yes |
| Present in a sample? | Yes | Yes |
| Effect of a bigger sample | Falls | May rise (more data to mishandle) |
| Can it be estimated? | Yes, from the sample | Only with difficulty |

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The counter-intuitive fact the examiner loves: a well-run <strong>sample</strong> can be more
    accurate overall than a <strong>census</strong>, because although the sample carries sampling
    error the census does not, the census can pile up so much non-sampling error that its total error
    is the larger. "A census has no error" is simply false — it has no <em>sampling</em> error, which
    is not the same thing.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. Classification and tabulation

Once collected, data must be arranged before they can be read. **Classification** is the sorting of
data into homogeneous groups according to a common characteristic; **tabulation** is the orderly
presentation of the classified data in rows and columns.

Classification is done on one of four bases:

- **Chronological** (by time) — production month by month.
- **Geographical** (by place) — sales state by state.
- **Qualitative** (by attribute) — workers by sex or by grade; when only one attribute is used it is
  *simple* classification, when two or more are crossed it is *manifold*.
- **Quantitative** (by the size of a variable) — families by number of children, workers by wage,
  which is exactly the frequency distribution of the next section.

A well-built **table** has a title, a stub (the row headings on the left), a caption (the column
headings across the top), the body of figures, and, where needed, a head-note, foot-note and source
note. Tabulation strips away words and lets the figures speak; a good table needs almost no prose to
be understood.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Classification comes <strong>before</strong> tabulation: you sort the data into groups, then you
    lay the groups out in a table. A frequency distribution is simply a quantitative classification
    presented as a table — which is why the two ideas of this section flow straight into the
    frequency distribution that follows.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. Frequency distributions and choosing the number of classes

A **frequency distribution** shows the values a variable takes alongside the **frequency** — the
number of times each value or band of values occurs. It is the single most important summary in this
chapter, and it comes in two forms.

- A **discrete (ungrouped) frequency distribution** lists each distinct value and its frequency —
  the number of children per family: 0 (occurs 8 times), 1 (occurs 15 times), 2 (occurs 22 times),
  and so on. Use it when the variable takes few distinct values.
- A **continuous (grouped) frequency distribution** groups the values into **classes** (intervals)
  and counts how many observations fall in each — marks 0–10, 10–20, 20–30, with frequencies against
  each. Use it when the variable is continuous or takes many distinct values.

How many classes should a grouped distribution have? Too few and the detail is crushed; too many and
the pattern scatters. The usual guide is **Sturges' rule**:

<div class="formula">Number of classes k = 1 + 3.322 × log₁₀ N, where N is the total number of observations</div>

Round the result to a whole number. For N = 100, k = 1 + 3.322 × 2 = 7.644, so use **8 classes**.
Once the number of classes is fixed, the class width follows from the spread of the data:

<div class="formula">Range = Largest value − Smallest value   ·   Approximate class width = Range ÷ Number of classes</div>

If the marks run from 10 to 90, the range is 80, and with 8 classes the width is 80 ÷ 8 = 10, giving
classes 10–20, 20–30, and so on. The width is usually rounded up to a convenient number so the
classes are easy to read.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Sturges' rule is a <strong>guide, not a law</strong>. It suggests a starting number of classes;
    the final choice rounds to something tidy and may be nudged so the class limits fall on round
    numbers. Do not report "7.644 classes" — a count of classes is a whole number. And compute the
    width from the <em>rounded</em> number of classes, not from the raw decimal, unless the question
    says otherwise.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. Class limits, boundaries, width and mid-value

A grouped distribution hides four quantities inside every class, and Paper 3 tests each by a small
computation. Take the class written 20–29.

- **Class limits** are the values written down: the **lower class limit** (20) and the **upper class
  limit** (29). This "20–29" style is an **inclusive** (discontinuous) distribution — both 20 and 29
  belong to the class, and it is used for **discrete** data where no value lies between 29 and 30.
- **Class boundaries** are the *true* limits, obtained by closing the gaps between one class and the
  next so the classes run continuously. For inclusive classes 20–29, 30–39, …, the gap between 29 and
  30 is 1, so each boundary is shifted by **half the gap**, 0.5:

<div class="formula">Lower boundary = Lower limit − ½(gap)   ·   Upper boundary = Upper limit + ½(gap)</div>

  So 20–29 has boundaries **19.5 to 29.5**. An **exclusive** distribution — written 20–30, 30–40,
  where the upper value belongs to the next class — already has its boundaries equal to its limits.
- **Class width (class size, i)** is the difference between the two **boundaries** of a class:

<div class="formula">Class width = Upper class boundary − Lower class boundary</div>

  For 20–29 (boundaries 19.5–29.5) the true width is 29.5 − 19.5 = **10**, even though the apparent
  spread 29 − 20 is only 9. The width can also be read as the difference between the lower limits of
  two successive classes (30 − 20 = 10).
- **Mid-value (class mark)** is the midpoint of the class, the single value that represents it in
  later averaging:

<div class="formula">Mid-value = (Lower class limit + Upper class limit) ÷ 2 = (Lower boundary + Upper boundary) ÷ 2</div>

  For 20–29 the mid-value is (20 + 29) ÷ 2 = 24.5; for the exclusive class 20–30 it is (20 + 30) ÷ 2
  = 25. The **difference between two successive mid-values equals the class width**, which is a quick
  way to recover the width when only the mid-values are given.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The commonest slip is to treat an <strong>inclusive</strong> class as if its written limits were
    its boundaries. The class 20–29 is 10 units wide, not 9, because its true boundaries are 19.5 and
    29.5. Histograms, mid-values used in averaging, and any width calculation must use the
    <strong>boundaries</strong>, not the raw inclusive limits. When the question shows classes with a
    one-unit gap (20–29, 30–39), reach for the boundaries first.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Cumulative and relative frequency

Two further columns turn a plain frequency distribution into a tool for answering "how many below"
and "what share" questions.

**Cumulative frequency** adds the frequencies up, and comes in two directions.

- A **less-than** cumulative frequency against an upper boundary is the number of observations
  **below** that value: it runs 0 upward to N.
- A **more-than** cumulative frequency against a lower boundary is the number of observations **at or
  above** that value: it runs N downward to 0.

<div class="formula">Frequency of a class = (its less-than cumulative frequency) − (the previous less-than cumulative frequency)</div>

The two cumulative columns always meet: the less-than figure at the top and the more-than figure at
the bottom both equal N, and any single class frequency can be recovered as the difference of two
neighbouring cumulative figures.

**Relative frequency** expresses each frequency as a **share of the total**, which lets you compare
distributions of different sizes.

<div class="formula">Relative frequency = Class frequency ÷ Total frequency (N)   ·   Percentage frequency = Relative frequency × 100</div>

A class of frequency 45 in a total of 300 has relative frequency 45 ÷ 300 = 0.15, that is 15%. The
relative frequencies of all classes always **sum to 1** (the percentages to 100), which is both a
check on your arithmetic and a fast way to find a missing frequency: if three classes carry 0.10,
0.25 and 0.30, the fourth must carry 1 − 0.65 = 0.35.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Turn the total-of-one fact into an examiner-proof check. Whenever a question gives you relative
    frequencies, add them: they must reach exactly 1. A missing relative frequency is found by
    subtraction, and the total frequency N is recovered from any single class by
    <strong>N = class frequency ÷ its relative frequency</strong> — 24 ÷ 0.16 = 150.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. Bar charts and pie charts

A **diagram** presents data to the eye. Two kinds dominate the objective paper.

- A **bar chart** shows the size of each category by the **length of a bar**. The bars are of equal
  width and are separated by gaps, because the categories are distinct — sales by region, output by
  year. Bars carry **one dimension of meaning, their length**; their width and area say nothing. A
  *component* (stacked) bar chart splits each bar into its parts; a *multiple* bar chart sets several
  bars side by side for comparison.
- A **pie chart** (circular diagram) shows how a **whole** divides into parts, by cutting a circle
  into sectors whose **angles are proportional to the components**. The full circle is 360°, so:

<div class="formula">Angle of a sector = (Component value ÷ Total) × 360°   ·   equivalently, each 1% of the total = 3.6°</div>

An item that is 35% of the total takes 35 × 3.6 = **126°**; a component of ₹ 1,800 out of ₹ 7,200
takes (1,800 ÷ 7,200) × 360 = **90°**. The reverse is just as common: a sector of 45° representing
₹ 5,000 implies a total of 5,000 × (360 ÷ 45) = ₹ 40,000, and a 60° sector of a ₹ 36,000 budget is
(60 ÷ 360) × 36,000 = ₹ 6,000.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The pie-chart trap is mixing up the three currencies — rupees, per cent and degrees. Convert
    through the total. A share in <strong>rupees</strong> becomes a fraction of the total, times 360
    for <strong>degrees</strong> or times 100 for <strong>per cent</strong>. The bridge worth
    memorising is <strong>1% = 3.6°</strong> and its reverse <strong>1° = (1 ÷ 360) of the total</strong>.
    Check your sectors by adding the angles: they must total 360°.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 11. Histogram, frequency polygon and ogive

A **graph** presents a frequency distribution on a pair of axes. Three are examinable, and each
answers a different question.

- A **histogram** is a set of **adjacent** rectangles, one per class, with **no gaps** (unlike a bar
  chart, because the classes are continuous). The class boundaries mark the base of each rectangle,
  and — this is the point students miss — it is the **area**, not the height, that represents the
  frequency. When all classes are the same width, height alone is a fair guide. When class widths
  **differ**, you must plot the **frequency density** so that area stays proportional to frequency:

<div class="formula">Frequency density = Class frequency ÷ Class width</div>

  A class 30–50 (width 20) with frequency 60 is plotted at a height of 60 ÷ 20 = 3, not at 60, so
  that a double-width class does not tower falsely over its neighbours.
- A **frequency polygon** joins the **mid-value, frequency** points of the classes with straight
  lines, and closes the figure to the axis at a mid-value one class below the first and one above the
  last. It is the histogram's outline, and it lets two distributions be overlaid for comparison.
- An **ogive (cumulative frequency curve)** plots **cumulative frequency against the class
  boundary**. The **less-than** ogive rises from left to right (from 0 up to N); the **more-than**
  ogive falls. Their most useful trick is reading the **median** straight off the curve: the median
  is the value below which half the observations lie, so:

<div class="formula">Median position = N ÷ 2 (mark this on the cumulative axis, read across to the curve, then down to the value)</div>

  With N = 80, mark 40 on the cumulative-frequency axis, run across to the less-than ogive and down
  to the horizontal axis; that reading is the median. The two ogives, drawn together, cross at
  exactly that point, which is a neat way to find the median without a formula. Where the reading is
  taken between plotted points, linear interpolation inside the median class gives the same figure a
  formula would:

<div class="formula">Median = L + [(N ÷ 2 − cf) ÷ f] × h, where L is the lower boundary of the median class, cf the cumulative frequency before it, f its frequency, and h its width</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Keep the three graphs sorted by what they plot. A <strong>histogram</strong> plots frequency
    (as area) against class boundaries; a <strong>frequency polygon</strong> plots frequency against
    <em>mid-values</em>; an <strong>ogive</strong> plots <em>cumulative</em> frequency against class
    <em>boundaries</em>. Only the ogive uses cumulative frequency, and only the ogive reads off the
    median. A "frequency curve" is a smoothed frequency polygon; an "ogive" is never smoothed away
    from its cumulative meaning.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 12. Answering under negative marking

Paper 3 is entirely objective. A correct answer earns 1 mark, a wrong answer costs 0.25 mark, and a
blank scores zero. That arithmetic should shape how you treat a definition you are unsure of.

Guess blindly among four options and you expect (1 ÷ 4)(+1) + (3 ÷ 4)(−0.25) = 0.25 − 0.1875 =
**+0.0625** marks — barely positive, almost worthless. Eliminate one option and it rises to
(1 ÷ 3)(+1) + (2 ÷ 3)(−0.25) = **+0.1667**; eliminate two and it reaches **+0.375**. The value of a
guess lives almost entirely in the options you can rule out.

In this chapter you can often rule out two options without any real work. A "number of classes" that
is not a whole number is wrong. A relative frequency above 1, or a set of relative frequencies that
do not sum to 1, is wrong. A pie-chart angle above 360°, or a set of sector angles that do not add
to 360°, is wrong. A class width read from inclusive limits without closing the gap is the classic
distractor, so the option that ignores the 0.5 shift is usually the trap, not the answer. Eliminate
on these grounds first, then choose. Leave a question blank only when nothing can be ruled out and
the time is better spent elsewhere.

## Fully worked examples

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — naming the scale of measurement (easy)">
  <p>
    For each variable, name the scale: (a) the jersey numbers of a cricket team, (b) the finishing
    positions in a marathon, (c) the temperature of five cities in °C, (d) the monthly incomes of
    five families.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Apply the two tests to each</div>
    Can the values be ranked? Is zero a true "nothing"?<br />
    (a) Jersey numbers are labels; number 7 is not "more" than number 4 — no order, so
    <strong>nominal</strong>.<br />
    (b) Positions can be ranked but the gaps are unequal (the winner may finish far ahead of the
    second) — order without equal intervals, so <strong>ordinal</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — The two harder ones</div>
    (c) Temperature in °C has equal intervals but no true zero (0 °C is not "no heat"), so
    <strong>interval</strong>; 40 °C is not twice 20 °C.<br />
    (d) Income has equal intervals and a true zero (₹ 0 is genuinely no income), and ₹ 40,000 really
    is twice ₹ 20,000, so <strong>ratio</strong>.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — choosing and sizing a sample (moderate)">
  <p>
    A firm has 2,400 employees: 1,440 in the factory and 960 in the office. The audit team wants a
    sample of 100. (a) If it uses systematic sampling on the full staff list, what is the sampling
    interval? (b) If it uses stratified sampling with proportional allocation, how many should it
    draw from each group?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) Systematic interval</div>
    k = N ÷ n = 2,400 ÷ 100 = <strong>24</strong><br />
    Pick a random start between 1 and 24, then take every 24th name on the list.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) Proportional allocation</div>
    Factory: (1,440 ÷ 2,400) × 100 = <strong>60</strong><br />
    Office: (960 ÷ 2,400) × 100 = <strong>40</strong><br />
    The two add back to 100, and each group is represented in proportion to its size — the point of
    stratifying.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — class limits, boundaries, width and mid-value (moderate)">
  <p>
    A distribution of daily wages is written with the inclusive classes 200–249, 250–299, 300–349,
    … For the class 250–299, find the class boundaries, the class width and the mid-value.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Close the gap for the boundaries</div>
    The gap between 249 and 250 is 1, so shift each limit by ½ × 1 = 0.5.<br />
    Lower boundary = 250 − 0.5 = <strong>249.5</strong>; upper boundary = 299 + 0.5 =
    <strong>299.5</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Width and mid-value</div>
    Width = 299.5 − 249.5 = <strong>50</strong> (not 299 − 250 = 49; the inclusive limits understate
    it).<br />
    Mid-value = (250 + 299) ÷ 2 = 274.5, equivalently (249.5 + 299.5) ÷ 2 = <strong>274.5</strong> —
    limits or boundaries give the same midpoint.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — Sturges' rule and the class width (moderate)">
  <p>
    A statistician has 100 observations ranging from a lowest of 12 to a highest of 92. Using
    Sturges' rule, how many classes should she use, and what class width does that imply? Take
    log₁₀ 100 = 2.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Number of classes</div>
    k = 1 + 3.322 × log₁₀ 100 = 1 + 3.322 × 2 = 1 + 6.644 = 7.644<br />
    A count of classes is a whole number, so round to <strong>8 classes</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Class width</div>
    Range = 92 − 12 = 80<br />
    Width = Range ÷ number of classes = 80 ÷ 8 = <strong>10</strong><br />
    Convenient classes would then be 10–20, 20–30, …, 90–100, which comfortably cover 12 to 92.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — building cumulative and relative frequency (moderate)">
  <p>
    The marks of 50 students fall in the classes 0–10, 10–20, 20–30, 30–40 with frequencies 6, 14,
    20 and 10. (a) Find the less-than cumulative frequency at each upper boundary. (b) What relative
    frequency and percentage does the class 20–30 carry? (c) How many students scored less than 30?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) Cumulate the frequencies upward</div>
    Less than 10: 6<br />
    Less than 20: 6 + 14 = 20<br />
    Less than 30: 20 + 20 = 40<br />
    Less than 40: 40 + 10 = <strong>50</strong> = N, the check that the column is complete.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) Relative and percentage frequency of 20–30</div>
    Relative frequency = 20 ÷ 50 = <strong>0.40</strong><br />
    Percentage = 0.40 × 100 = <strong>40%</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (c) Students scoring less than 30</div>
    Read the less-than-30 cumulative figure directly: <strong>40 students</strong>. The remaining
    50 − 40 = 10 scored 30 or more.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 6 — a pie chart of a household budget (moderate)">
  <p>
    A family's monthly spending of ₹ 36,000 splits as food ₹ 14,400, rent ₹ 9,000, education
    ₹ 5,400 and other ₹ 7,200. Find the sector angle for each, and confirm the angles close the
    circle.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Angle = (component ÷ total) × 360</div>
    Food: (14,400 ÷ 36,000) × 360 = 0.40 × 360 = <strong>144°</strong><br />
    Rent: (9,000 ÷ 36,000) × 360 = 0.25 × 360 = <strong>90°</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — The remaining two sectors</div>
    Education: (5,400 ÷ 36,000) × 360 = 0.15 × 360 = <strong>54°</strong><br />
    Other: (7,200 ÷ 36,000) × 360 = 0.20 × 360 = <strong>72°</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — The check</div>
    144 + 90 + 54 + 72 = <strong>360°</strong>. The sectors close the circle exactly, which proves
    the shares were handled correctly. Equivalently, the four percentages 40 + 25 + 15 + 20 = 100.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 7 — a histogram with unequal class widths (hard)">
  <p>
    A distribution has classes 0–10, 10–30 and 30–60 with frequencies 20, 60 and 45. Why can the raw
    frequencies not be used as bar heights, and what heights should the histogram use?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — The widths differ</div>
    Widths: 0–10 is 10 wide, 10–30 is 20 wide, 30–60 is 30 wide. A histogram represents frequency by
    <strong>area</strong>, so plotting raw frequencies as heights would let the wide classes claim
    far more area than their frequency deserves.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Use frequency density = frequency ÷ width</div>
    0–10: 20 ÷ 10 = <strong>2.0</strong><br />
    10–30: 60 ÷ 20 = <strong>3.0</strong><br />
    30–60: 45 ÷ 30 = <strong>1.5</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Read the result</div>
    On density, the middle class is the tallest at 3.0, and the last class — despite its large raw
    frequency of 45 — is the shortest at 1.5, because it is spread over the widest interval. Area
    (density × width) recovers each original frequency: 2×10 = 20, 3×20 = 60, 1.5×30 = 45.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 8 — reading the median off an ogive (hard)">
  <p>
    The marks of 50 students are grouped as 0–10, 10–20, 20–30, 30–40 with frequencies 6, 14, 20 and
    10. Find the median from the cumulative frequency, and confirm it with the interpolation formula.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Locate the median position</div>
    N ÷ 2 = 50 ÷ 2 = <strong>25</strong>. On a less-than ogive this is the cumulative frequency to
    read across from.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Find the median class</div>
    Less-than cumulative: &lt;10 = 6, &lt;20 = 20, &lt;30 = 40. The value 25 first passes into the
    class <strong>20–30</strong>, so that is the median class: L = 20, cf before it = 20, f = 20,
    h = 10.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Interpolate</div>
    Median = L + [(N ÷ 2 − cf) ÷ f] × h = 20 + [(25 − 20) ÷ 20] × 10 = 20 + 2.5 =
    <strong>22.5 marks</strong><br />
    On the graph, the two ogives (less-than rising, more-than falling) cross at exactly the marks-axis
    value 22.5, which is the same median read without any formula.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 9 — recovering a frequency table from partial data (hard)">
  <p>
    A four-class distribution of 200 items gives the relative frequencies 0.10, 0.25 and 0.30 for the
    first three classes, and a less-than cumulative frequency of 130 at the top of the third class.
    (a) Find the relative frequency of the fourth class. (b) Find the frequency of the third class.
    (c) Find the frequency of the fourth class.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) Relative frequencies sum to 1</div>
    Fourth class relative frequency = 1 − (0.10 + 0.25 + 0.30) = 1 − 0.65 = <strong>0.35</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) Third-class frequency from its relative frequency</div>
    Frequency = relative frequency × N = 0.30 × 200 = <strong>60</strong><br />
    As a cross-check, the first two classes carry 0.10 × 200 = 20 and 0.25 × 200 = 50, so the
    less-than cumulative at the top of the third class is 20 + 50 + 60 = 130, matching the data.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (c) Fourth-class frequency</div>
    Either 0.35 × 200 = 70, or N minus the cumulative-so-far: 200 − 130 = <strong>70</strong>. The two
    routes agree, which confirms the whole table.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes and examiner traps

- **Calling Celsius temperature a ratio scale.** Its zero is a convention, not an absence of heat,
  so it is an interval scale; 40 °C is not twice 20 °C. Only a scale with a true zero (income,
  height, weight, Kelvin) is a ratio scale.
- **Confusing primary and secondary data by the figure rather than the collector.** The same data
  are primary to the body that first collected them and secondary to everyone who reuses them.
- **Swapping stratified and cluster sampling.** Stratified samples *every* group (internally
  similar); cluster takes *some* groups and studies them *fully* (internally varied).
- **Claiming a census has no error.** A census has no *sampling* error, but it can carry heavy
  *non-sampling* error, sometimes more than a well-run sample.
- **Reporting a fractional number of classes.** Sturges' rule gives a starting figure that is
  rounded to a whole number; classes are counted, not measured.
- **Treating inclusive class limits as boundaries.** The class 20–29 is 10 units wide, not 9,
  because its true boundaries are 19.5 and 29.5. Close the half-unit gap before finding width or
  drawing a histogram.
- **Using raw frequencies as histogram heights when class widths differ.** Plot frequency density
  (frequency ÷ width) so that area, not height, carries the frequency.
- **Mixing rupees, per cent and degrees in a pie chart.** Convert through the total: 1% = 3.6°, and
  the sector angles must sum to 360°.
- **Forgetting that relative frequencies sum to 1.** Use it to find a missing frequency and to check
  the whole table; N = a class frequency ÷ its relative frequency.
- **Reading the median off a histogram or a frequency polygon.** Only the ogive plots cumulative
  frequency, and only the ogive reads off the median at N ÷ 2.

## One-page revision summary

**Vocabulary.** Statistics (singular) is the science of collecting, organising, presenting,
analysing and interpreting data; (plural) the figures themselves. A *variable* is measured, an
*attribute* is counted; variables are *discrete* (countable) or *continuous* (any value in a range).

**Data and scales**

<div class="formula">Primary data — collected first-hand for this enquiry · Secondary data — reused from another source. The test is the collector's relation to the enquiry, not the figure.</div>
<div class="formula">Scales: Nominal (labels) → Ordinal (rank) → Interval (equal gaps, no true zero, e.g. °C) → Ratio (true zero, e.g. income). Rankable from ordinal up; ratios valid only on a ratio scale.</div>

**Sampling**

<div class="formula">Census = every unit (no sampling error). Sample = a representative part (has sampling error, usually cheaper and often more accurate).</div>
<div class="formula">Systematic interval k = N ÷ n · Stratified (proportional): from a stratum = (stratum size ÷ N) × sample size.</div>
<div class="formula">Cluster = some groups studied fully; Stratified = every group sampled. Multistage = sampling in stages.</div>
<div class="formula">Sampling error: sample only, falls with size, estimable. Non-sampling error: census and sample, from process faults.</div>

**Frequency distribution**

<div class="formula">Sturges: number of classes k = 1 + 3.322 log₁₀ N (round to a whole number) · Width ≈ Range ÷ k · Range = max − min</div>
<div class="formula">Inclusive class boundaries: lower − ½(gap), upper + ½(gap) · Class width = upper boundary − lower boundary</div>
<div class="formula">Mid-value = (lower limit + upper limit) ÷ 2 · Successive mid-values differ by the class width</div>
<div class="formula">Class frequency = difference of two neighbouring less-than cumulative frequencies</div>
<div class="formula">Relative frequency = f ÷ N (they sum to 1) · Percentage frequency = relative frequency × 100 · N = f ÷ relative frequency</div>

**Diagrams and graphs**

<div class="formula">Pie sector angle = (component ÷ total) × 360° · 1% = 3.6° · sectors sum to 360°</div>
<div class="formula">Bar chart: length carries meaning, gaps between bars · Histogram: adjacent rectangles, area carries frequency</div>
<div class="formula">Frequency density = frequency ÷ class width (needed when widths differ)</div>
<div class="formula">Histogram → boundaries; Frequency polygon → mid-values; Ogive → cumulative frequency vs boundaries</div>
<div class="formula">Median off the ogive at N ÷ 2 · Median = L + [(N ÷ 2 − cf) ÷ f] × h</div>

**Directions to remember.** Only the ratio scale allows ratios · a bigger sample lowers sampling
error but may raise non-sampling error · an inclusive class is one whole unit wider than its written
limits suggest · relative frequencies always total 1 and pie angles always total 360° · only the
ogive yields the median.
