Sahyadri Foods, a small processor in Nashik, closes its books and three questions land on the
same desk. The two working partners must split a profit of ₹ 14,70,000 in the ratio 4 : 3. The
plant runs 12 hours a day with 15 workers; if 6 workers leave, how many hours must the rest work
to move the same output? And the turnover, which stood at ₹ 40,00,000 five years ago, is now
₹ 1,60,00,000 — the bank wants the compound annual growth rate before it renews the limit.

Three questions, three tools. The first is **ratio**, the second is **inverse variation**, and the
third cannot be answered at all without **indices** and **logarithms**: you need the fifth root of
4, and the quickest honest route to it is a logarithm. This chapter builds all three tools in
order, because the paper builds on them in the same order — mathematics of finance, sequences and
statistics all reuse what you learn here.

Paper 3 is fully objective. You will not be asked to show a proof; you will be asked for a number
or an option, under time pressure, with 0.25 marks deducted for a wrong answer. That changes what
"knowing the chapter" means. It is not enough to reach the right answer slowly — you also have to
recognise which wrong answer each careless step produces, so that a familiar-looking option does
not pull you in. Every worked example below names those wrong answers.

## 1. What a ratio is, and what it is not

A **ratio** compares two quantities **of the same kind** by division. If Sahyadri's two partners
put in ₹ 8,00,000 and ₹ 6,00,000, their capitals are in the ratio 8,00,000 : 6,00,000, which
simplifies to **4 : 3**.

Write it as `a : b`, read "a is to b". The first term `a` is the **antecedent**, the second term
`b` is the **consequent**. The ratio itself is the fraction a/b — a pure number with **no unit**.

Four things follow, and each of them is examined:

- **The quantities must be of the same kind**, measured in the same unit. ₹ 500 to 2 kg is not a
  ratio. ₹ 500 to ₹ 2,000 is, and so is 500 g to 2 kg **after** you convert: 500 g : 2,000 g = 1 : 4.
- **A ratio has no unit.** 4 : 3 is 4 : 3 whether the capitals were in rupees or in lakhs.
- **The consequent can never be zero**, because you cannot divide by zero.
- **Order matters.** 4 : 3 and 3 : 4 are different statements. Swapping them is the single most
  common way to lose a mark in this chapter.

A ratio is in its **lowest terms** (or simplest form) when the two terms have no common factor
other than 1. Divide both terms by their HCF: 84 : 126 has HCF 42, so it is 2 : 3.

Three forms of the same statement will appear on the paper, and you should read all three the
same way. "The capitals are in the ratio 4 : 3." "The capitals are as 4 is to 3." "The capitals
are 4x and 3x." The third is the most useful, because the multiplier x is what you actually solve
for: once you know one part is ₹ 2,10,000, every share follows by multiplication.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Watch the wording. "The ratio of A to B is 4 : 3" fixes the <em>order</em> — A is 4 parts and B
    is 3 parts. "A and B are in the ratio 4 : 3" says the same thing. But "A is divided in the ratio
    4 : 3" tells you only how a <strong>total</strong> is split, and "A is 4 : 3 <em>of</em> B" is
    not standard phrasing at all — if you meet it, read the sentence around it before assuming.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. The rules for handling and comparing ratios

Two rules do almost all the work.

<div class="formula">a : b = ka : kb for any non-zero k — multiplying or dividing both terms by the same non-zero number leaves the ratio unchanged</div>

<div class="formula">a : b and c : d are equal when ad = bc (cross multiplication)</div>

**Adding the same number to both terms does not preserve a ratio.** 2 : 3 is not 3 : 4. This is
worth stating flatly because so many wrong options are built on it.

To **compare** two ratios, convert both to fractions with a common denominator, or simply
cross-multiply. Is 5 : 8 bigger or smaller than 7 : 11? Cross-multiply: 5 × 11 = 55 and 8 × 7 = 56.
Since 55 is less than 56, **5 : 8 is the smaller ratio**. Equivalently, 0.625 against 0.6363...

To compare **three or more** ratios, express them all with the same consequent (LCM of the
consequents) and read off the antecedents.

Three names the examiner uses without explaining them:

- a ratio of **greater inequality** — the antecedent exceeds the consequent (7 : 5);
- a ratio of **lesser inequality** — the antecedent is smaller (5 : 7);
- a ratio of **equality** — the two terms are equal (5 : 5, that is 1 : 1).

Add the same positive number to both terms and the ratio always moves **towards 1 : 1**. A ratio of
greater inequality is reduced; a ratio of lesser inequality is increased. Add 3 to both terms of
7 : 5 and you get 10 : 8 = 1.25, down from 1.4. This is why "add 3 to both terms" is never a way of
simplifying — it changes the ratio.

## 3. Inverse, compound, duplicate and triplicate ratios

Five derived ratios carry names, and the paper uses the names rather than the formulas.

| Name | Formed from a : b | Example, on 4 : 9 |
|---|---|---|
| **Inverse (reciprocal) ratio** | b : a, that is 1/a : 1/b | 9 : 4 |
| **Compound ratio** of a : b and c : d | ac : bd | 4 : 9 with 3 : 2 gives 12 : 18 = 2 : 3 |
| **Duplicate ratio** | a² : b² | 16 : 81 |
| **Sub-duplicate ratio** | √a : √b | 2 : 3 |
| **Triplicate ratio** | a³ : b³ | 64 : 729 |
| **Sub-triplicate ratio** | ∛a : ∛b | on 8 : 27 it is 2 : 3 |

<div class="formula">Compound ratio of a : b and c : d = ac : bd — multiply antecedents together and consequents together</div>

The duplicate ratio is just the compound of a ratio **with itself**, and the triplicate ratio is
the compound of a ratio with itself twice over. That is worth remembering, because it tells you
which way the "sub-" versions run: sub-duplicate takes the square **root**, not the square.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Duplicate does not mean "double".</strong> The duplicate ratio of 4 : 9 is 16 : 81, not
    8 : 18. And 8 : 18 simplifies straight back to 4 : 9, which is exactly why that distractor looks
    plausible: doubling both terms changes nothing at all. If an option equals the original ratio,
    it cannot be the duplicate of it (unless the ratio is 1 : 1).
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Continued ratio: joining ratios that share a term

A **continued ratio** compares three or more quantities at once: a : b : c. You are usually given
two separate ratios that overlap in one quantity, and asked to join them.

If A : B = 3 : 4 and B : C = 6 : 7, you cannot simply write 3 : 4 : 7 — B is 4 in the first
statement and 6 in the second. Make B the same in both. The LCM of 4 and 6 is 12:

- multiply A : B = 3 : 4 by 3, giving 9 : 12;
- multiply B : C = 6 : 7 by 2, giving 12 : 14;
- therefore **A : B : C = 9 : 12 : 14**.

<div class="formula">If A : B = a : b and B : C = c : d, then A : B : C = ac : bc : bd</div>

That shortcut is the same operation in one line: multiply the first ratio through by c and the
second by b. On the numbers above, ac : bc : bd = 3×6 : 4×6 : 4×7 = 18 : 24 : 28 = 9 : 12 : 14.

The reverse job also appears: given A : B : C = 9 : 12 : 14, read off any pair by ignoring the
third term. A : C = 9 : 14.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    When three ratios chain — A : B, B : C and C : D — do them two at a time, or scale all three so
    that the shared terms agree. Never write the four antecedents down in a row and hope; the
    arithmetic takes about fifteen seconds, and a wrong answer costs 0.25 on top of the mark you
    did not earn.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Dividing an amount in a given ratio

This is the most examined single skill in the ratio half of the chapter, and it is mechanical.

<div class="formula">To divide an amount T in the ratio a : b : c — total parts = a + b + c; each share = T × (its own part ÷ total parts)</div>

Sahyadri's profit of ₹ 14,70,000 in the ratio 4 : 3. Total parts 7, so one part is
₹ 14,70,000 ÷ 7 = ₹ 2,10,000. The shares are 4 × ₹ 2,10,000 = **₹ 8,40,000** and
3 × ₹ 2,10,000 = **₹ 6,30,000**. They add back to ₹ 14,70,000, which is the check to run every
time.

Two variants appear constantly.

**"The difference between the shares is given."** If A and B share in 7 : 4 and A gets ₹ 66,000
more than B, then the difference is 7 − 4 = 3 parts, so one part is ₹ 22,000 and the total is
11 parts = **₹ 2,42,000**.

**"One share is given."** If the ratio is 5 : 3 : 2 and the middle share is ₹ 96,000, then 3 parts
= ₹ 96,000, one part = ₹ 32,000, total = 10 parts = **₹ 3,20,000**.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Reading "A gets ₹ 66,000 more than B" as "A's share is ₹ 66,000" is the classic slip, and the
    examiner always puts <em>both</em> answers on the page. Before you divide, ask whether the
    number you were given is a <strong>share</strong>, a <strong>difference</strong> or the
    <strong>total</strong> — three different denominators (7 parts, 3 parts, 11 parts) come out of
    the same sentence.
  </p>
</Callout>

**A third variant: the ratio itself changes.** "Two numbers are in the ratio 5 : 7. If 12 is added
to each, the ratio becomes 3 : 4. Find the numbers." Here you cannot work in parts alone, because
the added 12 is not a number of parts. Introduce the multiplier x, write the two numbers as 5x and
7x, and translate the second sentence into an equation:

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">The standard set-up</div>
  (5x + 12) ÷ (7x + 12) = 3 ÷ 4<br />
  Cross-multiply: 4(5x + 12) = 3(7x + 12), so 20x + 48 = 21x + 36<br />
  Therefore x = 12, and the numbers are 5 × 12 = <strong>60</strong> and 7 × 12 =
  <strong>84</strong><br />
  Check: (60 + 12) : (84 + 12) = 72 : 96 = 3 : 4 ✓
</div>

The same set-up handles the salary and capital versions of the question — "if each partner brings in
a further ₹ 40,000 the capitals become 3 : 4" — and it is the reason §2 insists that adding a
constant to both terms changes a ratio. If it did not, there would be nothing to solve.

## 6. Proportion, continued proportion and the mean proportional

Four quantities a, b, c, d are in **proportion** when a : b = c : d. Write it a : b :: c : d, read
"a is to b as c is to d".

<div class="formula">a : b :: c : d means a/b = c/d, and therefore ad = bc — the product of the extremes equals the product of the means</div>

Here a and d are the **extremes**, b and c are the **means**. The fourth quantity d is called the
**fourth proportional** to a, b and c: d = bc/a.

**Continued proportion.** Three quantities a, b, c are in continued proportion when
a : b = b : c — the same middle term serves twice. Cross-multiplying gives the result you will use
again and again:

<div class="formula">a, b, c in continued proportion ⇔ b² = ac</div>

Then **b is the mean proportional** (or geometric mean) between a and c, so b = √(ac), and **c is
the third proportional** to a and b, so c = b²/a.

Three names, three formulas, and students mix them up under time pressure. Fix them by counting
how many quantities you were given:

| You are given | You are asked for | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| a, b, c (three) | fourth proportional | d = bc/a |
| a, b (two) | third proportional | c = b²/a |
| a, c (two) | mean proportional | b = √(ac) |

The mean proportional between 4 and 25 is √100 = **10**. The third proportional to 4 and 10 is
100/4 = **25**. The fourth proportional to 4, 10 and 12 is (10 × 12)/4 = **30**.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A continued proportion extends: a, b, c, d are in continued proportion when
    a : b = b : c = c : d. Every consecutive pair has the same ratio, so the quantities form a
    <strong>geometric progression</strong> — the link that Ch 6 picks up. If a = 3 and the common
    ratio is 2, the four terms are 3, 6, 12, 24.
  </p>
</Callout>

**The k-method.** Because three quantities in continued proportion form a geometric progression,
they can be written as **a/r, a, ar** — one unknown for the size and one for the ratio. That single
substitution solves most continued-proportion word problems in three lines.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Three numbers in continued proportion, sum 21 and product 216</div>
  Write them as a/r, a, ar.<br />
  Product = (a/r) × a × (ar) = a³ = 216, so a = <strong>6</strong><br />
  Sum: 6/r + 6 + 6r = 21, so 6/r + 6r = 15, so 6 + 6r² = 15r<br />
  2r² − 5r + 2 = 0, giving r = 2 or r = 1/2<br />
  Either way the numbers are <strong>3, 6 and 12</strong> — r = 1/2 simply lists them in reverse.<br />
  Check: 6² = 3 × 12 ✓ and 3 + 6 + 12 = 21 ✓
</div>

Writing the three unknowns as a, b, c instead needs two equations and a substitution; writing them
as a/r, a, ar puts the whole of the continued-proportion condition into the notation, so the
condition never has to be used again.

## 7. The five operations on a proportion

Start from a/b = c/d. Five standard rearrangements have Latin names, and the paper asks for the
name as often as it asks for the algebra.

| Operation | From a/b = c/d you may write | What was done |
|---|---|---|
| **Invertendo** | b/a = d/c | Turn both sides upside down |
| **Alternendo** | a/c = b/d | Swap the two means |
| **Componendo** | (a + b)/b = (c + d)/d | Add 1 to both sides |
| **Dividendo** | (a − b)/b = (c − d)/d | Subtract 1 from both sides |
| **Componendo et dividendo** | (a + b)/(a − b) = (c + d)/(c − d) | Divide componendo by dividendo |

Two more, less often named but freely used:

- **Addendo** — if a/b = c/d = e/f = k, then (a + c + e)/(b + d + f) = k as well. Any sum of
  antecedents over the matching sum of consequents keeps the same ratio.
- **Subtrahendo** — the same with differences: (a − c)/(b − d) = k.

**Componendo et dividendo is the one that saves real time.** Whenever an equation has the shape
(x + p)/(x − p) = m/n, do not cross-multiply and grind; apply the rule in reverse and read x off.

Take (x + 5)/(x − 5) = 9/4. Reading it as componendo-et-dividendo applied to x/5 = ?, the operation
undoes to x/5 = (9 + 4)/(9 − 4) = 13/5, so **x = 13**. Check: 18/8 = 9/4. Correct.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Componendo adds the <strong>consequent</strong> to the antecedent and leaves the consequent as
    the denominator: (a + b)/b, not (a + b)/a. Getting the denominator wrong turns a two-line
    question into an answer that is nowhere on the option list — or worse, onto the one distractor
    built from exactly that slip.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. Direct, inverse and joint variation

**Direct variation.** y varies directly as x, written y ∝ x, when y = kx for some constant k. Double
x and y doubles. The graph is a straight line through the origin, and the giveaway in a question is
that **y ÷ x stays constant**.

<div class="formula">y ∝ x ⇒ y = kx ⇒ y₁/x₁ = y₂/x₂</div>

**Inverse variation.** y varies inversely as x, written y ∝ 1/x, when xy = k. Double x and y halves.
The giveaway is that **the product xy stays constant**.

<div class="formula">y ∝ 1/x ⇒ y = k/x ⇒ x₁y₁ = x₂y₂</div>

Sahyadri's plant is the standard example. 15 workers work 12 hours to move a fixed output, so the
worker-hours needed are 15 × 12 = 180. With 6 workers gone, 9 remain, and 180 ÷ 9 = **20 hours**.
The answer 7.2 hours — from treating the relation as direct, 12 × 9/15 — is always on the option
list.

**Joint variation.** y varies jointly as x and z when y = kxz. Mixed statements are common: "y
varies directly as x and inversely as the square of z" means y = kx/z².

**Partly constant, partly varying.** A cost that has a fixed component and a variable component is
written y = a + bx, and two sets of readings are enough to find both constants. This is the shape
every cost question takes, and it is not a ratio at all — treating it as one is the error the
question is testing.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Fixed plus variable, from two observations</div>
  Total cost is partly constant and partly varying with output: ₹ 2,60,000 for 4,000 units and
  ₹ 3,80,000 for 7,000 units. Find the cost of 10,000 units.<br />
  2,60,000 = a + 4,000b and 3,80,000 = a + 7,000b<br />
  Subtract: 1,20,000 = 3,000b, so b = <strong>₹ 40 per unit</strong><br />
  a = 2,60,000 − 4,000 × 40 = <strong>₹ 1,00,000 fixed</strong><br />
  Cost of 10,000 units = 1,00,000 + 10,000 × 40 = <strong>₹ 5,00,000</strong><br />
  Scaling the first figure in proportion instead — 2,60,000 × 10,000/4,000 = ₹ 6,50,000 — is the
  distractor, and it is wrong because the fixed ₹ 1,00,000 would be counted two and a half times.
</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Two habits make variation questions quick. First, decide the direction in words before touching
    numbers: <em>more workers, fewer hours</em> is inverse; <em>more workers, more output</em> is
    direct. Second, find k from the first set of data, then substitute the second — it is slower to
    write than a ratio shortcut, but it never inverts by accident.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Indices: the meaning and the laws

In the expression aⁿ, a is the **base** and n is the **index** (or exponent, or power). For a
positive whole number n it means a multiplied by itself n times: 2⁵ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32.

Five laws carry the whole topic. In each of them the bases must be **the same** before the law can
be used, and a is taken as a positive real number.

<div class="formula">1. aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ  ·  2. aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ  ·  3. (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ  ·  4. (ab)ⁿ = aⁿbⁿ  ·  5. (a/b)ⁿ = aⁿ/bⁿ</div>

Read the first two the way they are written. **Multiplying adds the indices; dividing subtracts
them.** Nothing is done to the base. 3⁴ × 3² = 3⁶ = 729, not 9⁶.

Law 3 is the one to keep separate from law 1. (a³)² = a⁶ because you have three a's, twice over.
But a³ × a² = a⁵. Same symbols, different operation: **power of a power multiplies, product of
powers adds**.

Laws 4 and 5 spread an index across a product or a quotient. They do **not** spread across a sum.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>(a + b)ⁿ is not aⁿ + bⁿ.</strong> Test it once with numbers and you will never do it
    again: (3 + 4)² = 49, while 3² + 4² = 25. The same warning applies to roots —
    √(9 + 16) = 5, not 3 + 4 = 7. Every "distribute the power over a sum" step is wrong, and an
    option built from it appears in most exponent questions.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. Zero, negative and fractional exponents

The laws above were built for whole numbers, and then extended so that they keep working. That is
where the three definitions come from — they are not arbitrary.

**Zero exponent.** By law 2, aⁿ ÷ aⁿ = aⁿ⁻ⁿ = a⁰. But anything divided by itself is 1.

<div class="formula">a⁰ = 1 for every a ≠ 0 (0⁰ is not defined)</div>

So (7,45,000)⁰ = 1, and (−13)⁰ = 1, and (2/3)⁰ = 1. The size of the base is irrelevant.

**Negative exponent.** By law 2 again, a⁰ ÷ aⁿ = a⁻ⁿ, and a⁰ = 1.

<div class="formula">a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ, and 1/a⁻ⁿ = aⁿ; therefore (a/b)⁻ⁿ = (b/a)ⁿ</div>

A negative index means **reciprocal**, never a negative number. 2⁻³ = 1/8, which is positive and
small. (2/5)⁻² = (5/2)² = 25/4.

**Fractional exponent.** By law 3, (a^(1/n))ⁿ = a¹ = a, so a^(1/n) must be the nth root of a.

<div class="formula">a^(1/n) = ⁿ√a  and  a^(m/n) = ⁿ√(aᵐ) = (ⁿ√a)ᵐ</div>

The denominator of the fraction is the **root**; the numerator is the **power**. 8^(2/3) = the cube
root of 8, squared = 2² = **4**. Taking the root first keeps the numbers small — 8² = 64 and then
the cube root of 64 is also 4, but on 32^(3/5) the difference between rooting first and powering
first is the difference between 2³ = 8 and the fifth root of 32,768.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Three slips, in order of frequency. <strong>(i)</strong> Reading 2⁻³ as −8 instead of 1/8.
    <strong>(ii)</strong> Reading 8^(2/3) as 8 × 2/3, or as the square root of 8 cubed — the
    denominator is the root. <strong>(iii)</strong> Writing a⁰ = 0. Each of these has its own
    distractor sitting on the option list, which is why a wrong answer here feels as convincing as
    a right one.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 11. Surds written as indices

A **surd** is a root that cannot be reduced to a rational number: √2, ∛5, √7. Its decimal never
terminates and never repeats, so it is left in root form and handled exactly.

Every surd is an index in disguise, and converting it is what makes surd questions easy:

- √a = a^(1/2), ∛a = a^(1/3), ⁿ√a = a^(1/n);
- ⁿ√(aᵐ) = a^(m/n);
- √a × √b = √(ab), because a^(1/2) × b^(1/2) = (ab)^(1/2);
- √a ÷ √b = √(a/b).

The **order** of a surd is the n in ⁿ√a. Surds of the same order combine directly; surds of
different orders must first be brought to a common order, exactly as fractions are brought to a
common denominator. To compare ∛3 with √2, write them as 3^(1/3) and 2^(1/2), take the LCM of the
denominators (6), and raise both to the sixth power: 3² = 9 against 2³ = 8. So **∛3 is larger**.

**Rationalising the denominator** removes a surd from the bottom of a fraction. Multiply above and
below by the same surd, or by the conjugate:

<div class="formula">1/√a = √a/a  ·  1/(√a + √b) = (√a − √b)/(a − b), using (x + y)(x − y) = x² − y²</div>

So 1/(√5 − √3) = (√5 + √3)/(5 − 3) = (√5 + √3)/2.

Two more habits worth having:

- **Simplify before you rationalise.** √50 = √(25 × 2) = 5√2, so 1/√50 = 1/(5√2) = √2/10. Taking
  √50 to the top untouched gives √50/50, which is the same number written badly.
- **Like surds add; unlike surds do not.** 3√2 + 5√2 = 8√2, but 3√2 + 5√3 cannot be combined at
  all. Reduce every surd to its simplest form first, because √8 + √18 looks unlike and is not:
  2√2 + 3√2 = 5√2.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Convert to index form first, always. A question that looks like "simplify ∛(x⁴) × √(x³) ÷
    x^(5/6)" is unpleasant in root notation and is three additions and a subtraction in index
    notation: 4/3 + 3/2 − 5/6 = 8/6 + 9/6 − 5/6 = 12/6 = 2, so the answer is x².
  </p>
</Callout>

## 12. Exponential equations: equating the bases

An equation with the unknown in the exponent is solved by making the two sides powers of **the same
base**, then equating the exponents. The rule that licenses it:

<div class="formula">If aˣ = aʸ and a is positive with a ≠ 1, then x = y</div>

Solve 3^(2x−1) = 81. Write 81 as 3⁴. Then 2x − 1 = 4, so **x = 2.5**.

Solve 2^(x+3) = 8^(x−1). The common base is 2, and 8 = 2³, so the right side is 2^(3x−3). Equate:
x + 3 = 3x − 3, giving 2x = 6 and **x = 3**.

Some equations reduce to a quadratic in a power. In 4ˣ − 5·2ˣ + 4 = 0, put y = 2ˣ; since
4ˣ = (2²)ˣ = (2ˣ)², the equation becomes y² − 5y + 4 = 0, so y = 1 or y = 4, giving **x = 0 or
x = 2**.

Two special cases are worth holding:

- **aˣ = 1** forces x = 0 (for a ≠ 1), because a⁰ = 1 and no other power of a equals 1.
- **aˣ = bˣ with a ≠ b** also forces x = 0, since (a/b)ˣ = 1.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The excluded values matter. The rule needs <strong>a positive and a ≠ 1</strong>. From
    1ˣ = 1ʸ nothing follows, because every power of 1 is 1. And an equation such as xˣ = x⁴ has the
    extra root x = 1 that base-equating alone would miss. At Foundation level the bases you meet are
    tame, but an option that says "x = 3 only" against "x = 0 or x = 3" is testing precisely this.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 13. Logarithms: the definition and the two-way conversion

A logarithm answers one question: **to what power must the base be raised to give this number?**

<div class="formula">logₐ N = x means exactly the same thing as aˣ = N (a &gt; 0, a ≠ 1, N &gt; 0)</div>

The two statements are the same fact written twice. 2⁵ = 32 in index form is log₂ 32 = 5 in
logarithmic form. Practise the conversion until it is automatic, because almost every logarithm
question begins by moving from one form to the other.

| Index form | Logarithmic form |
|---|---|
| 3⁴ = 81 | log₃ 81 = 4 |
| 10³ = 1,000 | log₁₀ 1,000 = 3 |
| 5⁰ = 1 | log₅ 1 = 0 |
| 2⁻³ = 1/8 | log₂ (1/8) = −3 |
| 49^(1/2) = 7 | log₄₉ 7 = 1/2 |

Read the conditions carefully, because they generate options on their own.

- **The base must be positive and must not be 1.** If the base were 1, every power of it would be 1
  and the logarithm could not be defined.
- **The number N must be positive.** No power of a positive base is negative or zero, so
  **the logarithm of a negative number and of zero does not exist** in the real system.
- **The logarithm itself may be any real number** — positive, zero or negative. log₂ (1/8) = −3 is
  perfectly ordinary. A negative logarithm means the number is between 0 and 1, not that the number
  is negative.

**Why the paper cares.** A logarithm is the only tool at Foundation level that
gets an unknown **out of an exponent**. Compound interest, the effective rate, the
number of years to double a deposit, a compound annual growth rate — every one of
them puts the unknown in the exponent, and every one of them is solved by taking
logarithms of both sides. Chapter 4 (Mathematics of Finance) uses this on almost
every question, so the time spent here is repaid twice.

Four results follow straight from the definition and are worth memorising as facts:

<div class="formula">logₐ 1 = 0  ·  logₐ a = 1  ·  a^(logₐ N) = N  ·  logₐ (aˣ) = x</div>

## 14. The laws of logarithms

Because a logarithm is an exponent, every law of indices becomes a law of logarithms. Throughout,
the base a is the same in every term, and m and n are positive.

<div class="formula">Product: logₐ (mn) = logₐ m + logₐ n</div>
<div class="formula">Quotient: logₐ (m/n) = logₐ m − logₐ n</div>
<div class="formula">Power: logₐ (mᵖ) = p × logₐ m</div>

That is the whole toolkit, and it is why logarithms were invented: they turn **multiplication into
addition**, division into subtraction, and raising to a power into multiplication. A fifth root
becomes a division by 5.

Two more that fall out of the power law:

- **Root law** — logₐ (ⁿ√m) = (1/n) × logₐ m, because ⁿ√m = m^(1/n).
- **Reciprocal law** — logₐ n × logₙ a = 1, so logₐ n = 1/logₙ a.

Used together, they collapse ugly expressions. Given log 2 = 0.3010 and log 3 = 0.4771,
log 12 = log (2² × 3) = 2 log 2 + log 3 = 0.6020 + 0.4771 = **1.0791**. No multiplication was
needed.

The laws run both ways, and the paper uses both directions.

**Expanding** breaks one logarithm into several: log (a²b/c³) = 2 log a + log b − 3 log c. Move the
exponents out in front, turn the multiplication into a plus and the division into a minus.

**Condensing** does the reverse, and it is the step that makes an equation solvable. Everything
must first be turned into a coefficient of 1 before the terms can be joined:

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Condensing to a single logarithm</div>
  Simplify log 2 + 2 log 3 − log 6.<br />
  Power law first: 2 log 3 = log 3² = log 9<br />
  Then product and quotient: log 2 + log 9 − log 6 = log (2 × 9 ÷ 6) = <strong>log 3</strong><br />
  Numerically: 0.3010 + 0.9542 − 0.7781 = 0.4771 = log 3 ✓
</div>

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">A short chain that appears in this form every year</div>
  Show that log (75/16) − 2 log (5/9) + log (32/243) = log 2.<br />
  2 log (5/9) = log (25/81)<br />
  The expression becomes log [(75/16) × (81/25) × (32/243)]<br />
  Inside the bracket: (75 × 81 × 32) ÷ (16 × 25 × 243) = 1,94,400 ÷ 97,200 = 2<br />
  So the expression is <strong>log 2</strong> ✓
</div>

Notice what made both of them work: every coefficient was absorbed by the power law **before** any
adding or subtracting began. Trying to add log 2 to 2 log 3 directly has no meaning.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>The log of a sum is not the sum of the logs.</strong> log (m + n) has no expansion at
    all — it is not log m + log n, and it is not log m × log n. Similarly
    log (m/n) is <em>not</em> (log m)/(log n), and (log m)² is not 2 log m. These four
    look-alike errors between them account for most of the wrong answers in the logarithm half of
    the chapter, and each of them lands on a specific option: expanding log (m + n) as
    log m + log n turns log 5 into log 2 + log 3 = 0.7781, which is the value of log 6.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 15. Change of base; common and natural logarithms

A logarithm to one base can always be rewritten in another base. This is what lets you use a
calculator or a table that only knows base 10.

<div class="formula">logₐ N = (logₚ N) ÷ (logₚ a), for any admissible new base p</div>

Setting N = p gives the reciprocal law again: logₐ p × logₚ a = 1. Setting the new base to 10
gives the practical form: **logₐ N = log N ÷ log a**, where log means base 10.

So log₂ 50 = log 50 ÷ log 2 = 1.6990 ÷ 0.3010 = **5.6445**.

**Two bases have their own names.**

- **Common logarithms** use base **10**. They are written simply as **log N**, with the base left
  out, and they are the ones used with tables. Throughout this chapter, `log` with no base written
  means base 10.
- **Natural logarithms** use base **e**, where e = 2.71828..., and are written **ln N** (sometimes
  logₑ N). They are the ones that appear in continuous-compounding and growth formulas later in the
  paper.

The two are proportional, because change of base is just a constant multiplier:

<div class="formula">ln N = 2.3026 × log N  ·  log N = 0.4343 × ln N  (since 1/2.3026 = 0.4343)</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    If a question writes <em>log</em> with no base, take base 10 unless the question is plainly about
    continuous growth. If it writes <em>ln</em>, take base e. And if an option list contains both
    a value and roughly 2.3 times that value, the question is testing exactly this distinction — the
    factor 2.3026 between the two answers is the tell.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 16. Characteristic, mantissa and antilogarithms

A common logarithm has two parts. The **characteristic** is the integral part; the **mantissa** is
the decimal part, and **the mantissa is always written as positive**.

log 4,270 = 3.6304 — characteristic 3, mantissa 0.6304.

The mantissa depends only on the **sequence of significant digits**, so 4.27, 42.7, 427 and 4,270
all share the mantissa 0.6304. The characteristic alone records where the decimal point sits, and
it is found by counting, not by table:

<div class="formula">Number ≥ 1 with n digits before the decimal point: characteristic = n − 1</div>
<div class="formula">Number below 1 with m zeros between the decimal point and the first significant digit: characteristic = −(m + 1)</div>

| Number | Digits / zeros | Characteristic | Logarithm |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4,270 | 4 digits before the point | 3 | 3.6304 |
| 42.7 | 2 digits | 1 | 1.6304 |
| 4.27 | 1 digit | 0 | 0.6304 |
| 0.427 | no zeros after the point | −1 | 1̄.6304 |
| 0.0427 | 1 zero after the point | −2 | 2̄.6304 |
| 0.00427 | 2 zeros after the point | −3 | 3̄.6304 |

The bar notation matters. **1̄.6304 is not −1.6304.** It means (−1) + 0.6304 = **−0.3696**: the
characteristic is negative and the mantissa stays positive. Keeping the mantissa positive is what
lets one table serve every decimal position, and it is a convention, not a rule of algebra.

An **antilogarithm** runs the process backwards: if log N = x then N = antilog x. Look up the
mantissa in the antilog table to get the digit sequence, then use the characteristic to place the
decimal point — characteristic c means the number has c + 1 digits before the point when c is zero
or positive, and c leading zeros after the point when c is negative, counting as above.

**Reading a four-figure table.** The body of the table is entered with the first three significant
digits; a separate "mean difference" column adjusts for the fourth. For log 4,273: the row 42,
column 7 gives 6304, and the mean-difference column 3 adds 3, giving a mantissa of 0.6307. The
characteristic is 3, so **log 4,273 = 3.6307**.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">An antilogarithm, step by step</div>
  Find N when log N = 2.8572.<br />
  Mantissa 0.8572 → the antilog table gives the digit sequence <strong>7197</strong><br />
  Characteristic 2 → the number has 2 + 1 = 3 digits before the decimal point<br />
  N = <strong>719.7</strong><br />
  (The exact value is 720. The gap is the four-figure rounding, not an error — a four-figure table
  is only good to about four significant figures, and an examiner who wants 720 will say "correct
  to the nearest whole number".)
</div>

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">The same with a negative characteristic</div>
  Find N when log N = 2̄.8572, that is −2 + 0.8572.<br />
  The mantissa is the same 0.8572, so the digits are again 7197<br />
  Characteristic −2 → one zero between the decimal point and the first significant digit<br />
  N = <strong>0.07197</strong><br />
  Notice that only the decimal point moved. The mantissa never changes when a number is multiplied
  or divided by a power of 10, which is the whole reason the tables are built this way.
</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Two errors, and both are arithmetic rather than conceptual. <strong>(i) Characteristic off by
    one</strong> — 4 digits gives 3, not 4; one zero after the decimal point gives −2, not −1.
    <strong>(ii) Treating a bar characteristic as an ordinary negative number</strong> — writing
    1̄.6304 as −1.6304 shifts the answer by twice the mantissa. Convert to a single negative decimal
    before you do anything else with it, or keep the two parts separate throughout; do not mix the
    two habits inside one question.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 17. Logarithmic equations and the extraneous-root check

An equation with the unknown inside a logarithm is solved in three steps: collapse each side into a
**single** logarithm using the laws, remove the logarithms, then **test every root against the
domain**.

The first step uses the laws of §14. The second uses the one-to-one property:

<div class="formula">If logₐ M = logₐ N then M = N; and if logₐ M = k then M = aᵏ</div>

The third step is the one that carries marks. A logarithm exists only for a **positive** argument,
so any root that makes any argument zero or negative must be **rejected** — it is an
**extraneous root**, produced by the algebra and not by the original equation.

**Worked in full.** Solve log₂ x + log₂ (x − 2) = 3.

- Combine: log₂ [x(x − 2)] = 3.
- Remove: x(x − 2) = 2³ = 8, so x² − 2x − 8 = 0.
- Factorise: (x − 4)(x + 2) = 0, giving x = 4 or x = −2.
- **Check.** x = 4 makes both arguments positive (4 and 2), so it is valid. x = −2 makes log₂ (−2)
  meaningless, so it is rejected. The solution is **x = 4 only**.

**Second pattern.** Solve log (x − 1) + log (x + 1) = log 8, base 10 throughout. Combining gives
log (x² − 1) = log 8, so x² = 9 and x = ±3. Only **x = 3** survives: at x = −3 both x − 1 and
x + 1 are negative.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Skipping the domain check is the standard trap, and the examiner builds the option list around
    it: one option gives both roots, one gives the valid root, one gives the rejected root alone.
    "x = 4 or x = −2" is a wrong answer to the worked equation above even though both numbers came
    out of correct algebra. The equation, not the quadratic, decides what counts as a solution.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 18. Negative marking, and when a guess is worth making

Paper 3 gives 1 mark for a correct answer, deducts 0.25 for a wrong one, and gives 0 for a blank.
It is worth seeing the arithmetic once, calmly, rather than carrying a vague fear of the deduction.

With four options and no information at all, a random answer is right one time in four. The
expected value is (1/4 × 1) + (3/4 × −0.25) = **+0.0625 marks** — slightly positive. Eliminate one
option you are sure is wrong and the expected value rises to about **+0.17**; eliminate two and it
is **+0.375**. On this scale the deduction is designed to remove the reward for blind ticking, not
to punish a reasoned attempt.

The number that does not appear in that calculation is the one that matters here. Guesses in this
chapter are **not random**. Every distractor in a well-set quantitative paper is the answer that a
particular slip produces — the inverted ratio, the dropped minus sign on an exponent, the log of a
sum expanded as a sum of logs, the characteristic counted one too high. A student who makes the
slip does not pick randomly among four; they walk straight to the prepared option and feel certain
about it.

So the practical rule is not about guessing at all. It is about **where you spend your check**.
When you finish a question in this chapter, re-read the single step where the named error of that
topic would occur — the order of the ratio, the sign of the index, the argument of the logarithm.
That check costs a few seconds and it is what separates a working method from a confident wrong
answer. If after it you still cannot choose between two options, answering is marginally better
than leaving it blank; if you have no basis for eliminating anything and the question is long,
moving on protects the time you need for the questions you can actually do.

## Fully worked examples

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — dividing a profit when only a difference is given (easy-moderate)">
  <p>
    Sahyadri Foods has three partners who share profit in the ratio <strong>5 : 4 : 3</strong>. For
    the year ended 31 March 2026 the second partner's share exceeds the third partner's share by
    <strong>₹ 84,000</strong>. Find the total profit and each partner's share.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — identify what the given number measures</div>
    The ₹ 84,000 is a <strong>difference</strong>, not a share. In parts, that difference is
    4 − 3 = <strong>1 part</strong>.<br />
    Therefore 1 part = ₹ 84,000.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — total and shares</div>
    Total parts = 5 + 4 + 3 = 12, so total profit = 12 × 84,000 = <strong>₹ 10,08,000</strong><br />
    First partner = 5 × 84,000 = <strong>₹ 4,20,000</strong><br />
    Second partner = 4 × 84,000 = <strong>₹ 3,36,000</strong><br />
    Third partner = 3 × 84,000 = <strong>₹ 2,52,000</strong><br />
    Check: 4,20,000 + 3,36,000 + 2,52,000 = ₹ 10,08,000 ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — the two wrong answers this question generates</div>
    Reading ₹ 84,000 as the <em>second partner's share</em> makes 4 parts = 84,000, one part
    ₹ 21,000 and the total ₹ 2,52,000 — which is also, confusingly, the correct third share.<br />
    Dividing 84,000 by the <em>sum</em> 4 + 3 = 7 instead of the difference gives one part ₹ 12,000
    and a total of ₹ 1,44,000.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — building a continued ratio, then dividing (moderate)">
  <p>
    Stock is held at three depots. Depot A to Depot B is <strong>3 : 4</strong>, and Depot B to
    Depot C is <strong>6 : 11</strong>. The three depots together hold <strong>3,44,000 kg</strong>.
    How much is at each depot?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — make B agree in both statements</div>
    B is 4 in the first ratio and 6 in the second; the LCM is 12.<br />
    A : B = 3 : 4, multiplied by 3, gives 9 : 12<br />
    B : C = 6 : 11, multiplied by 2, gives 12 : 22<br />
    Therefore <strong>A : B : C = 9 : 12 : 22</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — divide the total</div>
    Total parts = 9 + 12 + 22 = 43, so one part = 3,44,000 ÷ 43 = 8,000 kg<br />
    A = 9 × 8,000 = <strong>72,000 kg</strong> · B = 12 × 8,000 = <strong>96,000 kg</strong> ·
    C = 22 × 8,000 = <strong>1,76,000 kg</strong><br />
    Check: 72,000 + 96,000 + 1,76,000 = 3,44,000 kg ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — the trap</div>
    Writing the continued ratio as <strong>3 : 4 : 11</strong> — taking the terms as they stand
    without reconciling B — gives 18 parts, one part 19,111.11 kg, and three answers that do not
    even divide evenly. A total that refuses to divide cleanly is usually the signal that the
    continued ratio was never formed.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — a ratio compounded of three named ratios (moderate)">
  <p>
    Find the ratio compounded of the <strong>duplicate ratio of 4 : 5</strong>, the
    <strong>triplicate ratio of 2 : 3</strong>, and the <strong>sub-duplicate ratio of
    81 : 256</strong>.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — form each named ratio first</div>
    Duplicate of 4 : 5 = 4² : 5² = <strong>16 : 25</strong><br />
    Triplicate of 2 : 3 = 2³ : 3³ = <strong>8 : 27</strong><br />
    Sub-duplicate of 81 : 256 = √81 : √256 = <strong>9 : 16</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — compound them</div>
    Multiply the antecedents together and the consequents together:<br />
    (16 × 8 × 9) : (25 × 27 × 16) = 1,152 : 10,800<br />
    Divide both by 144: <strong>8 : 75</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — the two prepared wrong answers</div>
    Using 81 : 256 as it stands, without taking the sub-duplicate, gives
    (16 × 8 × 81) : (25 × 27 × 256) = 10,368 : 1,72,800 = <strong>3 : 50</strong>.<br />
    Taking the <em>square</em> instead of the square root for "sub-duplicate" gives a far larger
    consequent still. Both look like ordinary answers; neither is reachable if you write the three
    named ratios out separately before compounding.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — componendo et dividendo (moderate)">
  <p>
    If <strong>(4x + 3y) ÷ (4x − 3y) = 11 ÷ 5</strong>, find the ratio x : y. Then solve
    <strong>(x + 7) ÷ (x − 7) = 5 ÷ 3</strong> for x.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — recognise the shape</div>
    The left side is (P + Q)/(P − Q) with P = 4x and Q = 3y. Componendo et dividendo says that if
    P/Q = m/n then (P + Q)/(P − Q) = (m + n)/(m − n). Run it backwards.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — undo the operation</div>
    4x / 3y = (11 + 5) / (11 − 5) = 16/6 = 8/3<br />
    So 12x = 24y, giving x/y = 2, that is <strong>x : y = 2 : 1</strong><br />
    Check: with x = 2, y = 1, (8 + 3)/(8 − 3) = 11/5 ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — the second equation</div>
    Here P = x and Q = 7, so x/7 = (5 + 3)/(5 − 3) = 8/2 = 4, giving <strong>x = 28</strong><br />
    Check: (28 + 7)/(28 − 7) = 35/21 = 5/3 ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — the wrong answer to watch</div>
    Inverting the operation — writing 4x/3y = (11 − 5)/(11 + 5) = 6/16 = 3/8 — gives
    x : y = 9 : 32, and in the second equation x/7 = (5 − 3)/(5 + 3) = 1/4, so x = 1.75. Both
    appear on option lists. The sum always goes on top, because componendo (the operation that
    <em>adds</em>) supplies the numerator of the combined fraction.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — joint variation, direct one way and inverse the other (moderate)">
  <p>
    At Sahyadri's packing hall the time taken to complete an order varies <strong>directly</strong>
    as the number of cases and <strong>inversely</strong> as the number of machines running.
    <strong>12 machines</strong> pack <strong>3,000 cases</strong> in <strong>8 hours</strong>. How
    long will <strong>9 machines</strong> take to pack <strong>4,500 cases</strong>?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — write the relation and find k</div>
    t = k × C ÷ M, where C is cases and M is machines.<br />
    8 = k × 3,000 ÷ 12 = 250k, so k = 8 ÷ 250 = <strong>0.032</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — substitute the second set</div>
    t = 0.032 × 4,500 ÷ 9 = 0.032 × 500 = <strong>16 hours</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — the same answer as a ratio check</div>
    Cases rise by the factor 4,500 ÷ 3,000 = 1.5, which raises the time.<br />
    Machines fall by the factor 9 ÷ 12 = 0.75, and fewer machines also raise the time, by the
    inverse factor 12 ÷ 9 = 4/3.<br />
    t = 8 × 1.5 × 4/3 = <strong>16 hours</strong> ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — three wrong answers, each from one identifiable slip</div>
    Treating machines as a <em>direct</em> factor: 8 × 1.5 × 9/12 = <strong>9 hours</strong><br />
    Ignoring the machines entirely: 8 × 1.5 = <strong>12 hours</strong><br />
    Inverting both factors: 8 × 2/3 × 3/4 = <strong>4 hours</strong><br />
    Deciding the direction in words first — fewer machines must mean <em>more</em> hours — rules out
    9 and 4 before any arithmetic is done.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 6 — the laws of indices, and an exponential equation (moderate)">
  <p>
    (a) Simplify (x^(a−b))^(a+b) × (x^(b−c))^(b+c) × (x^(c−a))^(c+a).
    (b) Solve 5^(x+2) × 5^(2x−1) = 625^(x−1).
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — part (a), power of a power multiplies</div>
    (x^(a−b))^(a+b) = x^(a² − b²), and likewise for the other two factors.<br />
    Multiplying powers of the same base adds the exponents:<br />
    (a² − b²) + (b² − c²) + (c² − a²) = <strong>0</strong><br />
    So the product is x⁰ = <strong>1</strong>, for every x other than 0.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — part (b), bring both sides to base 5</div>
    Left side: 5^(x+2) × 5^(2x−1) = 5^(3x + 1)<br />
    Right side: 625 = 5⁴, so 625^(x−1) = 5^(4x − 4)<br />
    Equate the exponents: 3x + 1 = 4x − 4, so <strong>x = 5</strong><br />
    Check: left 5^16, right 625⁴ = 5^16 ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — the slips</div>
    In (a), multiplying the two exponents of a product instead of adding them gives x raised to a
    long product rather than x⁰ = 1, and the option "0" then looks right — but the value of the
    expression is 1, not 0; only the <em>exponent</em> is 0.<br />
    In (b), reading 625 as 5⁵ gives 3x + 1 = 5x − 5 and x = 3.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 7 — negative and fractional exponents together (moderate)">
  <p>
    Evaluate <strong>32^(−2/5) + 81^(3/4) − (1/64)^(−1/3)</strong>.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — first term: negative index means reciprocal</div>
    32^(−2/5) = 1 ÷ 32^(2/5)<br />
    32^(1/5) = 2, because 2⁵ = 32; so 32^(2/5) = 2² = 4<br />
    First term = <strong>1/4 = 0.25</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — second term: root first, then power</div>
    81^(1/4) = 3, because 3⁴ = 81; so 81^(3/4) = 3³ = <strong>27</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — third term: negative index flips the fraction</div>
    (1/64)^(−1/3) = 64^(1/3) = <strong>4</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — assemble</div>
    0.25 + 27 − 4 = <strong>23.25</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 5 — the prepared wrong answers</div>
    Reading 32^(−2/5) as −4 (a negative index taken as a negative value) gives −4 + 27 − 4 =
    <strong>19</strong>.<br />
    Leaving (1/64)^(−1/3) as 1/4 (forgetting to flip) gives 0.25 + 27 − 0.25 =
    <strong>27</strong>.<br />
    Reading 81^(3/4) as 81 × 3/4 gives 60.75 in place of 27.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 8 — working with a short table of logarithms (moderate)">
  <p>
    Given <strong>log 2 = 0.3010</strong> and <strong>log 3 = 0.4771</strong> (base 10 throughout),
    find (a) log 720, (b) log 4.5, and (c) the number of digits in 2³⁰.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — the one extra value you need</div>
    log 5 = log (10 ÷ 2) = log 10 − log 2 = 1 − 0.3010 = <strong>0.6990</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (a) factorise, then use the product and power laws</div>
    720 = 2⁴ × 3² × 5<br />
    log 720 = 4 log 2 + 2 log 3 + log 5 = 1.2040 + 0.9542 + 0.6990 = <strong>2.8572</strong><br />
    Sense check: the characteristic 2 says 720 has three digits ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (b) the quotient law</div>
    4.5 = 9 ÷ 2, so log 4.5 = 2 log 3 − log 2 = 0.9542 − 0.3010 = <strong>0.6532</strong><br />
    Characteristic 0, so the number lies between 1 and 10 ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — (c) digits from the characteristic</div>
    log 2³⁰ = 30 × 0.3010 = 9.030<br />
    The characteristic is 9, and a number with characteristic n has n + 1 digits, so 2³⁰ has
    <strong>10 digits</strong>. (It is 1,07,37,41,824.)
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 5 — the errors these three parts are built to catch</div>
    Computing log 4.5 as (log 9) ÷ (log 2) = 0.9542 ÷ 0.3010 = <strong>3.1702</strong> — the quotient
    law applies to the <em>number</em> inside the log, never to the logarithms themselves.<br />
    Answering "9 digits" in part (c) by reading the characteristic as the digit count.<br />
    Expanding log 720 as log 700 + log 20, or as log 7 + log 20, on a number that was never
    factorised.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 9 — the growth rate the bank asked for (hard: indices and logarithms together)">
  <p>
    Sahyadri Foods' turnover was <strong>₹ 40,00,000</strong> five years ago and is
    <strong>₹ 1,60,00,000</strong> today. Find the compound annual growth rate. Use
    log 2 = 0.3010, and antilog 0.1204 = 1.3195.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — set up the index equation</div>
    If the rate is r per annum, then 40,00,000 × (1 + r)⁵ = 1,60,00,000<br />
    So (1 + r)⁵ = 1,60,00,000 ÷ 40,00,000 = <strong>4</strong><br />
    The unknown sits in the base, under a fifth power — take logarithms of both sides.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — the power law turns the root into a division</div>
    5 × log (1 + r) = log 4 = 2 log 2 = 2 × 0.3010 = 0.6020<br />
    log (1 + r) = 0.6020 ÷ 5 = <strong>0.1204</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — read the characteristic before the antilog</div>
    The characteristic is <strong>0</strong>, so the number lies between 1 and 10 — which is the
    sense check that 1 + r must pass, since r is a growth rate of well under 900%.<br />
    1 + r = antilog 0.1204 = <strong>1.3195</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — the answer</div>
    r = 0.3195, that is <strong>31.95% per annum, about 32%</strong><br />
    Check: 1.3195⁵ = 4.00 to two decimals, and 40,00,000 × 4 = ₹ 1,60,00,000 ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 5 — the three wrong routes, each with its own option</div>
    <strong>Simple average growth:</strong> (1,60,00,000 − 40,00,000) ÷ 40,00,000 ÷ 5 = 60% per
    annum. It is a real number and a real concept, but it is not compound growth.<br />
    <strong>Dividing the ratio instead of its logarithm:</strong> 4 ÷ 5 = 0.8, read as 80%.<br />
    <strong>Antilogging 0.6020 and forgetting to divide by 5:</strong> 1 + r = 4, that is 300%.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 10 — a mixture whose ratio has to be changed (moderate)">
  <p>
    A tank holds <strong>60 litres</strong> of a mixture of fruit concentrate and water in the ratio
    <strong>7 : 5</strong>. (a) How much water must be added so that the ratio becomes
    <strong>7 : 9</strong>? (b) Starting again from the original 60 litres, what is the ratio if
    <strong>12 litres of the mixture</strong> is drawn off and replaced by water?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — split the 60 litres first</div>
    Total parts 7 + 5 = 12, so one part = 60 ÷ 12 = 5 litres<br />
    Concentrate = 7 × 5 = <strong>35 litres</strong> · Water = 5 × 5 = <strong>25 litres</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (a) only the water changes</div>
    Adding water leaves the concentrate at 35 litres. Let w litres be added.<br />
    35 ÷ (25 + w) = 7 ÷ 9, so 7(25 + w) = 315, giving 25 + w = 45<br />
    <strong>w = 20 litres</strong>, and the tank then holds 80 litres in the ratio 35 : 45 = 7 : 9 ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (b) drawing off mixture removes both, in the old ratio</div>
    12 litres of the mixture is itself 7 : 5, so it removes 12 × 7/12 = 7 litres of concentrate and
    12 × 5/12 = 5 litres of water.<br />
    Concentrate 35 − 7 = 28 litres; water 25 − 5 + 12 = 32 litres<br />
    New ratio = 28 : 32 = <strong>7 : 8</strong> (the total is still 60 litres ✓)
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — the two traps</div>
    In (a), treating the ratio 7 : 9 as a ratio of <em>parts of the new total</em> and computing
    water as 60 × 9/16 = 33.75 litres, then adding 33.75 − 25 = 8.75 litres — the concentrate would
    then be 26.25 litres, but no concentrate was removed.<br />
    In (b), taking the 12 litres drawn off as pure water: concentrate 35, water 25 − 12 + 12 = 25,
    ratio unchanged at 7 : 5. A drawn-off mixture always leaves in the ratio it was in.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 11 — a logarithmic equation and a change of base (hard)">
  <p>
    (a) Solve <strong>log₃ (x + 6) + log₃ (x − 2) = 2</strong>. (b) Evaluate
    <strong>log₈ 32</strong> and <strong>log₉ 27</strong> without a table.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) collapse the left side</div>
    The product law gives log₃ [(x + 6)(x − 2)] = 2<br />
    Remove the logarithm: (x + 6)(x − 2) = 3² = 9
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — solve the quadratic</div>
    x² + 4x − 12 = 9, so x² + 4x − 21 = 0<br />
    (x + 7)(x − 3) = 0, giving x = −7 or x = 3
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — the domain check, which decides the answer</div>
    x = 3: the arguments are 9 and 1, both positive. Valid, and log₃ 9 + log₃ 1 = 2 + 0 = 2 ✓<br />
    x = −7: the arguments are −1 and −9, both negative, and no logarithm of a negative number
    exists. <strong>Rejected as an extraneous root.</strong><br />
    Answer: <strong>x = 3 only</strong>. The option "x = 3 or x = −7" is on the paper, and it is the
    answer to the quadratic rather than to the equation.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — (b) change of base to 2 and to 3</div>
    log₈ 32 = (log₂ 32) ÷ (log₂ 8) = 5 ÷ 3 = <strong>5/3</strong><br />
    log₉ 27 = (log₃ 27) ÷ (log₃ 9) = 3 ÷ 2 = <strong>3/2</strong><br />
    Check the first from the definition: 8^(5/3) = (8^(1/3))⁵ = 2⁵ = 32 ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 5 — the slip in (b)</div>
    Dividing the numbers instead of their logarithms — 32 ÷ 8 = 4 — gives the option "4", and
    log₈ 32 = 4 would mean 8⁴ = 32, which is 4,096. A ten-second check from the definition kills
    that option outright.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

- **Inverting a ratio.** "A is to B as 4 is to 3" is not "B is to A as 4 is to 3". Write down which
  quantity is the antecedent before you touch the numbers; almost every ratio question carries the
  inverted answer as an option.
- **Adding the same number to both terms to "simplify".** Only multiplying or dividing both terms
  by the same non-zero number leaves a ratio unchanged.
- **Comparing quantities in different units.** Convert first. 750 g to 3 kg is 1 : 4, not 250 : 1.
- **"Duplicate" read as "double".** Duplicate squares the terms; sub-duplicate takes their square
  roots; triplicate cubes them; sub-triplicate takes cube roots.
- **Writing a continued ratio without reconciling the shared term.** From A : B = 3 : 4 and
  B : C = 6 : 11, the answer is 9 : 12 : 22, never 3 : 4 : 11.
- **Confusing a share, a difference and the total.** Decide what the given number counts before
  dividing by the number of parts.
- **Mixing up the three proportionals.** Fourth proportional to a, b, c is bc/a; third proportional
  to a, b is b²/a; mean proportional between a and c is √(ac).
- **Getting componendo's denominator wrong.** Componendo gives (a + b)/b, and dividendo gives
  (a − b)/b. Componendo et dividendo puts the sum on top: (a + b)/(a − b).
- **Assuming variation is direct.** Fewer workers means more hours, not fewer. State the direction
  in words, then compute.
- **Distributing a power over a sum.** (a + b)² is not a² + b², and √(a² + b²) is not a + b.
- **Reading a negative index as a negative number.** 2⁻³ = 1/8, a positive quantity.
- **Reversing a fractional index.** In a^(m/n), the denominator n is the root and the numerator m
  is the power. 8^(2/3) = 4, not 8^(3/2) = 22.63 and not 8 × 2/3.
- **Writing a⁰ = 0.** It is 1 for every non-zero base, however large or small the base is.
- **Expanding the log of a sum.** log (m + n) has no expansion. Nor is log (m/n) equal to
  (log m)/(log n), nor (log m)² equal to 2 log m.
- **Taking the log of a negative number or of zero.** It does not exist. If your working needs one,
  a root has to be rejected.
- **Counting the characteristic one out.** A number with 4 digits before the decimal point has
  characteristic 3; a number with 1 zero after the decimal point has characteristic −2.
- **Treating a bar characteristic as an ordinary negative decimal.** 1̄.6304 is −0.3696, not
  −1.6304, because the mantissa stays positive.
- **Forgetting the domain check in a logarithmic equation.** Solve the quadratic, then test every
  root inside the original logarithms and discard the ones that make an argument zero or negative.
- **Mixing log and ln.** Unless the question says otherwise, log means base 10 and ln means base e,
  and the two differ by the factor 2.3026.

## One-page revision summary

**Ratio.** a : b compares two quantities of the same kind and unit; a is the antecedent, b the
consequent; the ratio has no unit and b cannot be zero.

<div class="formula">a : b = ka : kb (k ≠ 0)  ·  a : b = c : d ⇔ ad = bc  ·  adding the same number to both terms changes the ratio</div>

**Derived ratios**, from a : b:

<div class="formula">Inverse b : a  ·  Duplicate a² : b²  ·  Sub-duplicate √a : √b  ·  Triplicate a³ : b³  ·  Sub-triplicate ∛a : ∛b</div>
<div class="formula">Compound of a : b and c : d = ac : bd</div>
<div class="formula">If A : B = a : b and B : C = c : d, then A : B : C = ac : bc : bd</div>
<div class="formula">Dividing T in the ratio a : b : c — each share = T × (own part ÷ total parts)</div>

**Proportion.** a : b :: c : d means ad = bc; a and d are the extremes, b and c the means.

<div class="formula">Fourth proportional to a, b, c: d = bc/a  ·  Third proportional to a, b: c = b²/a  ·  Mean proportional between a and c: b = √(ac)</div>
<div class="formula">a, b, c in continued proportion ⇔ b² = ac</div>

**The five operations**, from a/b = c/d:

<div class="formula">Invertendo b/a = d/c  ·  Alternendo a/c = b/d  ·  Componendo (a+b)/b = (c+d)/d  ·  Dividendo (a−b)/b = (c−d)/d  ·  Componendo et dividendo (a+b)/(a−b) = (c+d)/(c−d)</div>
<div class="formula">Addendo: if a/b = c/d = e/f = k then (a+c+e)/(b+d+f) = k</div>

**Variation.**

<div class="formula">Direct: y = kx, so y₁/x₁ = y₂/x₂  ·  Inverse: xy = k, so x₁y₁ = x₂y₂  ·  Joint: y = kxz</div>

**Indices.**

<div class="formula">aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ  ·  aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ  ·  (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ  ·  (ab)ⁿ = aⁿbⁿ  ·  (a/b)ⁿ = aⁿ/bⁿ</div>
<div class="formula">a⁰ = 1 (a ≠ 0)  ·  a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ  ·  (a/b)⁻ⁿ = (b/a)ⁿ  ·  a^(m/n) = ⁿ√(aᵐ)</div>
<div class="formula">If aˣ = aʸ with a positive and a ≠ 1, then x = y; aˣ = 1 forces x = 0</div>

**Surds.** ⁿ√a = a^(1/n); √a × √b = √(ab); compare surds of different orders by raising both to the
LCM of the orders; rationalise with 1/√a = √a/a and 1/(√a + √b) = (√a − √b)/(a − b).

**Logarithms.**

<div class="formula">logₐ N = x ⇔ aˣ = N, with a positive, a ≠ 1 and N positive</div>
<div class="formula">logₐ 1 = 0  ·  logₐ a = 1  ·  a^(logₐ N) = N</div>
<div class="formula">log (mn) = log m + log n  ·  log (m/n) = log m − log n  ·  log (mᵖ) = p log m  ·  log ⁿ√m = (1/n) log m</div>
<div class="formula">Change of base: logₐ N = (logₚ N) ÷ (logₚ a)  ·  logₐ p × logₚ a = 1</div>
<div class="formula">ln N = 2.3026 log N  ·  log N = 0.4343 ln N</div>

**No expansion exists** for log (m + n). log (m/n) is not (log m)/(log n).

**Characteristic and mantissa** (common logarithms only). The mantissa depends only on the digit
sequence and is always positive; the characteristic locates the decimal point.

<div class="formula">Number ≥ 1 with n digits before the point: characteristic = n − 1  ·  Number below 1 with m zeros after the point: characteristic = −(m + 1), written with a bar</div>

A number with characteristic c has c + 1 digits before the decimal point. Antilog reverses the
process: the mantissa gives the digits, the characteristic places the point.

**Logarithmic equations.** Collapse each side to a single logarithm, use logₐ M = logₐ N ⇒ M = N or
logₐ M = k ⇒ M = aᵏ, then **reject every root that makes any argument zero or negative**.

**Marking.** 1 mark correct, −0.25 wrong, 0 blank. A four-option random guess has expected value
+0.0625; eliminating one option raises it to about +0.17 and two to +0.375. The real risk is not
random guessing but a confident wrong answer, because every distractor is built from a specific
error — check the step where that error would occur before you move on.
