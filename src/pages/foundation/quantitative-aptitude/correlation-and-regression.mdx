A poultry farm near Namakkal keeps two numbers for every batch of birds: the average daily
temperature in the shed and the number of eggs collected that day. Over a hot month the manager
notices that on warmer days the count falls, and on cooler days it rises. She wants two different
answers from the same pair of columns. First, **how strong** is the link between temperature and
egg output, on a single scale she can compare against last year. Second, **if tomorrow is forecast
at a particular temperature, how many eggs should she expect**, so she can plan the packing crates.

The first question is answered by **correlation**, the second by **regression**. They use the same
sums — Σx, Σy, Σxy, Σx² and Σy² — yet they answer different questions, and confusing the two is the
single most common error in this chapter. Correlation gives one pure number that measures how
tightly the two quantities move together. Regression gives an equation, a straight line, that lets
you estimate one variable from the other.

Paper 3 is fully objective, so you will not write an essay on either idea. You will compute a
coefficient, a rank correlation, a regression coefficient or a predicted value, and pick the right
option quickly. This chapter builds every formula from its sums, shows how the pieces connect, and
warns you where the examiner sets the traps.

## 1. What correlation measures

Two quantities are **correlated** when they tend to change together. Height and weight, advertising
spend and sales, temperature and egg output — in each pair, knowing one tells you something about
the likely value of the other. Correlation puts a single number on the strength and direction of
that tendency.

The direction is the sign:

- **Positive correlation** — the two move the **same** way. Large values of one go with large
  values of the other, small with small. Height and weight, or study hours and marks.
- **Negative correlation** — the two move in **opposite** ways. Large values of one go with small
  values of the other. Price and quantity demanded, or shed temperature and egg count.
- **Zero correlation** — no linear tendency at all. The values of one give no straight-line clue to
  the other.

The strength is how tightly the points cluster around a straight line. Perfectly tight is the
extreme; a loose scatter with only a faint drift is weak. The coefficient of correlation, written
**r**, captures both at once: its sign is the direction, and its size from 0 to 1 is the strength.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Correlation measures a <strong>linear</strong> tendency only. Two quantities can be tied by a
    perfect curved rule and still return an r near zero, because r asks one narrow question: how
    close are the points to a straight line. A small r means "little straight-line relationship", not
    "no relationship of any kind". At Foundation level every question is about linear correlation, so
    this rarely bites, but the wording of a concept question can turn on it.
  </p>
</Callout>

A last word of caution that examiners like to test. Correlation is not causation. A high r between
two series says they move together; it does not say one drives the other. Both may be driven by a
third thing, or the pairing may be pure coincidence — a **spurious** or **nonsense** correlation.
The number is silent on cause; only judgement supplies that.

## 2. Scatter diagrams and the shape of a relationship

Before any formula, the quickest read on a relationship is a **scatter diagram**: plot each pair
(x, y) as a dot, x across and y up. The cloud of dots shows the pattern at a glance, and you should
be able to describe one in words even though Paper 3 will not ask you to draw it.

- **Perfect positive.** All dots lie exactly on a single upward-sloping line. Here r = +1.
- **Perfect negative.** All dots lie exactly on a single downward-sloping line. Here r = −1.
- **High positive.** The dots slope up and hug a line closely but not exactly. r is near +1, say
  +0.8 to +0.95.
- **Low negative.** The dots drift downward but scatter widely around the trend. r is a small
  negative number, say −0.3.
- **No correlation.** The dots fill a shapeless blob or a horizontal band with no tilt. r is near 0.

Two readings of a scatter are examinable. The **direction** of the tilt is the sign of r, and the
**tightness** of the cluster around the line is the size of r. A tight upward band and a loose
upward band both have positive r, but the tight one is larger. When the dots sit exactly on a line,
correlation is perfect and, as a later section shows, the two regression lines become one.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The scatter diagram is the honest picture; the coefficient is the summary. Always let the sign
    you expect from the story — price up, demand down, so negative — sanity-check the sign your
    arithmetic produces. If the story says negative and your r comes out positive, you have almost
    certainly dropped a minus sign in Σxy or in one of the deviations.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. Karl Pearson's product-moment coefficient r

The workhorse measure of linear correlation is **Karl Pearson's product-moment coefficient**. It is
the covariance of the two variables divided by the product of their standard deviations:

<div class="formula">r = Cov(x, y) ÷ (σx × σy)</div>

where the covariance is the average product of the paired deviations from the two means:

<div class="formula">Cov(x, y) = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) ÷ n   ·   σx = √(Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ n)   ·   σy = √(Σ(y − ȳ)² ÷ n)</div>

Dividing covariance by the two standard deviations strips out the units and the scale, leaving a
pure number between −1 and +1. That single line is the whole idea; everything else is a way of
computing it without heavy arithmetic.

**The computing formula.** Deviations from a mean are ugly when the mean is not a whole number, so
in practice r is built straight from the five raw sums. Multiply through by n and the means
disappear:

<div class="formula">r = [nΣxy − (Σx)(Σy)] ÷ √([nΣx² − (Σx)²] × [nΣy² − (Σy)²])</div>

This is the form to use in the exam. Lay out a table with columns for x, y, xy, x² and y², total
each column, and drop the five sums into the formula. Nothing in it needs a mean or a standard
deviation as a separate step.

**Worked illustration.** Take five pairs: x = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and y = 3, 2, 5, 6, 4.

- Σx = 15, Σy = 20, Σxy = 66, Σx² = 55, Σy² = 90, n = 5
- Numerator = nΣxy − (Σx)(Σy) = 5×66 − 15×20 = 330 − 300 = 30
- Under the root: nΣx² − (Σx)² = 275 − 225 = 50; nΣy² − (Σy)² = 450 − 400 = 50
- r = 30 ÷ √(50 × 50) = 30 ÷ 50 = **0.6**

The sign is positive and the size is moderate: y rises with x, but loosely. Notice the two bracket
values under the root are each a variance multiplied by n, so they can never be negative, and the
sign of r always comes from the numerator alone.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The commonest arithmetic slip is squaring the wrong thing under the root. It is
    <strong>nΣx² − (Σx)²</strong>, that is n times the sum of the squares, minus the square of the
    sum. Writing (Σx)² where nΣx² belonged, or the reverse, wrecks the denominator. A quick check:
    each bracket must come out positive, and each must be smaller than nΣx² itself.
  </p>
</Callout>

**The assumed-mean short form.** When the numbers are large, subtract a convenient constant from
each variable first (a *change of origin*) and, if the values share a common gap, divide by it (a
*change of scale*). Because r is unaffected by either move — the next section proves it — you may
compute r on the small transformed numbers and the answer is unchanged. This is exactly why r is
such a convenient measure: you can shift and rescale the data into easy figures without touching the
result.

## 4. Properties of the correlation coefficient

Six properties are examinable, and most concept questions in this chapter test one of them.

1. **r lies between −1 and +1.** That is, −1 ≤ r ≤ 1 always. A computed r outside that range is a
   signal of an arithmetic error, most often in the denominator. r = +1 is perfect positive
   correlation, r = −1 perfect negative, r = 0 no linear correlation.

2. **r is a pure number.** It carries no units. Correlate height in centimetres with weight in
   kilograms, or the same data in inches and pounds, and r is identical. This is what makes it
   comparable across completely different pairs of variables.

3. **r is unaffected by a change of origin.** Add or subtract the same constant from every x, or
   from every y, and r does not change. Shifting where you measure from cannot alter how tightly two
   series move together.

4. **r is unaffected by a change of scale**, except that multiplying one variable by a **negative**
   number flips the sign of r. Multiply every x by a positive constant and r is unchanged; multiply
   by a negative constant and r keeps its size but reverses its sign. Formally, if u = (x − a) ÷ h
   and v = (y − b) ÷ k, then r between x and y equals r between u and v when h and k have the same
   sign, and equals its negative when h and k have opposite signs.

5. **r is symmetric.** The correlation of x with y equals the correlation of y with x. There is only
   one correlation coefficient for a pair, unlike regression, which has two coefficients.

6. **r is the geometric mean of the two regression coefficients**, r = ±√(b_yx × b_xy), a link
   developed fully in section 9. Its sign is the common sign of the two regression coefficients.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Property 3 and property 4 together are why the assumed-mean method is legitimate: subtracting a
    constant is a change of origin, dividing by a common gap is a change of scale by a positive
    number, and neither touches r. So r = 0.6 whether you compute it on the raw marks or on the tidy
    small deviations. The one trap is a negative scale factor — for instance replacing x by
    (50 − x) — which reverses the sign of r. Read whether the transformation multiplies by a
    negative number.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Rank correlation

Sometimes the data are not measurements but **ranks** — a first, a second, a third — or the
relationship is monotonic but not straight, so Pearson's r is not the right tool. Two judges rank
the same ten contestants; you want to know how far the two orderings agree. **Spearman's rank
correlation coefficient**, written R (or ρ), answers exactly that:

<div class="formula">R = 1 − 6Σd² ÷ [n(n² − 1)]</div>

Here n is the number of pairs and d is the difference between the two ranks of each pair. The steps
are mechanical: rank each series, take the difference d for every pair, square it, add the squares
to get Σd², and substitute.

**Worked illustration — no ties.** Seven items are ranked by two judges. The rank differences d come
out as −2, +1, −1, +2, −1, +1, 0, so d² = 4, 1, 1, 4, 1, 1, 0 and Σd² = 12.

- n = 7, so n(n² − 1) = 7 × 48 = 336
- R = 1 − (6 × 12) ÷ 336 = 1 − 72 ÷ 336 = 1 − 0.2143 = **0.7857**

The two judges agree fairly well but not perfectly. R runs from −1 (exactly reversed orderings)
through 0 (no agreement) to +1 (identical orderings), the same range as Pearson's r.

**Tied ranks.** When two or more items share a value, they share a rank: give each the **average**
of the rank positions they occupy. Two items tied for the 3rd and 4th places each get rank 3.5;
three items tied for 5th, 6th and 7th each get rank 6. Ties bias the plain formula slightly, so a
**correction** is added to Σd², one term for every group of ties:

<div class="formula">Corrected Σd² = Σd² + Σ (m³ − m) ÷ 12</div>

where m is the number of items in a tied group and the sum runs over every such group. Then
substitute the corrected figure into the same R formula.

**Worked illustration — with ties.** Five candidates are marked by two examiners:

- Examiner X gives 50, 70, 60, 60, 80; Examiner Y gives 55, 60, 40, 55, 60.
- X ranks (highest mark = rank 1): 80→1, 70→2, the two 60s share ranks 3 and 4 → 3.5 each, 50→5.
  So X ranks are 5, 2, 3.5, 3.5, 1.
- Y ranks: the two 60s share ranks 1 and 2 → 1.5 each, the two 55s share ranks 3 and 4 → 3.5 each,
  40→5. So Y ranks are 3.5, 1.5, 5, 3.5, 1.5.
- d = 1.5, 0.5, −1.5, 0, −0.5, so d² = 2.25, 0.25, 2.25, 0, 0.25 and Σd² = 5.
- Tie correction: one tied pair in X (m = 2) gives (8 − 2)/12 = 0.5; two tied pairs in Y give
  0.5 + 0.5 = 1.0. Total correction = 1.5, so corrected Σd² = 5 + 1.5 = 6.5.
- n = 5, n(n² − 1) = 5 × 24 = 120
- R = 1 − (6 × 6.5) ÷ 120 = 1 − 39 ÷ 120 = 1 − 0.325 = **0.675**

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Two tie slips recur. The first is ranking tied items 1, 2 instead of giving both the average
    1.5 — the average keeps the rank total correct. The second is forgetting the correction term
    altogether, or adding only one term when several tied groups exist. Add one
    <strong>(m³ − m) ÷ 12</strong> for <em>each</em> group, in <em>either</em> series, and add them
    all to Σd² before dividing.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. Probable error and the significance of r

An r computed from a sample is only an estimate of the true correlation. The **probable error**
(PE) measures how far the sample r might reasonably sit from the truth:

<div class="formula">PE = 0.6745 × (1 − r²) ÷ √n</div>

The constant 0.6745 is fixed; n is the number of pairs and r is the sample coefficient. Two uses
follow, and both are examinable.

**The likely range of the true correlation.** The true value is expected to lie within one probable
error either side of the sample r, that is between r − PE and r + PE.

**A rough test of significance**, using the rule of thumb built on PE:

- If r is **less than PE**, correlation is treated as **not significant** — it could easily be zero.
- If r is **more than six times PE** (r &gt; 6 × PE), correlation is treated as **significant** — it
  is very unlikely to be zero.
- Between those, no firm verdict from PE alone.

**Worked illustration.** A sample of 64 pairs gives r = 0.6.

- 1 − r² = 1 − 0.36 = 0.64; √n = √64 = 8
- PE = 0.6745 × 0.64 ÷ 8 = 0.6745 × 0.08 = **0.05396**
- The true correlation is expected between 0.6 − 0.054 = 0.546 and 0.6 + 0.054 = 0.654.
- 6 × PE = 0.324, and r = 0.6 is well above it, so the correlation is significant.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Probable error shrinks in two ways: as r moves toward ±1, the (1 − r²) factor falls toward zero,
    and as the sample grows, √n in the denominator rises. So a strong correlation on a large sample
    has a tiny probable error and is easy to call significant, while a weak correlation on a small
    sample has a large one. If a question hands you r and n, the PE is one line; if it hands you PE
    and n and asks for r, rearrange to solve for r².
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. Regression: the two lines

Correlation stops at "how strong". **Regression** goes further and gives an equation to
**estimate** one variable from the other. Because you can estimate either way, there are **two**
regression lines for a pair of variables.

- The **regression line of y on x** estimates y from a known x. Its equation is
  <div class="formula">y − ȳ = b_yx (x − x̄)</div>
- The **regression line of x on y** estimates x from a known y. Its equation is
  <div class="formula">x − x̄ = b_xy (y − ȳ)</div>

Both lines are drawn by the method of **least squares**: the line of y on x is the straight line
that makes the total of the squared vertical gaps between the dots and the line as small as
possible, and the line of x on y minimises the squared horizontal gaps. They are different lines
because they minimise different gaps — one measures error in y, the other error in x.

Two facts about the pair of lines are heavily tested:

1. **Both lines pass through the point of means (x̄, ȳ).** Whatever the data, the two lines cross at
   the mean of x and the mean of y. So if you are given the two line equations, solving them
   simultaneously gives you x̄ and ȳ at once.
2. **The lines coincide only when r = ±1, and are at right angles when r = 0.** Perfect correlation
   collapses the two lines into one; zero correlation makes them perpendicular, one horizontal and
   one vertical. The angle between the two lines is a picture of the strength of correlation: the
   closer they are, the stronger the relationship.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Use the right line for the job. To estimate y from a given x, use the line of <strong>y on
    x</strong>; to estimate x from a given y, use the line of <strong>x on y</strong>. Using the
    wrong line to predict is a real and costly error, because the two lines have different slopes and
    give different answers. The only time it does not matter is r = ±1, when the two lines are the
    same line.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. The regression coefficients

The slope of each line is its **regression coefficient**. The coefficient of the line of y on x is
b_yx, and of the line of x on y is b_xy. Each is built from the same five sums as r:

<div class="formula">b_yx = [nΣxy − (Σx)(Σy)] ÷ [nΣx² − (Σx)²]</div>
<div class="formula">b_xy = [nΣxy − (Σx)(Σy)] ÷ [nΣy² − (Σy)²]</div>

Notice the numerators are identical — the very same numerator as r — and only the denominators
differ: b_yx divides by the x-spread, b_xy by the y-spread. Written with covariance and variances:

<div class="formula">b_yx = Cov(x, y) ÷ σx²   ·   b_xy = Cov(x, y) ÷ σy²</div>

and written with r and the standard deviations, the form most often quoted:

<div class="formula">b_yx = r × (σy ÷ σx)   ·   b_xy = r × (σx ÷ σy)</div>

**Worked illustration.** For the five pairs of section 3 (x = 1..5, y = 3, 2, 5, 6, 4) the shared
numerator was 30, and nΣx² − (Σx)² = 50 while nΣy² − (Σy)² = 50, so here b_yx = 30 ÷ 50 = 0.6 and
b_xy = 30 ÷ 50 = 0.6. When the two spreads differ, the two coefficients differ too — that is the
usual case.

Three properties of the regression coefficients are examinable:

1. **Both coefficients carry the same sign**, and it is the sign of r. If both are positive, r is
   positive; if both negative, r is negative. They can never have opposite signs, because they share
   a numerator.
2. **Their product is r²**, so b_yx × b_xy = r². This is the anchor of the next section.
3. **They are affected by change of scale but not by change of origin.** Unlike r, a regression
   coefficient does carry units, so rescaling a variable rescales its coefficient. Shifting the
   origin, though, leaves both coefficients unchanged.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Do not mix up the two denominators. b_yx (the y-on-x slope) divides the common numerator by
    <strong>nΣx² − (Σx)²</strong>, the x term; b_xy divides by the <strong>y</strong> term. Swapping
    them silently switches the two lines. A memory hook: the coefficient of "y on x" is divided by
    the spread of the variable you are given, which is x.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Linking r and the regression coefficients

Because b_yx × b_xy = r², the correlation coefficient is the **geometric mean** of the two
regression coefficients:

<div class="formula">r = ±√(b_yx × b_xy)</div>

The sign is not decided by the square root; it is the **common sign of the two coefficients**. If
both b's are positive, take the positive root; if both are negative, take the negative root. This
single formula is a favourite of the examiner, because it lets a question give you the two slopes
and ask for the correlation without any data table at all.

**Worked illustration.** A firm's regression coefficients are b_yx = 0.8 and b_xy = 0.45.

- Product = 0.8 × 0.45 = 0.36
- r = +√0.36 = **0.6** (positive, because both coefficients are positive)

Two useful consistency checks fall out of this link:

- **Neither b may exceed the reciprocal of the other beyond r² = 1.** Because r² = b_yx × b_xy ≤ 1,
  the product of the two coefficients can never be more than 1. If a question offers b_yx = 1.5 and
  b_xy = 0.9, their product is 1.35 &gt; 1, which is impossible — one of the figures is wrong. Use
  this to reject an impossible option instantly.
- **One large and one small.** Since the product is at most 1, if one coefficient is large the other
  must be small. A slope of 1.6 for y on x forces the x-on-y slope to be at most 0.625.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A related property: the arithmetic mean of the two regression coefficients is never less than r,
    that is (b_yx + b_xy) ÷ 2 ≥ r. It follows from the fact that the arithmetic mean of two positive
    numbers is at least their geometric mean. You will rarely compute with it, but it is a clean
    concept-question fact, and it double-checks a suspicious pair of slopes.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. Predicting with a regression line

Prediction is the practical payoff. Build the correct line, substitute the known value, read off the
estimate.

**Worked illustration — estimating y from x.** For the five pairs of section 3, x̄ = 3, ȳ = 4 and
b_yx = 0.6, so the line of y on x is

<div class="formula">y − 4 = 0.6 (x − 3), that is y = 0.6x + 2.2</div>

To estimate y when x = 10: y = 0.6 × 10 + 2.2 = 6 + 2.2 = **8.2**.

**Worked illustration — estimating x from y.** For the same data, b_xy = 0.6, so the line of x on y
is x − 3 = 0.6 (y − 4), that is x = 0.6y + 0.6. To estimate x when y = 9: x = 0.6 × 9 + 0.6 = **6.0**.

Two points guard against error. First, use the line that predicts the variable you want: to get y,
use y on x. Second, an estimate far outside the range of the observed data is unreliable, because
the straight-line rule was only fitted inside that range; the arithmetic still runs, but the answer
should be treated with caution.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    When a question hands you the two <em>regression equations</em> rather than the data, you can
    still do everything. Solve the two equations together to get x̄ and ȳ (they meet at the means).
    Identify which equation is y on x by writing each in slope form and checking that the product of
    the two slopes does not exceed 1 — the correct pairing is the one that gives r² ≤ 1. Then read
    the slopes as b_yx and b_xy, take r = ±√(b_yx × b_xy), and predict from the appropriate line.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 11. Correlation versus regression

The two ideas share their sums but answer different questions, and keeping them apart is worth a
mark or two on its own.

| | Correlation (r) | Regression (b_yx, b_xy) |
|---|---|---|
| Question answered | How strongly do x and y move together? | How do we estimate one from the other? |
| Output | One pure number, −1 to +1 | Two lines / two slopes, in units |
| Symmetry | One value; r of x, y = r of y, x | Two values; b_yx ≠ b_xy in general |
| Units | None (a pure number) | Carries units of the variables |
| Change of origin | No effect | No effect |
| Change of scale | No effect (sign may flip) | Rescales the coefficient |
| Cause and effect | Implies neither | Treats x as the estimator of y (or vice versa) |

Three connections tie the two together and are all fair game:

- r and the two b's always share the **same sign**.
- r is the **geometric mean** of b_yx and b_xy.
- Both regression lines pass through **(x̄, ȳ)**, and they coincide exactly when r = ±1 and are
  perpendicular when r = 0.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The headline error is treating a regression coefficient as if it were the correlation
    coefficient, or the reverse. b_yx can be larger than 1 (a slope has no ceiling), but r never
    exceeds 1. If an option offers a "correlation" of 1.6, it is wrong on its face; if it offers a
    "regression coefficient" of 1.6, that is perfectly possible. Read which of the two the question
    is asking for.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 12. Answering under negative marking

Paper 3 is entirely objective: a correct answer earns 1 mark, a wrong answer costs 0.25 mark, and a
blank scores zero. That arithmetic should shape how you treat a question you cannot finish.

A blind guess among four options expects (1 ÷ 4)(+1) + (3 ÷ 4)(−0.25) = 0.25 − 0.1875 = **+0.0625**
marks — barely positive, nearly worthless. Eliminate one option and the expectation rises to
+0.1667; eliminate two and it reaches +0.375. The value of a guess comes almost entirely from the
options you can rule out first.

This chapter hands you eliminations for free. Any option offering a correlation outside −1 to +1 is
impossible. Any pair of regression coefficients whose product exceeds 1 is impossible. A correlation
whose sign disagrees with the sign of the regression coefficients is impossible. A predicted value
that ignores the intercept, or uses the wrong line, is usually one of the planted distractors. Rule
those out, then choose. Attempt what you can narrow to two; leave truly blank only what you cannot
touch at all.

    Σx² = 1+4+9+16+25 = 55 · Σy² = 9+4+25+36+16 = 90 · n = 5

  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Numerator and denominator</div>
    Numerator = nΣxy − (Σx)(Σy) = 5×66 − 15×20 = 330 − 300 = 30<br />

  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Substitute</div>
    r = 30 ÷ √(50 × 50) = 30 ÷ 50 = <strong>0.6</strong><br />
    Positive and moderate: y rises with x but not tightly. Both bracket values are positive, as they
    must be, so the sign came from the numerator.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — r by the change-of-origin short form (moderate)">
  <p>
    Six months of data give x = 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 and y = 5, 4, 9, 8, 12. Find the coefficient of
    correlation, and confirm that subtracting a constant from each series leaves it unchanged.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Raw sums</div>
    Σx = 30 · Σy = 38 · Σxy = 10+16+54+64+120 = 264 · Σx² = 220 · Σy² = 330 · n = 5<br />
    Numerator = 5×264 − 30×38 = 1320 − 1140 = 180
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Denominator and r</div>
    nΣx² − (Σx)² = 1100 − 900 = 200 · nΣy² − (Σy)² = 1650 − 1444 = 206<br />
    r = 180 ÷ √(200 × 206) = 180 ÷ √41200 = 180 ÷ 202.98 = <strong>0.8868</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — The origin does not matter</div>
    Replace x by (x − 6) and y by (y − 8): the deviations, and therefore the numerator 180 and both
    bracket values 200 and 206, are exactly the same, because a change of origin cancels in every
    term. So r stays 0.8868. This is why you may shift large data to small numbers before computing.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — effect of change of scale on r (moderate)">
  <p>
    A set of paired data has correlation r = 0.6. Each x value is now doubled, and each y value is
    replaced by (40 − y). What is the new correlation coefficient?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Doubling x</div>
    Multiplying every x by +2 is a change of scale by a positive number. It does not change r. So far
    r remains 0.6 in size and sign.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Replacing y by (40 − y)</div>
    This is (−1)×y + 40: a change of origin (the +40, no effect) and a change of scale by −1. A
    negative scale factor keeps the size of r but reverses its sign.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Combine</div>
    Size unchanged at 0.6; one negative scale factor flips the sign once. New r = <strong>−0.6</strong>.<br />
    Had both transformations used negative factors, the sign would flip twice and return to +0.6.
    Count the negative scale factors: an odd number flips the sign, an even number does not.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — rank correlation without ties (easy)">
  <p>
    Two judges rank seven paintings. The rank differences for the seven paintings are
    d = −2, +1, −1, +2, −1, +1, 0. Find the rank correlation coefficient.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Square and total the differences</div>
    d² = 4, 1, 1, 4, 1, 1, 0 · Σd² = 12 · n = 7
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Substitute</div>
    n(n² − 1) = 7 × (49 − 1) = 7 × 48 = 336<br />
    R = 1 − (6 × 12) ÷ 336 = 1 − 72 ÷ 336 = 1 − 0.2143 = <strong>0.7857</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Reading it</div>
    R is positive and fairly high: the two judges broadly agree on the ordering. Had every rank
    matched, Σd² would be 0 and R = 1; had the orders been exactly reversed, R would be −1.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — rank correlation with tied ranks (hard)">
  <p>
    Two examiners mark five candidates. Examiner X awards 50, 70, 60, 60, 80; Examiner Y awards
    55, 60, 40, 55, 60. Find the rank correlation coefficient, correcting for ties.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Rank each examiner (highest mark = rank 1)</div>
    X: 80→1, 70→2, the two 60s occupy places 3 and 4 → 3.5 each, 50→5. X ranks = 5, 2, 3.5, 3.5, 1.<br />
    Y: the two 60s occupy places 1 and 2 → 1.5 each, the two 55s occupy places 3 and 4 → 3.5 each,
    40→5. Y ranks = 3.5, 1.5, 5, 3.5, 1.5.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Differences and their squares</div>
    d = 5−3.5, 2−1.5, 3.5−5, 3.5−3.5, 1−1.5 = 1.5, 0.5, −1.5, 0, −0.5<br />
    d² = 2.25, 0.25, 2.25, 0, 0.25 · Σd² = 5
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Tie correction</div>
    One tied pair in X (m = 2): (2³ − 2) ÷ 12 = 6 ÷ 12 = 0.5<br />
    Two tied pairs in Y (m = 2 each): 0.5 + 0.5 = 1.0<br />
    Total correction = 1.5, so corrected Σd² = 5 + 1.5 = 6.5
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — Substitute</div>
    n(n² − 1) = 5 × 24 = 120<br />
    R = 1 − (6 × 6.5) ÷ 120 = 1 − 39 ÷ 120 = 1 − 0.325 = <strong>0.675</strong><br />
    Skipping the 1.5 correction would have given 1 − 30 ÷ 120 = 0.75, an option the examiner will
    certainly offer.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 6 — probable error and significance (moderate)">
  <p>
    A correlation coefficient of 0.6 is found from a sample of 64 pairs. Find the probable error,
    state the likely range of the true correlation, and decide whether the correlation is
    significant.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Probable error</div>
    1 − r² = 1 − 0.36 = 0.64 · √n = √64 = 8<br />
    PE = 0.6745 × 0.64 ÷ 8 = 0.6745 × 0.08 = <strong>0.05396</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Likely range of the true correlation</div>
    r ± PE = 0.6 ± 0.054, so the true value is expected between <strong>0.546 and 0.654</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Significance</div>
    6 × PE = 6 × 0.05396 = 0.3238. Since r = 0.6 is greater than 6 × PE, the correlation is
    <strong>significant</strong>. It is also far above PE itself, so it could not plausibly be zero.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 7 — both regression coefficients and r from data (moderate)">
  <p>
    For eight pairs the sums are Σx = 56, Σy = 40, Σxy = 364, Σx² = 524, Σy² = 256, with n = 8.
    Find b_yx, b_xy and the coefficient of correlation.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — The shared numerator and the two spreads</div>
    Numerator = nΣxy − (Σx)(Σy) = 8×364 − 56×40 = 2912 − 2240 = 672<br />
    nΣx² − (Σx)² = 8×524 − 56² = 4192 − 3136 = 1056<br />
    nΣy² − (Σy)² = 8×256 − 40² = 2048 − 1600 = 448
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — The two regression coefficients</div>
    b_yx = 672 ÷ 1056 = <strong>0.6364</strong> · b_xy = 672 ÷ 448 = <strong>1.5</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — r as the geometric mean</div>
    r = +√(b_yx × b_xy) = √(0.6364 × 1.5) = √0.9546 = <strong>0.9770</strong><br />
    Positive, because both coefficients are positive. Directly from the sums,
    r = 672 ÷ √(1056 × 448) = 672 ÷ 687.8 = 0.9770 — the two routes agree.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 8 — building the two lines and predicting (moderate)">
  <p>
    Using again x = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and y = 3, 2, 5, 6, 4 (means x̄ = 3, ȳ = 4, both regression
    coefficients 0.6), write the two regression lines, estimate y when x = 10, and estimate x when
    y = 9.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — The two lines</div>
    y on x: y − 4 = 0.6(x − 3) → y = 0.6x + 2.2<br />
    x on y: x − 3 = 0.6(y − 4) → x = 0.6y + 0.6
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Estimate y from x = 10</div>
    Use the line of y on x: y = 0.6 × 10 + 2.2 = 6 + 2.2 = <strong>8.2</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Estimate x from y = 9</div>
    Use the line of x on y: x = 0.6 × 9 + 0.6 = 5.4 + 0.6 = <strong>6.0</strong><br />
    x = 10 sits outside the observed range 1–5, so the first estimate is an extrapolation and should
    be read with caution; the second is a mild extrapolation too. The arithmetic is sound; the
    reliability is a separate matter.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 9 — means and r from two regression equations (hard)">
  <p>
    Two regression lines are 8x − 10y + 66 = 0 and 40x − 18y − 214 = 0. Find the mean of x, the mean
    of y, and the coefficient of correlation.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — The means are where the lines cross</div>
    Solve 8x − 10y = −66 and 40x − 18y = 214 together.<br />
    From the first, 4x − 5y = −33; from the second, 20x − 9y = 107. Eliminate x:
    5×(4x − 5y) gives 20x − 25y = −165, subtract to get −16y = −272, so <strong>ȳ = 17</strong>,
    and then 4x = 5×17 − 33 = 52, so <strong>x̄ = 13</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Identify the two lines by testing r² ≤ 1</div>
    Suppose 8x − 10y + 66 = 0 is y on x: 10y = 8x + 66 → b_yx = 0.8.<br />
    Suppose 40x − 18y − 214 = 0 is x on y: 40x = 18y + 214 → b_xy = 18 ÷ 40 = 0.45.<br />
    Product = 0.8 × 0.45 = 0.36 ≤ 1, so the assumption is valid.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — The correlation coefficient</div>
    r = +√(b_yx × b_xy) = √0.36 = <strong>0.6</strong>, positive because both coefficients are
    positive.<br />
    Had the other pairing been tried, the product would have exceeded 1 — an impossible r² — which is
    the built-in check that you have matched each equation to the right line.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

- **Squaring the sum where the sum of squares belonged.** The bracket is nΣx² − (Σx)², n times the
  sum of squares minus the square of the sum. Each bracket must come out positive.
- **Reading r as larger than 1.** The correlation coefficient never leaves −1 to +1. An answer or an
  option outside that range is wrong on its face; a *regression coefficient*, by contrast, may exceed 1.
- **Confusing the two regression denominators.** b_yx divides the common numerator by the x term,
  nΣx² − (Σx)²; b_xy divides by the y term. Swapping them switches the two lines.
- **Taking the wrong sign on r = ±√(b_yx × b_xy).** The square root does not fix the sign; the common
  sign of the two regression coefficients does. Two negative slopes give a negative r.
- **Accepting an impossible pair of slopes.** If b_yx × b_xy exceeds 1, the pair cannot be genuine,
  because that product is r² and r² can be at most 1.
- **Predicting with the wrong line.** To estimate y use y on x; to estimate x use x on y. The two
  lines have different slopes and give different answers except when r = ±1.
- **Forgetting the tie correction in rank correlation**, or adding only one term when several tied
  groups exist. Add (m³ − m) ÷ 12 for every tied group, in either series.
- **Ranking tied items 1, 2 instead of 1.5, 1.5.** Tied items share the average of the positions
  they occupy, which keeps the rank total correct.
- **Mixing up which variable is the origin shift and which the scale.** A change of origin never
  touches r or the regression coefficients; a change of scale rescales a regression coefficient and
  can flip the sign of r if the factor is negative.
- **Treating correlation as causation.** A high r says two series move together, nothing about one
  causing the other; the correlation may be spurious.
- **Using r where a regression coefficient was asked, or the reverse.** They share a numerator but
  answer different questions and, in general, take different values.

## One-page revision summary

**Symbols.** x, y = the two variables · n = number of pairs · Σx, Σy, Σxy, Σx², Σy² = the five raw
sums · x̄, ȳ = the two means · σx, σy = the two standard deviations · Cov(x, y) = covariance ·
r = correlation coefficient · R = rank correlation · b_yx, b_xy = regression coefficients ·
PE = probable error · d = rank difference · m = size of a tied group.

**Correlation**

<div class="formula">r = Cov(x, y) ÷ (σx × σy)</div>
<div class="formula">r = [nΣxy − (Σx)(Σy)] ÷ √([nΣx² − (Σx)²] × [nΣy² − (Σy)²])</div>
<div class="formula">Always −1 ≤ r ≤ 1 · pure number · unaffected by change of origin and scale (a negative scale factor flips the sign)</div>

**Rank correlation**

<div class="formula">R = 1 − 6Σd² ÷ [n(n² − 1)]</div>
<div class="formula">With ties: use corrected Σd² = Σd² + Σ (m³ − m) ÷ 12, one term per tied group, and average the tied ranks</div>

**Probable error**

<div class="formula">PE = 0.6745 × (1 − r²) ÷ √n · true r expected in r ± PE · significant if r &gt; 6 × PE · not significant if r &lt; PE</div>

**Regression**

<div class="formula">Line of y on x: y − ȳ = b_yx (x − x̄) · Line of x on y: x − x̄ = b_xy (y − ȳ)</div>
<div class="formula">b_yx = [nΣxy − (Σx)(Σy)] ÷ [nΣx² − (Σx)²] = r × (σy ÷ σx)</div>
<div class="formula">b_xy = [nΣxy − (Σx)(Σy)] ÷ [nΣy² − (Σy)²] = r × (σx ÷ σy)</div>
<div class="formula">b_yx × b_xy = r² · r = ±√(b_yx × b_xy) with the common sign of the two coefficients</div>
<div class="formula">Both lines pass through (x̄, ȳ) · they coincide when r = ±1 · they are perpendicular when r = 0</div>

**Directions to remember.** r and both regression coefficients share one sign · r never exceeds 1
but a regression coefficient may · a change of origin touches nothing, a change of scale rescales a
regression coefficient and can flip the sign of r · use y on x to predict y and x on y to predict x
· solving the two regression equations together gives the means · correlation measures strength,
regression estimates a value.
