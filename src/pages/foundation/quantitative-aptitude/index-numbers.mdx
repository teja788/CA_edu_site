A homemaker in Pune keeps every grocery bill. In 2015 her monthly basket of rice, oil, pulses and
milk cost ₹ 8,000. The same basket, bought in the same shops in the exact same quantities, cost
₹ 10,400 by 2024. She has bought nothing extra and nothing better; only the price tags have moved.
Her cost of living has risen to 130 for every 100 it once was, and that single number, 130, is an
index number. It says nothing about any one price. It measures how a whole bundle of prices has
moved together, against a starting point fixed at 100.

That is the entire idea of the chapter. Prices, wages, output and share values all change at once and
by different amounts, and no single figure among them describes the crowd. An index number is a
device that reduces the crowd to one number by comparing a later situation with an earlier
**base** situation set equal to 100. Economists call it a barometer because, like a barometer, it
does not weigh anything directly — it registers pressure, the net upward or downward push of many
prices at the same time.

Paper 3 is fully objective, so you will not derive a formula in the exam. You will be handed a small
table of prices and quantities and asked for one index to two decimal places. This chapter takes each
formula in turn, builds it from the table, and shows exactly which sum — Σp₁q₀ or Σp₀q₁ — belongs on
top. Get the four sums right and every weighted index in the paper is one division away.

## 1. What an index number is, and what it is used for

An **index number** is a ratio, expressed as a percentage, that measures the relative change in a
variable or a group of variables between a **current period** (subscript 1) and a **base period**
(subscript 0). The base period value is always fixed at **100**, so an index of 130 means a 30% rise
over the base and an index of 92 means an 8% fall.

Three features separate an index number from an ordinary average:

1. **It is expressed relative to a base.** The base period is chosen as normal — not a boom, not a
   slump — and set to 100. Every later figure is read against it.
2. **It is a pure number.** Because it is a ratio of money values to money values, or of prices to
   prices, the units cancel. An index of 130 carries no rupee sign and no kilogram.
3. **It measures net change in a group.** A single price uses a **price relative**; an index number
   summarises many prices, or many quantities, at once.

The notation used throughout is **P₀₁** for a price index of period 1 on base period 0, and **Q₀₁**
for the matching quantity index. Read the subscripts left to right as "from 0 to 1".

Where index numbers are used at this level:

- **Cost of living / consumer price index** — to measure how the cost of a fixed basket of goods
  changes, and so to revise wages, pensions and dearness allowance.
- **Wholesale price index** — to track the general price level and gauge inflation.
- **Deflating** — to strip the price effect out of a money figure and recover its real value, so a
  money wage becomes a real wage.
- **Index of industrial production** — a quantity index that tracks output.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Every subscript in this chapter is either 0 (base) or 1 (current). A quantity carries a
    <strong>price subscript</strong> and a <strong>quantity subscript</strong> once it enters a sum:
    Σp₁q₀ means "current prices weighted by base quantities". Reading the two subscripts correctly is
    the whole skill in weighted index numbers, so slow down and name each sum before you add it.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. Price relatives

A **price relative** measures the change in the price of a single commodity. It is the current price
as a percentage of the base price:

<div class="formula">Price relative = (p₁ ÷ p₀) × 100</div>

If a litre of oil cost ₹ 120 in the base year and ₹ 150 now, its price relative is
150 ÷ 120 × 100 = 125, a 25% rise. A relative below 100 signals a fall: a price that dropped from
₹ 80 to ₹ 72 has a relative of 90.

The price relative is the atom from which the simpler index numbers are built. A **quantity
relative** does the same job for a quantity, q₁ ÷ q₀ × 100, and a **value relative** compares total
money values, p₁q₁ ÷ p₀q₀ × 100. Everything in the next few sections is a way of averaging price
relatives across several commodities.

## 3. Simple aggregate method

The crudest index adds the current prices, adds the base prices, and divides:

<div class="formula">Simple aggregate price index = (Σp₁ ÷ Σp₀) × 100</div>

For four commodities priced ₹ 25, 20, 15 and 40 in the base year and ₹ 30, 24, 20 and 46 now, the
index is (30 + 24 + 20 + 46) ÷ (25 + 20 + 15 + 40) × 100 = 120 ÷ 100 × 100 = **120**.

It is simple, and that is its only virtue. Two faults make it unreliable:

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The simple aggregate index is <strong>distorted by the units in which prices are quoted</strong>.
    Quote wheat per quintal instead of per kilogram and its price is a hundred times larger, so it
    swamps the total and the index bends toward wheat, though nothing real has changed. It also gives
    every commodity <strong>equal importance</strong> — a costly item bought once a year counts the
    same as a cheap item bought daily. Both faults are cured by using relatives and by weighting.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Simple average of price relatives

Instead of adding the prices, add the **price relatives** and average them. Because each relative is
already a pure number that starts at 100, the units problem disappears:

<div class="formula">Simple average of price relatives = Σ(p₁ ÷ p₀ × 100) ÷ N, where N is the number of commodities</div>

For four commodities whose prices move 40 to 50, 20 to 25, 50 to 60 and 25 to 30, the relatives are
125, 125, 120 and 120, and the index is (125 + 125 + 120 + 120) ÷ 4 = 490 ÷ 4 = **122.5**.

This cures the units defect — a relative does not care whether wheat is priced per kilogram or per
quintal. It does **not** cure the weighting defect: a daily necessity and an annual luxury still
count alike, because every relative carries the weight 1. The arithmetic mean is the usual choice,
though the geometric mean of the relatives is sometimes preferred because it treats a rise and the
matching fall symmetrically.

## 5. Weighted index numbers — the four sums

Real commodities are not equally important, so a good index **weights** each price by a quantity that
reflects how much of the commodity is traded. Every weighted price index in the syllabus is built
from the same four sums, so compute all four once and read the formulas off them:

<div class="formula">Σp₀q₀ · Σp₁q₀ · Σp₀q₁ · Σp₁q₁</div>

Read each sum by its two subscripts. **Σp₁q₀** is current prices on base quantities; **Σp₀q₁** is
base prices on current quantities; **Σp₀q₀** and **Σp₁q₁** are the base-year and current-year total
values. Lay a table out in these four columns and the rest is division.

| Commodity | p₀ | q₀ | p₁ | q₁ | p₀q₀ | p₁q₀ | p₀q₁ | p₁q₁ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 5 | 50 | 8 | 60 | 250 | 400 | 300 | 480 |
| B | 8 | 40 | 10 | 30 | 320 | 400 | 240 | 300 |
| C | 12 | 20 | 15 | 25 | 240 | 300 | 300 | 375 |
| D | 4 | 100 | 5 | 90 | 400 | 500 | 360 | 450 |
| **Σ** | | | | | **1210** | **1600** | **1200** | **1605** |

This one table drives the next five formulas. Keep it in view.

## 6. Laspeyres index — base-year weights

The **Laspeyres** price index weights every price by the **base-year** quantity q₀. It answers: what
would the base-year basket cost now, against what it cost then?

<div class="formula">Laspeyres index (P₀₁) = (Σp₁q₀ ÷ Σp₀q₀) × 100</div>

From the table, Laspeyres = 1600 ÷ 1210 × 100 = **132.23**. The weights q₀ are fixed once, in the
base year, so a whole series of yearly Laspeyres indices uses the same basket — an advantage for
comparison and for data collection. Its bias runs one way: because consumers move away from goods
that become dear, holding the old quantities fixed **overstates** the true rise in the cost of
living, so Laspeyres tends to read a little high.

## 7. Paasche index — current-year weights

The **Paasche** price index weights every price by the **current-year** quantity q₁. It answers:
what does the current basket cost now, against what the same current basket would have cost in the
base year?

<div class="formula">Paasche index (P₀₁) = (Σp₁q₁ ÷ Σp₀q₁) × 100</div>

From the table, Paasche = 1605 ÷ 1200 × 100 = **133.75**. The weights change every year, so each
Paasche index needs fresh quantity data — a cost. Its bias runs the other way: current quantities
already reflect the switch away from dear goods, so Paasche tends to **understate** the rise and read
a little low.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A memory hook that never fails: <strong>Laspeyres uses the past</strong> (base-year q₀),
    <strong>Paasche the present</strong> (current-year q₁) — both begin with the same letter. The
    only difference between the two formulas is the quantity subscript. The prices are always
    current on top and base below; it is the weight that switches.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. Fisher's ideal index — the geometric mean of the two

Laspeyres reads high and Paasche reads low, so the truth sits between them. **Fisher's ideal index**
takes the **geometric mean** of the two — the square root of their product:

<div class="formula">Fisher's index (P₀₁) = square root of (Laspeyres × Paasche)</div>
<div class="formula">Equivalently, Fisher = square root of [(Σp₁q₀ ÷ Σp₀q₀) × (Σp₁q₁ ÷ Σp₀q₁)] × 100</div>

From the table, Fisher = square root of (132.2314 × 133.75) = square root of 17685.75 = **132.99**.
It is called **ideal** for two reasons: it splits the difference between the upward Laspeyres bias
and the downward Paasche bias by using both base and current weights, and — the deeper reason — it
is the only one of these formulas that passes **both** the time-reversal and the factor-reversal
tests of section 10.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Fisher is the <strong>geometric</strong> mean of Laspeyres and Paasche, not the arithmetic mean.
    The arithmetic mean (Laspeyres + Paasche) ÷ 2 is a different index — the Dorbish-Bowley of
    section 9 — and it fails the reversal tests. Take the square root of the product, never half the
    sum, when the question says Fisher.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Marshall-Edgeworth and Dorbish-Bowley

Two more weighted indices combine base and current weights, each in its own way.

The **Marshall-Edgeworth** index weights each price by the **sum** of the base and current
quantities, (q₀ + q₁):

<div class="formula">Marshall-Edgeworth index = [Σp₁(q₀ + q₁) ÷ Σp₀(q₀ + q₁)] × 100 = [(Σp₁q₀ + Σp₁q₁) ÷ (Σp₀q₀ + Σp₀q₁)] × 100</div>

From the same four sums, Marshall-Edgeworth = (1600 + 1605) ÷ (1210 + 1200) × 100 =
3205 ÷ 2410 × 100 = **132.99**. It is a close and honest approximation to Fisher, and it too uses
both sets of weights.

The **Dorbish-Bowley** (also written Drobish-Bowley) index is simply the **arithmetic mean** of
Laspeyres and Paasche:

<div class="formula">Dorbish-Bowley index = (Laspeyres + Paasche) ÷ 2</div>

Here Dorbish-Bowley = (132.2314 + 133.75) ÷ 2 = **132.99**. Notice that Fisher, Marshall-Edgeworth
and Dorbish-Bowley all land near the same value — that is expected, because all three sit between the
same Laspeyres and Paasche. What separates them is the reversal tests, which only Fisher passes.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A single table of four column-sums (Σp₀q₀, Σp₁q₀, Σp₀q₁, Σp₁q₁) answers Laspeyres, Paasche,
    Fisher, Marshall-Edgeworth and Dorbish-Bowley all at once. Build the table first, total the four
    columns, and then no index costs more than one division and, for Fisher, one square root. Never
    restart the arithmetic for each formula.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. The tests of a good index number

A trustworthy index should behave consistently. Two tests, both examinable, check that.

**The time-reversal test.** Reversing the roles of the two periods should reverse the index exactly.
If the index from 0 to 1 is P₀₁ and from 1 to 0 is P₁₀, both taken as **ratios** (drop the ×100),
their product should equal 1:

<div class="formula">Time-reversal test: P₀₁ × P₁₀ = 1</div>

**The factor-reversal test.** A price index and the matching quantity index, multiplied together,
should equal the **value ratio** — the change in total money value, because price times quantity is
value:

<div class="formula">Factor-reversal test: P₀₁ × Q₀₁ = Σp₁q₁ ÷ Σp₀q₀ (the value ratio, as a ratio)</div>

Laspeyres and Paasche each fail both tests. **Fisher passes both**, which is exactly why it earns the
name "ideal". To see the time-reversal test for Fisher, write it out: P₀₁ is the square root of
(Σp₁q₀ ÷ Σp₀q₀ × Σp₁q₁ ÷ Σp₀q₁), and P₁₀ is the same expression with the periods swapped, the square
root of (Σp₀q₁ ÷ Σp₁q₁ × Σp₀q₀ ÷ Σp₁q₀). Multiply them and every sum cancels against its partner,
leaving 1. The factor-reversal test cancels the same way and leaves Σp₁q₁ ÷ Σp₀q₀. For the table in
section 5 that value ratio is 1605 ÷ 1210 = 1.3264, and Fisher's price index times Fisher's quantity
index reproduces it to the last decimal.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    In both tests the indices are used as <strong>ratios, without the ×100</strong>. A student who
    keeps the hundreds gets P₀₁ × P₁₀ = 10,000 instead of 1, and panics. Strip the ×100 before you
    multiply: an index of 132.23 enters the test as 1.3223.
  </p>
</Callout>

A third test, the **circular test** (P₀₁ × P₁₂ × P₂₀ = 1), is passed only by the simple aggregate and
by fixed-weight indices; Fisher fails it. At Foundation level, remember the headline: Fisher passes
time-reversal and factor-reversal, and is the only one of the standard weighted indices to pass both.

## 11. Quantity and value index numbers

Everything so far measured price. A **quantity index** measures how quantities have moved, holding
prices as the weights. Just swap the roles of p and q in the formulas:

<div class="formula">Laspeyres quantity index = (Σq₁p₀ ÷ Σq₀p₀) × 100 · Paasche quantity index = (Σq₁p₁ ÷ Σq₀p₁) × 100</div>
<div class="formula">Fisher quantity index = square root of (Laspeyres quantity × Paasche quantity)</div>

For the section 5 table, the Laspeyres quantity index is Σp₀q₁ ÷ Σp₀q₀ × 100 = 1200 ÷ 1210 × 100 =
**99.17** — quantities barely moved, so the index sits just below 100. The Fisher quantity index is
**99.74**.

A **value index** needs no weights at all, because value already combines price and quantity:

<div class="formula">Value index = (Σp₁q₁ ÷ Σp₀q₀) × 100</div>

Here the value index is 1605 ÷ 1210 × 100 = **132.64**. It is the product of the Fisher price and
Fisher quantity indices (as ratios), which is the factor-reversal test read forwards.

## 12. Cost of living index — the two methods

The **cost of living index number (CLI)**, also called the **consumer price index (CPI)**, measures
the change in the cost of a fixed basket that a defined group of consumers actually buys. It is
computed by either of two methods, which give the **same** answer.

**Aggregate expenditure method.** Weight each price by the base-year quantity and take the ratio of
total expenditures — this is exactly the Laspeyres formula:

<div class="formula">Cost of living index (aggregate expenditure) = (Σp₁q₀ ÷ Σp₀q₀) × 100</div>

For a basket priced ₹ 8, 6 and 5 in the base year, bought in quantities 25, 50 and 20, and priced
₹ 10, 7 and 6 now, the index is (10×25 + 7×50 + 6×20) ÷ (8×25 + 6×50 + 5×20) × 100 =
(250 + 350 + 120) ÷ (200 + 300 + 100) × 100 = 720 ÷ 600 × 100 = **120**.

**Family budget method.** Weight each **price relative** by the value the family spends on that item,
W = p₀q₀, and take the weighted average:

<div class="formula">Cost of living index (family budget) = Σ(R × W) ÷ ΣW, where R = (p₁ ÷ p₀) × 100 and W = p₀q₀</div>

The two methods are algebraically identical, because R × W = (p₁ ÷ p₀ × 100) × p₀q₀ = 100 × p₁q₀, so
Σ(RW) ÷ ΣW = Σp₁q₀ ÷ Σp₀q₀ × 100. When a question gives you group **weights** and group **relatives**
directly — food weighted 50 with a relative of 140, and so on — the family budget form is the one to
reach for.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The family budget weight W is a <strong>value</strong> (₹ spent), not a quantity, and the R it
    multiplies is a <strong>relative</strong> (a percentage), not a price. A common slip is to weight
    the price by W or to weight the relative by the quantity. Match relative with value-weight, and
    the answer equals the aggregate-expenditure Laspeyres figure exactly.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 13. Deflating — from a money wage to a real wage

Money wages that rise while prices rise faster leave a worker worse off. **Deflating** removes the
price effect by dividing the money figure by the cost of living index and restoring the base of 100:

<div class="formula">Real wage = (Money wage ÷ Cost of living index) × 100</div>

A worker paid ₹ 18,000 a month when the cost of living index stands at 120 (base 100) has a real
wage of 18,000 ÷ 120 × 100 = **₹ 15,000** in base-year rupees. The extra ₹ 3,000 of money wage has
been entirely eaten by the 20% rise in prices; in real terms the worker is exactly where the base
year left them.

The same division measures the **purchasing power of the rupee**, which is the reciprocal of the
index, restored to a rupee:

<div class="formula">Purchasing power of the rupee = 100 ÷ Cost of living index (in rupees)</div>

When the index is 125, a rupee buys 100 ÷ 125 = **₹ 0.80** of what it bought in the base year — a
fifth of its purchasing power has gone. A **real wage index** carries the same information as a
series: real wage of the year divided by real wage of the base year, times 100.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Deflating <strong>divides</strong> by the index and multiplies by 100; it never multiplies by the
    index. If your "real" wage comes out larger than the money wage while prices are rising, you have
    multiplied where you should have divided. Real wage is always below money wage once the cost of
    living index exceeds 100.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 14. Base shifting

A series built on an old base — say 2010 = 100 — becomes hard to read once the base year is long
past. **Base shifting** re-expresses the whole series on a new, more recent base without collecting
any new data. Divide every index by the old index of the chosen new base year, and multiply by 100:

<div class="formula">Index on the new base = (Old index of the current year ÷ Old index of the new base year) × 100</div>

Take a series 2019 = 100, 2020 = 120, 2021 = 150, 2022 = 180 and shift the base to 2021. Divide every
figure by 150 and multiply by 100: 2021 becomes 100 by construction, and 2022 becomes
180 ÷ 150 × 100 = **120**. The shape of the series is untouched; only the year that reads 100 has
moved.

## 15. Splicing

**Splicing** joins two index series that overlap in one year — typically an old series that was
discontinued and a new series begun on a fresh base — into one continuous series. Multiply the new
series by the old series' value in the overlap year, and divide by 100 (the new series' base):

<div class="formula">Spliced figure = (New-series index × Old-series index in the overlap year) ÷ 100</div>

Suppose an old series ends at 2020 = 150 (on some earlier base) and a new series begins 2020 = 100,
2021 = 110, 2022 = 125. To carry the new series onto the old base, multiply each new figure by 150 ÷
100. The 2022 figure splices to 125 × 150 ÷ 100 = **187.5**, and the two series now read on one
continuous scale. Splicing a series **backwards** (old onto the new base) uses the reciprocal ratio.

## 16. Answering under negative marking

Paper 3 is entirely objective. A correct answer earns 1 mark, a wrong answer costs 0.25 mark, and a
blank scores zero. Index-number questions reward a quick sanity check before you commit.

If you guess blindly among four options you expect (1 ÷ 4)(+1) + (3 ÷ 4)(−0.25) = **+0.0625** marks —
barely positive, nearly worthless. Eliminate one option and it rises to +0.1667; eliminate two and
it reaches +0.375. The value of a guess is almost entirely in the options you can rule out, and in
this chapter two are often obvious without full computation: a Fisher index must lie **between**
Laspeyres and Paasche, a real wage must be **below** the money wage when prices have risen, and a
cost of living index above 100 means prices went **up**, so an option below 100 is wrong on its face.
Compute the four column-sums, cross-check that Fisher sits between the other two, and move on.

## Fully worked examples

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — price relatives and the simple average (easy)">
  <p>
    Four commodities had base-year prices ₹ 60, ₹ 40, ₹ 25 and ₹ 50, and current prices ₹ 72, ₹ 54,
    ₹ 30 and ₹ 55. Find each price relative and the simple average of price relatives.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Each relative is p₁ ÷ p₀ × 100</div>
    Commodity 1: 72 ÷ 60 × 100 = 120<br />
    Commodity 2: 54 ÷ 40 × 100 = 135<br />
    Commodity 3: 30 ÷ 25 × 100 = 120<br />
    Commodity 4: 55 ÷ 50 × 100 = 110
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Average the four relatives</div>
    Simple average = (120 + 135 + 120 + 110) ÷ 4 = 485 ÷ 4 = <strong>121.25</strong><br />
    Because each relative is a pure number, the units the prices were quoted in never mattered.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — Laspeyres, Paasche and Fisher from one table (moderate)">
  <p>
    For three commodities the base and current prices and quantities are: A — p₀ 6, q₀ 10, p₁ 10,
    q₁ 12; B — p₀ 2, q₀ 20, p₁ 3, q₁ 25; C — p₀ 4, q₀ 15, p₁ 5, q₁ 10. Compute the Laspeyres,
    Paasche and Fisher price indices.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Build the four column-sums</div>
    Σp₀q₀ = 6×10 + 2×20 + 4×15 = 60 + 40 + 60 = 160<br />
    Σp₁q₀ = 10×10 + 3×20 + 5×15 = 100 + 60 + 75 = 235<br />
    Σp₀q₁ = 6×12 + 2×25 + 4×10 = 72 + 50 + 40 = 162<br />
    Σp₁q₁ = 10×12 + 3×25 + 5×10 = 120 + 75 + 50 = 245
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Laspeyres and Paasche</div>
    Laspeyres = Σp₁q₀ ÷ Σp₀q₀ × 100 = 235 ÷ 160 × 100 = <strong>146.88</strong><br />
    Paasche = Σp₁q₁ ÷ Σp₀q₁ × 100 = 245 ÷ 162 × 100 = <strong>151.23</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Fisher is the geometric mean of the two</div>
    Fisher = square root of (146.875 × 151.2346) = square root of 22212.58 = <strong>149.04</strong><br />
    Fisher sits between Laspeyres and Paasche, as it must. If yours does not, one of the four sums is
    wrong.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — Marshall-Edgeworth and Dorbish-Bowley (moderate)">
  <p>
    Using the four sums of worked example 2 (Σp₀q₀ = 160, Σp₁q₀ = 235, Σp₀q₁ = 162, Σp₁q₁ = 245),
    find the Marshall-Edgeworth and the Dorbish-Bowley price indices.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Marshall-Edgeworth uses (q₀ + q₁) weights</div>
    Numerator = Σp₁q₀ + Σp₁q₁ = 235 + 245 = 480<br />
    Denominator = Σp₀q₀ + Σp₀q₁ = 160 + 162 = 322<br />
    Marshall-Edgeworth = 480 ÷ 322 × 100 = <strong>149.07</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Dorbish-Bowley is the arithmetic mean of L and P</div>
    Dorbish-Bowley = (146.875 + 151.2346) ÷ 2 = 298.1096 ÷ 2 = <strong>149.05</strong><br />
    Both land beside the Fisher figure of 149.04 — three different roads between the same two towns.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — checking the time-reversal test for Fisher (hard)">
  <p>
    Using the section 5 table (Σp₀q₀ = 1210, Σp₁q₀ = 1600, Σp₀q₁ = 1200, Σp₁q₁ = 1605), show that
    Fisher's price index satisfies the time-reversal test, P₀₁ × P₁₀ = 1.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Fisher forwards, as a ratio</div>
    P₀₁ = square root of (Σp₁q₀ ÷ Σp₀q₀ × Σp₁q₁ ÷ Σp₀q₁)<br />
    = square root of (1600 ÷ 1210 × 1605 ÷ 1200) = square root of (1.322314 × 1.337500) = 1.329885
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Fisher backwards, periods swapped</div>
    P₁₀ = square root of (Σp₀q₁ ÷ Σp₁q₁ × Σp₀q₀ ÷ Σp₁q₀)<br />
    = square root of (1200 ÷ 1605 × 1210 ÷ 1600) = square root of (0.747664 × 0.756250) = 0.751945
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Multiply the two ratios</div>
    P₀₁ × P₁₀ = 1.329885 × 0.751945 = <strong>1.0000</strong><br />
    The product is exactly 1, so Fisher passes. Note that both indices entered as ratios; had we kept
    the ×100 the product would have read 10,000.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — the factor-reversal test for Fisher (hard)">
  <p>
    For the same table, verify the factor-reversal test: Fisher's price index times Fisher's quantity
    index equals the value ratio Σp₁q₁ ÷ Σp₀q₀.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Fisher price index as a ratio</div>
    P₀₁ = square root of (1600 ÷ 1210 × 1605 ÷ 1200) = 1.329885 (from worked example 4)
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Fisher quantity index as a ratio</div>
    Q₀₁ = square root of (Σp₀q₁ ÷ Σp₀q₀ × Σp₁q₁ ÷ Σp₁q₀)<br />
    = square root of (1200 ÷ 1210 × 1605 ÷ 1600) = square root of (0.991736 × 1.003125) = 0.997414
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Their product is the value ratio</div>
    P₀₁ × Q₀₁ = 1.329885 × 0.997414 = <strong>1.326446</strong><br />
    Value ratio = Σp₁q₁ ÷ Σp₀q₀ = 1605 ÷ 1210 = <strong>1.326446</strong> ✓<br />
    The two agree to the last decimal, so Fisher passes the factor-reversal test as well.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 6 — cost of living by both methods (moderate)">
  <p>
    A working-class family buys three goods. Base-year prices ₹ 8, ₹ 6 and ₹ 5, base-year quantities
    25, 50 and 20; current prices ₹ 10, ₹ 7 and ₹ 6. Find the cost of living index by the aggregate
    expenditure method and by the family budget method, and confirm they agree.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Aggregate expenditure (Laspeyres)</div>
    Σp₁q₀ = 10×25 + 7×50 + 6×20 = 250 + 350 + 120 = 720<br />
    Σp₀q₀ = 8×25 + 6×50 + 5×20 = 200 + 300 + 100 = 600<br />
    Index = 720 ÷ 600 × 100 = <strong>120</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Family budget: relatives and value-weights</div>
    Relatives R: 10÷8×100 = 125, 7÷6×100 = 116.667, 6÷5×100 = 120<br />
    Weights W = p₀q₀: 200, 300, 100 (ΣW = 600)<br />
    Σ(RW) = 125×200 + 116.667×300 + 120×100 = 25,000 + 35,000 + 12,000 = 72,000
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Divide and compare</div>
    Family budget index = Σ(RW) ÷ ΣW = 72,000 ÷ 600 = <strong>120</strong><br />
    Identical to the aggregate expenditure figure, because R × W = 100 × p₁q₀ term by term. Two
    methods, one answer.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 7 — cost of living from group weights and relatives (moderate)">
  <p>
    A consumer price index is built from five groups with the weights and group index numbers
    (relatives) shown. Find the cost of living index. Food — weight 50, index 140; Clothing — weight
    10, index 120; Fuel and light — weight 15, index 110; Rent — weight 15, index 125; Miscellaneous
    — weight 10, index 130.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — This is the family budget form, Σ(RW) ÷ ΣW</div>
    Σ(RW) = 140×50 + 120×10 + 110×15 + 125×15 + 130×10<br />
    = 7,000 + 1,200 + 1,650 + 1,875 + 1,300 = 13,025<br />
    ΣW = 50 + 10 + 15 + 15 + 10 = 100
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Divide</div>
    Cost of living index = 13,025 ÷ 100 = <strong>130.25</strong><br />
    When the weights add to 100, the divisor is 100 and the index is just the total of the RW column.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 8 — real wage and purchasing power (moderate)">
  <p>
    A clerk earned ₹ 12,000 a month in the base year, when the cost of living index was 100, and
    earns ₹ 18,000 now, when the index has risen to 120. (a) What is the real wage now, in base-year
    rupees? (b) By how much has real income actually changed? (c) What is the purchasing power of the
    rupee now?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) Deflate the money wage</div>
    Real wage = Money wage ÷ index × 100 = 18,000 ÷ 120 × 100 = <strong>₹ 15,000</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) Compare with the base-year real wage</div>
    Base-year real wage = 12,000 ÷ 100 × 100 = ₹ 12,000<br />
    Real income has risen from ₹ 12,000 to ₹ 15,000, a genuine gain of <strong>₹ 3,000</strong> — the
    ₹ 6,000 money rise was not all swallowed by prices, only ₹ 3,000 of it.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (c) Purchasing power of the rupee</div>
    Purchasing power = 100 ÷ 120 = <strong>₹ 0.833</strong><br />
    A rupee now buys about 83 paise of what it bought in the base year.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 9 — base shifting and splicing (hard)">
  <p>
    An old wholesale price index reads 2018 = 100, 2019 = 125, 2020 = 150. A revised series was then
    started: 2020 = 100, 2021 = 112, 2022 = 130. (a) Shift the old series to a 2019 base. (b) Splice
    the new series onto the old base so the whole runs on one scale, and give the 2022 figure.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) Base shifting to 2019 = 100</div>
    Divide every old figure by the 2019 value (125) and multiply by 100:<br />
    2018: 100 ÷ 125 × 100 = 80 · 2019: 125 ÷ 125 × 100 = 100 · 2020: 150 ÷ 125 × 100 = <strong>120</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) Splice the new series onto the old base</div>
    Overlap year 2020: old = 150, new = 100. Multiply each new figure by 150 ÷ 100 = 1.5:<br />
    2021: 112 × 1.5 = 168 · 2022: 130 × 1.5 = <strong>195</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Reading the joined series</div>
    On the original 2018 = 100 scale the series now runs 100, 125, 150, 168, 195 — continuous across
    the changeover, with no jump at 2020. Splicing rescales; it never re-collects prices.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

- **Swapping the quantity weight.** Laspeyres uses base quantities q₀; Paasche uses current
  quantities q₁. Put Σp₁q₀ over Σp₀q₀ for Laspeyres and Σp₁q₁ over Σp₀q₁ for Paasche. The prices are
  always current over base; only the weight switches.
- **Averaging Laspeyres and Paasche for Fisher.** Fisher is the geometric mean — the square root of
  the product. The arithmetic mean (L + P) ÷ 2 is Dorbish-Bowley, a different index.
- **Keeping the ×100 in the reversal tests.** Time-reversal and factor-reversal use the indices as
  ratios. Strip the hundreds: 132.23 enters as 1.3223, not 132.23.
- **Using the simple aggregate index across mixed units.** A price quoted per quintal dominates one
  quoted per kilogram. Use relatives or weights instead.
- **Weighting the price in the family budget method.** The family budget method weights the price
  **relative** R by the **value** W = p₀q₀. Weighting a price, or weighting by a quantity, breaks the
  identity with the aggregate expenditure Laspeyres figure.
- **Multiplying instead of dividing when deflating.** Real wage = money wage ÷ index × 100. If the
  real wage comes out above the money wage while prices rose, the operation was inverted.
- **Confusing the value index with a price index.** The value index Σp₁q₁ ÷ Σp₀q₀ × 100 mixes price
  and quantity change together; it is not a price index and needs no separate weights.
- **Counting the base year wrongly in base shifting.** Divide the whole series by the old index of
  the **new** base year — the year you want to read as 100 — not by the first year of the series.
- **Splicing with the wrong ratio.** To carry a new series onto an old base, multiply by
  old ÷ new in the overlap year (here 150 ÷ 100). Going the other way uses the reciprocal.
- **Forgetting that a Fisher index must lie between Laspeyres and Paasche.** If it does not, a
  column-sum is wrong — a free check the examiner cannot take away.

## One-page revision summary

**Notation.** p₀, p₁ = base and current prices · q₀, q₁ = base and current quantities ·
P₀₁ = price index (period 1 on base 0) · Q₀₁ = quantity index · R = price relative · W = value weight
p₀q₀ · N = number of commodities. Build the four sums first: **Σp₀q₀, Σp₁q₀, Σp₀q₁, Σp₁q₁**.

**Unweighted indices**

<div class="formula">Price relative = (p₁ ÷ p₀) × 100</div>
<div class="formula">Simple aggregate = (Σp₁ ÷ Σp₀) × 100 — distorted by units</div>
<div class="formula">Simple average of relatives = Σ(p₁ ÷ p₀ × 100) ÷ N — cures units, not weighting</div>

**Weighted price indices** (prices current over base; only the weight changes)

<div class="formula">Laspeyres = (Σp₁q₀ ÷ Σp₀q₀) × 100 — base-year weights, reads high</div>
<div class="formula">Paasche = (Σp₁q₁ ÷ Σp₀q₁) × 100 — current-year weights, reads low</div>
<div class="formula">Fisher = square root of (Laspeyres × Paasche) — the ideal index</div>
<div class="formula">Marshall-Edgeworth = [(Σp₁q₀ + Σp₁q₁) ÷ (Σp₀q₀ + Σp₀q₁)] × 100</div>
<div class="formula">Dorbish-Bowley = (Laspeyres + Paasche) ÷ 2 — arithmetic mean</div>

**Tests of a good index** (indices used as ratios, no ×100)

<div class="formula">Time-reversal: P₀₁ × P₁₀ = 1 — Fisher passes</div>
<div class="formula">Factor-reversal: P₀₁ × Q₀₁ = Σp₁q₁ ÷ Σp₀q₀ — Fisher passes; Fisher passes both, hence "ideal"</div>

**Quantity and value**

<div class="formula">Laspeyres quantity = (Σq₁p₀ ÷ Σq₀p₀) × 100 · Fisher quantity = square root of (Laspeyres q × Paasche q)</div>
<div class="formula">Value index = (Σp₁q₁ ÷ Σp₀q₀) × 100 = Fisher price × Fisher quantity (as ratios)</div>

**Cost of living, deflating, rescaling**

<div class="formula">Cost of living (aggregate expenditure) = (Σp₁q₀ ÷ Σp₀q₀) × 100</div>
<div class="formula">Cost of living (family budget) = Σ(RW) ÷ ΣW, R = price relative, W = p₀q₀ — same answer</div>
<div class="formula">Real wage = (Money wage ÷ cost of living index) × 100</div>
<div class="formula">Purchasing power of the rupee = 100 ÷ cost of living index</div>
<div class="formula">Base shifting: new index = (old index of the year ÷ old index of the new base year) × 100</div>
<div class="formula">Splicing: new-series index × (old-series overlap value ÷ 100)</div>

**Direction of every relationship.** Laspeyres reads above Paasche when consumers shift away from
dear goods · Fisher, Marshall-Edgeworth and Dorbish-Bowley all sit between Laspeyres and Paasche ·
a cost of living index above 100 means prices rose · a real wage falls below the money wage once the
index passes 100 · the purchasing power of the rupee is the reciprocal of the index.
