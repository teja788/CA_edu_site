A dealer in Coimbatore is offered the same textile machine on two terms. Pay ₹ 12,00,000 today, or
pay ₹ 13,50,000 at the end of two years. Nothing else changes: same machine, same warranty, same
delivery date. The second price is ₹ 1,50,000 higher, so the first looks cheaper. Yet the dealer's
banker points out that ₹ 12,00,000 left in a deposit earning 8% compounded yearly grows to
₹ 13,99,680 in two years. On those numbers the *dearer* price is the better deal, and by almost
₹ 50,000.

That reversal is the whole chapter. Money has a price called interest, so a sum of money means
nothing until you also say **when** it is paid. Every formula that follows does one of two jobs:
it pushes a rupee forward in time (compounding) or pulls a rupee backward in time (discounting).
Learn to see which job a question wants, and most of the arithmetic becomes routine.

Paper 3 is fully objective, so you will not be asked to write out a derivation. You will be asked
to produce one number quickly and correctly. Expect questions on simple interest, on compound
interest with a conversion period that is not a year, on effective rates, on the present or future
value of an annuity, on the equated instalment of a loan, and on net present value. This chapter
takes each of them in turn, and shows you how to build every factor you need from scratch, so that
a missing interest table never costs you a mark.

## 1. Why a rupee today is not a rupee next year

A rupee available today is worth more than a rupee promised a year from now. Three plain reasons:

1. **It can earn.** Deposit today's rupee and it becomes more than a rupee by next year. The
   promised rupee earns nothing in the meantime.
2. **Prices drift up.** What a rupee buys next year is usually a little less than what it buys
   today.
3. **A promise can fail.** The person who owes you next year's rupee may not pay it.

At Foundation level only the first reason is measured. The rate of interest is the price of using
someone else's money for a period of time, and it is the single number that converts a rupee at one
date into a rupee at another date. Fix the vocabulary now, because every later formula reuses it.

- **Principal (P)** — the sum lent, borrowed or invested at the start. Also called the present
  value when you are pulling money backward in time.
- **Rate of interest (i or r)** — interest for one period, on one unit of principal. Quoted as a
  percentage, used as a decimal. 9% per annum means i = 0.09.
- **Time (t or n)** — the number of periods. It must be counted in the *same* unit as the rate.
- **Interest (I)** — the money charge itself.
- **Amount or accumulated value (A)** — principal plus interest at the end.

<div class="formula">Amount (A) = Principal (P) + Interest (I)</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The rate and the time must always speak the same language. A rate "per annum" goes with a time
    in years; a rate "per quarter" goes with a time in quarters. More marks are lost in this chapter
    to a mismatched pair than to any arithmetic slip. Whenever a question mentions months, quarters
    or half-years, write down the periodic rate and the number of periods <em>before</em> touching
    the formula.
  </p>
</Callout>

Two ways exist to charge interest, and the whole difference between them is one question: does
interest itself earn interest?

- **Simple interest** — interest is calculated on the original principal for every period. Interest
  earned is taken away and does not join the principal.
- **Compound interest** — interest earned at the end of a period is added to the principal, and the
  enlarged principal earns interest in the next period.

Over one period the two give the same figure. From the second period onward compound interest is
always the larger, and the gap widens as the time lengthens.

## 2. Simple interest

<div class="formula">I = P × i × t   and   A = P (1 + i t)</div>

Here `i` is the rate per period as a decimal and `t` is the number of periods. That is the entire
theory. Everything examinable about simple interest is a rearrangement of those two lines:

<div class="formula">P = I ÷ (i t)   ·   i = I ÷ (P t)   ·   t = I ÷ (P i)   ·   P = A ÷ (1 + i t)</div>

The amount grows in a straight line. Plot A against t and you get a straight line whose intercept
is P and whose slope is P i — the same rupee interest is added every year, for ever.

**Worked illustration.** ₹ 80,000 is lent for 3 years 6 months at 9% per annum simple interest.

- i = 0.09, t = 3.5 years
- I = 80,000 × 0.09 × 3.5 = ₹ 25,200
- A = 80,000 + 25,200 = ₹ 1,05,200

**Solving for the unknown.** If a sum doubles itself in 12 years at simple interest, the interest
earned equals the principal, so P = P × i × 12, giving i = 1 ÷ 12 = 0.0833, that is 8.33% per annum.
Notice that the principal cancels — you never needed to know it.

**Part periods.** A time given in months is treated as an exact fraction of a year: 8 months is
8/12 of a year, 3 years 4 months is 3 + 4/12 = 10/3 years. A time given in days is converted using
a **365-day year** in this chapter, so 73 days is 73/365 = 0.2 of a year. Some commercial questions
use a 360-day year, and a few count the first day or the last day but not both. Any question that
depends on the convention will say which one it wants; where it says nothing, use 365 days and
exact twelfths of a year.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Leaving the rate as a whole number is the commonest slip of all. 9% per annum is
    <strong>0.09</strong>, not 9. If your interest figure comes out a hundred times too large, this
    is why. The alternative is to divide by 100 inside the formula — I = P × R × t ÷ 100 — but pick
    one habit and keep it.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Two rates over one loan.** When the rate changes part-way, simple interest is worked out slice by
slice on the same original principal and the slices are added. ₹ 60,000 at 7% for the first 2 years
and 10% for the next 3 years earns 60,000 × 0.07 × 2 = ₹ 8,400 plus 60,000 × 0.10 × 3 = ₹ 18,000,
a total of ₹ 26,400. The principal never changes, because simple interest never joins it.

## 3. Compound interest

Under compounding, each period's interest is added to the principal before the next period begins.
Start with P. After one period the balance is P(1 + i). That whole balance earns in period two, so
it becomes P(1 + i)(1 + i), and so on:

<div class="formula">A = P (1 + i)^n   ·   Compound interest (CI) = A − P = P [(1 + i)^n − 1]</div>

- **A** is the accumulated amount, sometimes written FV (future value).
- **i** is the rate **per conversion period**, as a decimal.
- **n** is the **number of conversion periods**, not necessarily the number of years.

The factor (1 + i)^n is the **compounding factor** or future value interest factor. Read it as "one
rupee today becomes this many rupees after n periods".

**Worked illustration.** ₹ 2,00,000 invested for 3 years at 10% per annum compounded yearly.

- (1.10)^3 = 1.10 × 1.10 × 1.10 = 1.3310
- A = 2,00,000 × 1.3310 = ₹ 2,66,200
- Compound interest = 2,66,200 − 2,00,000 = ₹ 66,200

Simple interest on the same facts would have been 2,00,000 × 0.10 × 3 = ₹ 60,000. The ₹ 6,200 gap
is interest earned on interest, and it is exactly what the examiner tests when a question gives you
the *difference* between simple and compound interest.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Two shortcuts worth memorising. For <strong>two years</strong>, the difference between compound
    and simple interest is P i², so ₹ 2,00,000 at 10% gives 2,00,000 × 0.01 = ₹ 2,000. For
    <strong>three years</strong> the difference is P i² (3 + i), which here gives
    2,00,000 × 0.01 × 3.10 = ₹ 6,200 — matching the figure above. Both follow from expanding
    (1 + i)^n, and both let you answer a difference question in one line.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Changing rates.** When the rate changes over the life of the deposit, the factors multiply. ₹
1,00,000 for 2 years at 8% and then 3 years at 10%, all compounded yearly, grows to
1,00,000 × (1.08)² × (1.10)³ = 1,00,000 × 1.1664 × 1.3310 = ₹ 1,55,247.84, say ₹ 1,55,248.

**Depreciation and other decay.** The same formula runs backwards when a quantity shrinks at a
constant rate. Write the rate as negative:

<div class="formula">Value after n periods = P (1 − d)^n, where d is the rate of decline per period</div>

A machine bought for ₹ 8,00,000 losing 15% of its written-down value each year is worth
8,00,000 × (0.85)³ = 8,00,000 × 0.614125 = ₹ 4,91,300 after three years. This is the mathematics of
the reducing-balance pattern; the accounting entries and the choice of method belong to Paper 1 and
are not tested here. What Paper 3 asks is the arithmetic of growth and decay at a constant rate.

## 4. Conversion periods and compounding more often than yearly

The **conversion period** (or interest period) is the interval at the end of which interest is added
to the principal. A rate quoted per annum with a shorter conversion period must be converted before
it can be used:

<div class="formula">i = (annual nominal rate) ÷ (number of conversions per year)   ·   n = (years) × (number of conversions per year)</div>

| Compounded | Conversions per year | Rate per period on 12% p.a. | Periods in 5 years |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yearly | 1 | 0.12 | 5 |
| Half-yearly | 2 | 0.06 | 10 |
| Quarterly | 4 | 0.03 | 20 |
| Monthly | 12 | 0.01 | 60 |

Both columns move together. Halve the period and you halve the rate *and* double the count — never
one without the other.

**Worked illustration.** ₹ 50,000 for 2 years at 12% per annum compounded quarterly.

- i = 0.12 ÷ 4 = 0.03; n = 2 × 4 = 8
- (1.03)^8 = 1.2668 to four decimal places (1.266770 at full precision)
- A = 50,000 × 1.266770 = ₹ 63,338.50; compound interest = ₹ 13,338.50

Yearly compounding on the same facts gives 50,000 × (1.12)² = ₹ 62,720. More frequent compounding
always produces a larger amount, because interest starts earning sooner.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>The rounding rule used throughout this chapter.</strong> Every factor is shown to four
    decimal places so that you can follow the working, but the money is computed from the unrounded
    factor and rounded only at the <em>last</em> step, to the nearest rupee (or to two decimal places
    where the question is about paise). Interest is not rounded at the end of each period unless a
    question expressly says the bank does so. Rounding early, period by period, can move a five-year
    answer by several rupees, which is enough to land on the wrong option.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Solving for the rate and for the time

The compound interest formula has four quantities. Give the examiner any three and the fourth is
fixed. Two of the four rearrangements need a little more than division.

**Solving for the rate.** Take the n-th root of the growth ratio and subtract one:

<div class="formula">i = (A ÷ P)^(1/n) − 1</div>

An investment of ₹ 1,50,000 becomes ₹ 2,50,000 in 6 years, compounded yearly. The ratio is
2,50,000 ÷ 1,50,000 = 1.666667. Its sixth root is 1.088867, so i = 0.088867, that is **8.89% per
annum**. Note the answer is the rate per conversion period; if the compounding were quarterly, the
same working would give the rate per quarter, and the annual nominal rate would be four times it.

**Solving for the time.** Time sits in the exponent, so logarithms are the tool. Take logs of both
sides of A = P(1 + i)^n:

<div class="formula">n = [log A − log P] ÷ log (1 + i)</div>

Any base works, provided you use the same base on top and below. Common logarithms (base 10) are
the ones the tables carry.

**Worked illustration — how long to double at 8% compounded yearly?**

- A ÷ P = 2, so n = log 2 ÷ log 1.08
- log 2 = 0.3010, log 1.08 = 0.0334
- n = 0.3010 ÷ 0.0334 = 9.01 years, so the money doubles a shade after **9 years**

Because only the ratio A ÷ P appears, the principal is irrelevant — ₹ 5,000 and ₹ 5,00,00,000
double in exactly the same time. That is why doubling questions never give you the principal, and
why a candidate who hunts for it wastes a minute.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A rough check you can do in your head: the <strong>rule of 72</strong>. Divide 72 by the rate in
    percent and you get the approximate doubling time — 72 ÷ 8 = 9 years, against the exact 9.01
    above. Use it to sanity-test an option, never to choose between two options that are close
    together.
  </p>
</Callout>

The same method answers "how long to reach 1.5 times" or "to reach ₹ 4,00,000". Divide the target
by the principal first, then take logs. To grow 50% at 6% compounded yearly needs
log 1.5 ÷ log 1.06 = 0.1761 ÷ 0.0253 = 6.96 years, so seven years of interest are required before
the target is passed.

## 6. Nominal rate, effective rate and continuous compounding

A bank that advertises "12% per annum compounded quarterly" is quoting a **nominal rate**. Nobody
actually earns 12% in the year, because the quarterly interest compounds. The **effective annual
rate** is the single yearly rate that would produce the same amount in one year with yearly
compounding. It is the only honest basis for comparing two offers.

<div class="formula">E = (1 + r ÷ m)^m − 1, where r is the annual nominal rate and m is the number of conversions per year</div>

| Nominal 12% p.a. compounded | Rate per period | Effective annual rate |
|---|---|---|
| Yearly | 0.12 | 12.0000% |
| Half-yearly | 0.06 | 12.3600% |
| Quarterly | 0.03 | 12.5509% |
| Monthly | 0.01 | 12.6825% |
| Continuously | — | 12.7497% |

Three readings of that table are examinable. The effective rate is **never below** the nominal
rate, and equals it only when compounding is yearly. It **rises** as compounding gets more frequent.
And the rise **slows down** — the jump from yearly to half-yearly is 0.36 percentage points, while
the jump from monthly all the way to continuous is under 0.07. The effective rate does not run away;
it approaches a ceiling.

That ceiling is **continuous compounding**, the limit as the conversion period shrinks to nothing:

<div class="formula">A = P e^(r t)   ·   Effective annual rate under continuous compounding = e^r − 1</div>

Here e = 2.71828 (to five decimal places), r is the annual nominal rate as a decimal and t is the
time in years. ₹ 1,00,000 for 5 years at 8% compounded continuously grows to
1,00,000 × e^(0.08 × 5) = 1,00,000 × e^0.40 = 1,00,000 × 1.491825 = ₹ 1,49,182.47, against
1,00,000 × (1.08)^5 = ₹ 1,46,932.81 with yearly compounding.

**Going the other way.** Given an effective rate, the nominal rate that produces it with m
conversions a year is r = m [(1 + E)^(1/m) − 1]. An effective 10.25% with half-yearly compounding
implies 2 × [(1.1025)^0.5 − 1] = 2 × 0.05 = **10% nominal**.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Using the nominal rate where the effective rate was wanted, or the reverse, is one of the two or
    three errors this chapter is built to catch. Read the last line of the question again: "what
    rate of interest, compounded yearly, gives the same return" asks for the effective rate;
    "what rate should the bank advertise" asks for the nominal rate. Comparing a 12% quarterly offer
    with a 12.4% yearly offer by their nominal figures picks the wrong bank.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. Present value and future value of a single sum

Compounding pushes a sum forward in time. **Discounting** pulls it backward, and it is nothing more
than the compound interest formula solved for P:

<div class="formula">FV = PV (1 + i)^n   ·   PV = FV ÷ (1 + i)^n = FV (1 + i)^(−n)</div>

The multiplier (1 + i)^(−n) is the **discount factor** or present value interest factor. It is
always less than one, it falls as the rate rises, and it falls as the time lengthens. The rate used
for discounting is often called the **discount rate** or the required rate of return; it is the
return the investor could earn elsewhere on money of the same risk.

**Worked illustration.** What is ₹ 5,00,000 receivable in 6 years worth today, if money is worth 9%
per annum compounded yearly?

- Discount factor = (1.09)^(−6) = 0.5963 to four decimal places
- PV = 5,00,000 × 0.596267 = ₹ 2,98,133.66, say **₹ 2,98,134**

Read that as: ₹ 2,98,134 invested today at 9% would itself grow to ₹ 5,00,000 in six years, so the
two sums are worth the same to this investor. That equivalence is the whole idea, and it is what
lets you compare cash flows that arrive on different dates — you move them all to one date first,
usually today.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Compounding and discounting are exact opposites, so every discount factor is the reciprocal of
    the matching compound factor. If (1.09)^6 = 1.6771, then the six-year 9% discount factor is
    1 ÷ 1.6771 = 0.5963. One table serves both jobs, and one calculation checks the other.
  </p>
</Callout>

A stream of unequal cash flows has no shortcut: discount each one separately and add. Three
receipts of ₹ 40,000, ₹ 50,000 and ₹ 60,000 at the ends of years 1, 2 and 3, at 10%, are worth
40,000(0.9091) + 50,000(0.8264) + 60,000(0.7513) = 36,364 + 41,322 + 45,079 = **₹ 1,22,765** today.
Only when the amounts are *equal* and the intervals are *regular* can you use the annuity formulas
that follow, and that is exactly why the next four sections exist.

## 8. Annuities and the vocabulary you must fix

An **annuity** is a series of equal payments made at equal intervals of time. A monthly loan
instalment, a yearly insurance premium, a quarterly deposit into a recurring account — all are
annuities. Three words settle everything about which formula to use.

- **Ordinary annuity** (annuity immediate, annuity regular) — payments fall at the **end** of each
  period. The first payment is one period from today.
- **Annuity due** — payments fall at the **beginning** of each period. The first payment is today.
- **Perpetuity** — the payments never stop.

The number of payments is n, the payment itself is R (or A, or PMT — this chapter uses **R**), and
the rate per period is i. As always, the payment interval, the rate and the count of periods must
all use the same unit of time.

| The question says | Treat it as | First payment falls |
|---|---|---|
| "at the end of every year", "yearly instalments" | Ordinary annuity | End of year 1 |
| "at the beginning of every year", "the first deposit today" | Annuity due | Today |
| "annual payment", nothing else said | Ordinary annuity (the default) | End of year 1 |
| "for ever", "in perpetuity", "an endowment" | Perpetuity | End of year 1 |

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Where a question is silent, the ordinary annuity is the standing assumption in this chapter and
    in the study material's worked patterns. Rent and lease payments in real life are usually paid
    in advance, so a question that means an annuity due almost always says so in words — "payable in
    advance", "at the commencement of each year", "the first instalment is paid immediately".
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Future value of an ordinary annuity

Deposit R at the end of each of n periods. The first deposit earns interest for n − 1 periods, the
second for n − 2, and the last one earns nothing at all, because it arrives on the very day you
close the account. Adding that geometric series gives:

<div class="formula">FV of an ordinary annuity = R × [(1 + i)^n − 1] ÷ i</div>

The bracketed multiplier is the **future value annuity factor**, often written A(n, i). It is always
greater than n, because every deposit except the last earns something.

**Worked illustration.** ₹ 25,000 deposited at the end of each year for 10 years at 8% per annum.

- (1.08)^10 = 2.1589 to four decimal places
- Factor = (2.158925 − 1) ÷ 0.08 = 1.158925 ÷ 0.08 = 14.4866
- FV = 25,000 × 14.486562 = ₹ 3,62,164.06, say **₹ 3,62,164**

The depositor put in 10 × 25,000 = ₹ 2,50,000 of her own money; the remaining ₹ 1,12,164 is
interest. Notice the factor 14.4866 is bigger than 10, as promised.

**Solving for the deposit.** Turn the formula round when the target is known and the instalment is
not:

<div class="formula">R = FV × i ÷ [(1 + i)^n − 1]</div>

To accumulate ₹ 3,62,164 in ten years at 8%, the yearly deposit must be
3,62,164 ÷ 14.486562 = ₹ 25,000 — the same illustration read backwards. This rearrangement is the
sinking-fund calculation of section 13.

## 10. Present value of an ordinary annuity

Now ask the opposite question: what single sum today is equivalent to R at the end of each of the
next n periods? Discount every payment and add:

<div class="formula">PV of an ordinary annuity = R × [1 − (1 + i)^(−n)] ÷ i</div>

The multiplier is the **present value annuity factor**, written P(n, i). It is always **less than
n**, because every payment is worth less than its face value once discounted. That single sentence
catches most careless answers: if your annuity factor exceeds the number of payments, you have used
the future value formula by mistake.

**Worked illustration.** ₹ 60,000 receivable at the end of each year for 5 years, discounted at 10%.

- (1.10)^(−5) = 0.6209 to four decimal places
- Factor = (1 − 0.620921) ÷ 0.10 = 0.379079 ÷ 0.10 = 3.7908
- PV = 60,000 × 3.790787 = ₹ 2,27,447.21, say **₹ 2,27,447**

The five payments total ₹ 3,00,000 in face value, and are worth ₹ 2,27,447 today. The ₹ 72,553
difference is the interest the payer gets to keep by paying later rather than now.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The two annuity factors are linked by the compound factor: P(n, i) × (1 + i)^n = A(n, i). Here
    3.790787 × 1.61051 = 6.10510, which is exactly ((1.10)^5 − 1) ÷ 0.10. If a question gives you
    one factor and wants the other, this line saves the whole calculation.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Solving for the payment.** Rearranged, this is the loan instalment formula of section 14:

<div class="formula">R = PV × i ÷ [1 − (1 + i)^(−n)]</div>

**Deferred annuities.** If the first of n equal payments arrives at the end of year 4 rather than
year 1, work in two steps. Value the annuity at the moment one period before its first payment —
here, the end of year 3 — using the ordinary annuity formula, then discount that single sum back to
today by three years. Do not discount the annuity by n + 3 years; the factor already handles the
first n periods.

## 11. Annuity due and the one extra period

An annuity due pays at the **beginning** of each period, so every one of the n payments sits one
period earlier than it would in an ordinary annuity. Each payment therefore earns interest for one
period longer, or is discounted one period less. That is the entire adjustment:

<div class="formula">FV of an annuity due = R × [(1 + i)^n − 1] ÷ i × (1 + i)</div>
<div class="formula">PV of an annuity due = R × [1 − (1 + i)^(−n)] ÷ i × (1 + i)</div>

Compute the ordinary annuity value first, then multiply by (1 + i). One multiplication, and it is
the single most frequently missed step in this chapter.

**Worked illustration.** ₹ 60,000 at the **beginning** of each year for 5 years, at 10%.

- Ordinary annuity present value (section 10) = ₹ 2,27,447.21
- Multiply by 1.10: PV = 2,27,447.21 × 1.10 = ₹ 2,50,191.93, say **₹ 2,50,192**

The whole stream is worth ₹ 22,745 more, simply because each rupee arrives twelve months sooner.

An equivalent way to see the present value of an annuity due: the first payment is received today
and needs no discounting at all, and what remains is an ordinary annuity of n − 1 payments. So
PV = R + R × P(n − 1, i). Here that is 60,000 + 60,000 × 3.169865 = 60,000 + 1,90,191.93 =
₹ 2,50,191.93 — the same figure by a different road. Use whichever version you can hold in your
head under exam conditions.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Two traps sit side by side. The first is forgetting the (1 + i) altogether, which understates a
    present value and understates a future value. The second is applying it twice — once through the
    formula and once by shifting the periods by hand. Pick <strong>one</strong> route: either
    multiply the ordinary annuity result by (1 + i), or rebuild the series payment by payment. Never
    both.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 12. Perpetuity and growing perpetuity

A **perpetuity** is an annuity whose payments never end — a permanent endowment that funds a prize
each year, an irredeemable bond, a piece of land let on a perpetual lease. Let n run to infinity in
the present value annuity formula and (1 + i)^(−n) vanishes, leaving:

<div class="formula">PV of a perpetuity = R ÷ i</div>

At 8%, a prize of ₹ 40,000 a year for ever needs an endowment of 40,000 ÷ 0.08 = **₹ 5,00,000**.
The fund is never touched; only its interest is spent. That is why the answer is finite even though
the payments are infinite — payments in the distant future are worth almost nothing today.

A **growing perpetuity** raises the payment by a constant proportion g every period. The first
payment R, made one period from now, is followed by R(1 + g), then R(1 + g)², and so on:

<div class="formula">PV of a growing perpetuity = R ÷ (i − g), valid only while g is less than i</div>

If the prize must rise by 3% a year to keep pace with prices, and money is still worth 8%, the
endowment needed is 40,000 ÷ (0.08 − 0.03) = 40,000 ÷ 0.05 = **₹ 8,00,000**. The growth condition
matters: if g were equal to or greater than i, the payments would grow at least as fast as the
discounting shrinks them and no finite sum could fund them.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    In R ÷ (i − g), the numerator R is the payment <strong>one period from now</strong>, not the
    payment just made. If a question gives last year's prize of ₹ 40,000 and says it will grow at
    3%, the numerator is 40,000 × 1.03 = ₹ 41,200 and the endowment is ₹ 8,24,000. Read which
    payment you have been handed.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 13. Sinking funds

A **sinking fund** is money set aside at regular intervals so that a known sum is available on a
known future date — to redeem a debenture issue, to replace a machine at the end of its life, to
meet a lease renewal premium. The deposits earn interest until the date arrives, so the total
deposited is always less than the sum required.

A sinking fund is an ordinary annuity read backwards: the target is the future value, and the
unknown is the deposit.

<div class="formula">Periodic deposit R = S × i ÷ [(1 + i)^n − 1], where S is the sum required</div>

**Worked illustration.** A machine costing ₹ 15,00,000 must be replaced in 8 years. Deposits are
made at the end of each year into a fund earning 9% per annum compounded yearly.

- (1.09)^8 = 1.992563; factor = (1.992563 − 1) ÷ 0.09 = 11.0285
- R = 15,00,000 ÷ 11.028474 = ₹ 1,36,011.57, say **₹ 1,36,012 a year**

Eight deposits total ₹ 10,88,093. The remaining ₹ 4,11,907 is interest earned inside the fund.

Two variations appear regularly. If the deposits are made at the **beginning** of each year, divide
by the annuity-due factor instead — that is, by 11.028474 × 1.09 = 12.021037 — which lowers the
deposit to ₹ 1,24,781. And if the fund already holds an opening balance, compound that balance
forward to the target date first and let the annuity fund only the shortfall.

## 14. Loan amortisation and the equated instalment

A loan repaid by equal instalments is the mirror image of an annuity. The lender hands over a lump
sum today; the borrower hands back a stream of equal payments. The two must be worth the same on
the day the loan is made, so the loan amount is the **present value of the instalments**:

<div class="formula">Loan = R × [1 − (1 + i)^(−n)] ÷ i   ·   R = Loan × i ÷ [1 − (1 + i)^(−n)]</div>

For a monthly instalment, i is the monthly rate (annual nominal rate ÷ 12) and n is the number of
months. A five-year loan of ₹ 5,00,000 at 10% per annum compounded monthly needs
i = 0.10 ÷ 12 = 0.008333 and n = 60, giving R = ₹ 10,623.52 a month.

Every instalment is split. Interest for the period is charged on the **opening balance**; whatever
is left of the instalment reduces the principal. Because the balance falls, the interest slice
shrinks and the principal slice grows, instalment after instalment, while the instalment itself
never changes.

<div class="formula">Interest for the period = Opening balance × i   ·   Principal repaid = Instalment − Interest   ·   Closing balance = Opening balance − Principal repaid</div>

**Worked illustration — a full schedule.** ₹ 5,00,000 borrowed at 10% per annum, repayable in 4
equal yearly instalments, the first at the end of year 1.

- P(4, 10%) = (1 − (1.10)^(−4)) ÷ 0.10 = 3.169865
- R = 5,00,000 ÷ 3.169865 = ₹ 1,57,735.40

| Year | Opening balance | Instalment | Interest at 10% | Principal repaid | Closing balance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5,00,000.00 | 1,57,735.40 | 50,000.00 | 1,07,735.40 | 3,92,264.60 |
| 2 | 3,92,264.60 | 1,57,735.40 | 39,226.46 | 1,18,508.94 | 2,73,755.66 |
| 3 | 2,73,755.66 | 1,57,735.40 | 27,375.57 | 1,30,359.84 | 1,43,395.82 |
| 4 | 1,43,395.82 | 1,57,735.40 | 14,339.58 | 1,43,395.82 | 0.00 |

Read the schedule across and three facts stand out. Total paid = 4 × 1,57,735.40 = ₹ 6,30,941.61,
of which ₹ 5,00,000 is principal and ₹ 1,30,941.61 is interest. The interest column falls every
year while the principal column rises. And the closing balance reaches exactly zero — it is not
forced there, it falls out of the arithmetic, which is the check that the instalment was right.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    You rarely need the whole schedule. The balance outstanding after k instalments is just the
    present value of the instalments still to come:
    <strong>Balance after k payments = R × P(n − k, i)</strong>. After two years above,
    1,57,735.40 × P(2, 10%) = 1,57,735.40 × 1.735537 = ₹ 2,73,755.66, matching the table without
    building it. This is the fastest way to answer "how much is still owed" questions.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Charging interest on the <em>original</em> loan every year turns an amortisation question into a
    simple-interest question and inflates the total interest badly. Interest is always on the
    <strong>opening balance of that period</strong>. A related slip is rounding the instalment to
    the nearest rupee and then insisting the last balance is exactly nil — a rounded instalment
    leaves a few rupees over or short, which the final instalment absorbs. This chapter builds every
    schedule from the unrounded instalment.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 15. Net present value and the accept-reject rule

A business spends money now to earn money later. **Net present value (NPV)** puts both sides of that
bargain on the same date — today — and subtracts:

<div class="formula">NPV = Sum of the present values of all cash inflows − Present value of the cash outflows</div>

The discount rate is the return the business requires, usually its cost of capital. The decision
rule is short:

- **NPV positive** — accept. The project returns more than the required rate, and the NPV is the
  surplus, measured in today's rupees.
- **NPV negative** — reject. The project earns less than money costs.
- **NPV zero** — the project earns exactly the required rate; the decision turns on other grounds.

**Worked illustration.** A machine costs ₹ 9,00,000 today and is expected to produce net cash
inflows of ₹ 3,00,000, ₹ 3,50,000, ₹ 4,00,000 and ₹ 2,50,000 at the ends of years 1 to 4. The
required return is 12%.

| Year | Cash flow | Factor at 12% | Present value |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3,00,000 | 0.8929 | 2,67,857.14 |
| 2 | 3,50,000 | 0.7972 | 2,79,017.86 |
| 3 | 4,00,000 | 0.7118 | 2,84,712.10 |
| 4 | 2,50,000 | 0.6355 | 1,58,879.52 |
| | | **Total** | **9,90,466.62** |

NPV = 9,90,466.62 − 9,00,000 = **₹ 90,466.62**, positive, so the machine is worth buying at a 12%
required return.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The outlay is spent today, so its discount factor is 1 and it is never discounted. Only outlays
    made in later years get a factor. Equally, a cash flow described as "in the third year" is taken
    at the <strong>end</strong> of year 3 unless the question says otherwise, and any scrap or
    salvage value is simply another inflow in its own year.
  </p>
</Callout>

Where the inflows are equal year after year, the annuity factor replaces the four separate lines. An
outlay of ₹ 4,00,000 returning ₹ 1,20,000 a year for 5 years at 10% has an NPV of
1,20,000 × 3.790787 − 4,00,000 = 4,54,894.41 − 4,00,000 = ₹ 54,894.41.

## 16. Profitability index and choosing between projects

NPV tells you how much value a project adds; it says nothing about how much had to be committed to
get it. The **profitability index (PI)**, also called the benefit-cost ratio or desirability factor,
supplies that:

<div class="formula">PI = Present value of cash inflows ÷ Present value of the initial outlay</div>

- **PI greater than 1** — accept. This is the same signal as a positive NPV, and the two can never
  disagree on a single project, because PI exceeds 1 exactly when inflows exceed the outlay.
- **PI less than 1** — reject.

For the machine in section 15, PI = 9,90,466.62 ÷ 9,00,000 = **1.1005**. Every rupee committed
brings back about ₹ 1.10 in today's money.

**Choosing between projects.** When two projects are independent and money is plentiful, accept
every project with a positive NPV. When they are **mutually exclusive** — the same factory floor,
the same licence — take the one with the **higher NPV**, because NPV measures rupees of added value.
When capital is **rationed** and small projects can be combined, the PI ranking is the more useful
guide, because it measures value per rupee of scarce capital.

| | Project X | Project Y |
|---|---|---|
| Outlay today | ₹ 5,00,000 | ₹ 2,00,000 |
| Present value of inflows | ₹ 6,00,000 | ₹ 2,60,000 |
| NPV | ₹ 1,00,000 | ₹ 60,000 |
| PI | 1.20 | 1.30 |

X adds more value; Y works the harder rupee. If the firm can fund only one and has ₹ 5,00,000
available, X is the better choice. If it has ₹ 5,00,000 and can find two more projects like Y, the
PI ranking wins. Foundation questions state which situation applies; read that sentence before
ranking anything.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Ranking mutually exclusive projects by PI when the outlays differ is a genuine trap, and so is
    comparing the NPVs of projects with different lives without saying so. At this level, follow the
    instruction in the question: "which project should be selected" with a single budget means the
    higher NPV, unless the question mentions capital rationing.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 17. Compound annual growth rate

**Compound annual growth rate (CAGR)** is the single yearly rate at which a quantity would have to
grow, compounding, to move from its beginning value to its ending value over the period. It smooths
away the year-to-year bumps.

<div class="formula">CAGR = (Ending value ÷ Beginning value)^(1 ÷ n) − 1, where n is the number of YEARS between the two values</div>

**Worked illustration.** Turnover rose from ₹ 25,00,000 in 2020-21 to ₹ 48,60,000 in 2025-26.

- The two figures are 5 years apart, not 6 — count the intervals, not the data points.
- Ratio = 48,60,000 ÷ 25,00,000 = 1.944
- CAGR = (1.944)^(1/5) − 1 = 1.142192 − 1 = 0.142192, that is **14.22% per annum**

CAGR is the compound-interest rate formula of section 5 under another name, which is why the same
rearrangement solves both. It ignores everything that happened in between: a business that fell for
three years and then doubled shows the same CAGR as one that grew steadily, so it describes the
endpoints only.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The simple average of the yearly growth rates is <em>not</em> the CAGR, and is always the higher
    of the two whenever the yearly rates differ. A quantity that rises 50% and then falls 20% has an
    arithmetic average growth of 15% a year, while its true CAGR is
    (1.50 × 0.80)^(1/2) − 1 = 9.54%. Counting years wrongly is the other standard error — from
    2020-21 to 2025-26 is <strong>5</strong> years.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 18. Valuing a simple bond

A bond is a loan cut into tradeable pieces. The issuer promises a fixed **coupon** each period and
the **redemption value** (usually the face value) on a fixed maturity date. Its value today is what
those promises are worth, discounted at the return the market now requires — the **yield**:

<div class="formula">Bond value = Coupon × [1 − (1 + y)^(−n)] ÷ y + Redemption value × (1 + y)^(−n)</div>

The first term is an ordinary annuity of coupons; the second is a single sum. Nothing new is
involved, only the two formulas you already have, added together.

**Worked illustration.** A bond of face value ₹ 1,000 carries a 9% annual coupon, is redeemable at
par after 5 years, and investors now require 11%.

- Coupon = 9% of 1,000 = ₹ 90 a year for 5 years
- P(5, 11%) = (1 − (1.11)^(−5)) ÷ 0.11 = 3.695897; (1.11)^(−5) = 0.593451
- Value = 90 × 3.695897 + 1,000 × 0.593451 = 332.63 + 593.45 = **₹ 926.08**

The bond sells at a **discount** to face value because its 9% coupon is meaner than the 11% the
market wants; the shortfall is made up by buying the bond for less than ₹ 1,000. Reverse the rates
and the logic reverses: at a required return of 7% the same bond is worth
90 × 4.100197 + 1,000 × 0.712986 = **₹ 1,082.00**, a **premium**.

| Required yield versus coupon rate | Bond sells at |
|---|---|
| Yield greater than coupon rate | A discount (below face value) |
| Yield equal to coupon rate | Par (exactly face value) |
| Yield below the coupon rate | A premium (above face value) |

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Two rates live in every bond question and they do different work. The <strong>coupon rate</strong>
    is applied to the <em>face</em> value to find the rupee coupon, and never changes. The
    <strong>yield</strong> is used to <em>discount</em>, and it moves with the market. Using the
    coupon rate to discount always returns the face value and always looks suspiciously round —
    that is the built-in warning that the rates have been swapped.
  </p>
</Callout>

If coupons are paid half-yearly, halve both the coupon and the yield and double the number of
periods, exactly as in section 4. A ₹ 1,000 bond with a 9% coupon paid half-yearly for 5 years at a
required 11% has ₹ 45 payable for 10 half-years discounted at 5.5%.

## 19. Building any factor without an interest table

Interest tables are convenient, not essential. Every factor in this chapter can be built from
multiplication, and this section shows the two moves that make it quick. Both are worth practising,
because a factor you can build is a factor you cannot misread off the wrong row.

**Move one: repeated squaring.** To raise (1 + i) to a power, square your way up rather than
multiplying ten times.

- (1.08)² = 1.1664
- (1.08)⁴ = (1.1664)² = 1.360489
- (1.08)⁸ = (1.360489)² = 1.850930
- (1.08)¹⁰ = (1.08)⁸ × (1.08)² = 1.850930 × 1.1664 = **2.158925**

Three squarings and one multiplication replace nine multiplications, and each step can be checked
by eye.

**Move two: one factor gives you the other three.** From a single compound factor, everything else
follows by a division or two:

<div class="formula">Discount factor = 1 ÷ compound factor   ·   A(n, i) = (compound factor − 1) ÷ i   ·   P(n, i) = (1 − discount factor) ÷ i</div>

Continuing with 8% and 10 years: the compound factor is 2.158925, so the discount factor is
1 ÷ 2.158925 = 0.463193, the future value annuity factor is (2.158925 − 1) ÷ 0.08 = 14.486562, and
the present value annuity factor is (1 − 0.463193) ÷ 0.08 = 6.710081. Four numbers from one.

**How many decimals to carry.** This chapter quotes every factor to **four decimal places** in the
text, and every worked example carries the unrounded value through the arithmetic and rounds only
the final money figure. In the exam, four decimals in the factor is enough for the options to be
distinguishable; three is usually enough; two is not, once the sums run into lakhs.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Rounding a factor to two decimals and then multiplying by a large principal is the quiet
    mark-loser. 14.49 instead of 14.486562, applied to a deposit of ₹ 25,000, shifts a ten-year
    answer by about ₹ 86 — small in itself, but examiners set distractors precisely where an early
    rounding lands.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 20. Answering under negative marking

Paper 3 is entirely objective. A correct answer earns 1 mark, an incorrect answer costs 0.25 mark,
and an unanswered question scores zero. That single fact should change how you treat a question you
cannot finish, and the arithmetic of it is worth doing once, calmly.

If you can eliminate nothing and pick at random from four options, you expect
(1 ÷ 4)(+1) + (3 ÷ 4)(−0.25) = 0.25 − 0.1875 = **+0.0625** marks. The expected value is slightly
positive, so a blind guess is not harmful, but it is very nearly worthless. Eliminate one option
and the same sum gives (1 ÷ 3)(+1) + (2 ÷ 3)(−0.25) = **+0.1667**. Eliminate two and it rises to
**+0.375**. The value of a guess comes almost entirely from the options you can rule out, and in
this chapter you can often rule out two without computing anything: an annuity present value factor
larger than the number of payments, a compound amount smaller than the simple-interest amount, or a
present value larger than the future sum.

The practical reading is simple. Attempt what you can eliminate down to two, mark it and move on.
Leave a question untouched only when nothing at all can be ruled out and the time would be better
spent elsewhere. There is no penalty for a blank, and no need to fill every box.

  <p>
    Ravindra lends ₹ 1,20,000 for 3 years at 10% per annum. Find the interest under (a) simple
    interest and (b) compound interest with yearly conversion, and (c) the difference between them.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) Simple interest</div>
    I = P × i × t = 1,20,000 × 0.10 × 3 = <strong>₹ 36,000</strong><br />
    Amount = 1,20,000 + 36,000 = ₹ 1,56,000
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) Compound interest</div>
    (1.10)³ = 1.3310<br />
    Amount = 1,20,000 × 1.3310 = ₹ 1,59,720<br />
    Compound interest = 1,59,720 − 1,20,000 = <strong>₹ 39,720</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (c) The difference, two ways</div>
    Directly: 39,720 − 36,000 = <strong>₹ 3,720</strong><br />
    By the three-year shortcut: P i² (3 + i) = 1,20,000 × 0.01 × 3.10 = ₹ 3,720 — the same figure,
    and a useful check when the question asks only for the difference.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — comparing two bank offers on their effective rates (moderate)">
  <p>
    Bank A offers 9.6% per annum compounded quarterly. Bank B offers 9.8% per annum compounded
    half-yearly. A depositor with ₹ 5,00,000 wants the better of the two. Which should she choose,
    and by how much is she better off after one year?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Bank A, effective annual rate</div>
    Rate per quarter = 0.096 ÷ 4 = 0.024; conversions m = 4<br />
    E = (1.024)⁴ − 1 = 1.099512 − 1 = 0.099512 = <strong>9.9512%</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Bank B, effective annual rate</div>
    Rate per half-year = 0.098 ÷ 2 = 0.049; conversions m = 2<br />
    E = (1.049)² − 1 = 1.100401 − 1 = 0.100401 = <strong>10.0401%</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — The decision</div>
    Bank B is better, although its compounding is less frequent, because its nominal rate is enough
    higher to outweigh that.<br />
    Interest after one year: A = 5,00,000 × 0.099512 = ₹ 49,756; B = 5,00,000 × 0.100401 = ₹ 50,201<br />
    Advantage of Bank B = <strong>₹ 445</strong> in the first year<br />
    Comparing the nominal rates alone would have reached the same conclusion here only by luck. At
    9.6% quarterly against 9.7% half-yearly the ranking flips, which is precisely why the effective
    rate exists.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — solving for time with logarithms (moderate)">
  <p>
    In how many years will ₹ 2,00,000 grow to ₹ 6,00,000 at 12% per annum compounded yearly? Take
    log 3 = 0.4771 and log 1.12 = 0.0492.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Set up the ratio</div>
    A = P (1 + i)^n → 6,00,000 = 2,00,000 (1.12)^n → (1.12)^n = 3<br />
    The principal disappears; only the multiple of 3 matters.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Take logarithms</div>
    n log 1.12 = log 3 → n = 0.4771 ÷ 0.0492 = <strong>9.69 years</strong><br />
    At full precision, n = 0.477121 ÷ 0.049218 = 9.694 years.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Reading the answer</div>
    If the fund credits interest only at the end of each year, the balance is
    2,00,000 × (1.12)⁹ = ₹ 5,54,616 after nine years and 2,00,000 × (1.12)¹⁰ = ₹ 6,21,170 after ten.
    So the target is first reached at the <strong>tenth</strong> credit. "How long does it take" and
    "when is the money actually there" can have different answers — read which one is asked.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — present value of an uneven stream (moderate)">
  <p>
    A buyer offers ₹ 1,50,000 at the end of year 1, ₹ 2,00,000 at the end of year 2, nothing in year
    3 and ₹ 3,00,000 at the end of year 4. Money is worth 11% per annum. What single amount payable
    today is equivalent, and how does it compare with the ₹ 6,50,000 face value of the offer?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Build the discount factors</div>
    (1.11)^(−1) = 0.9009 · (1.11)^(−2) = 0.8116 · (1.11)^(−4) = 0.6587<br />
    Each is the reciprocal of the matching compound factor: 1 ÷ 1.11 = 0.9009, 1 ÷ 1.2321 = 0.8116,
    1 ÷ 1.518070 = 0.6587.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Discount each flow separately</div>
    Year 1: 1,50,000 × 0.900901 = ₹ 1,35,135.14<br />
    Year 2: 2,00,000 × 0.811622 = ₹ 1,62,324.49<br />
    Year 3: nil<br />
    Year 4: 3,00,000 × 0.658731 = ₹ 1,97,619.28<br />
    Present value = <strong>₹ 4,95,078.91</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — What the comparison means</div>
    The face value is ₹ 6,50,000, so the deferral costs the seller ₹ 1,54,921 in today's money. Any
    cash offer above ₹ 4,95,079 today beats this stream. The annuity formula is no help here: the

  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — a recurring deposit, end of quarter against beginning (moderate)">
  <p>
    Sunitha deposits ₹ 8,000 into a recurring deposit at the <strong>end</strong> of every quarter
    for 4 years. The bank pays 8% per annum compounded quarterly. Find the maturity amount, and find
    what it would have been had she deposited at the <strong>beginning</strong> of every quarter.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Convert the rate and the time</div>
    Rate per quarter i = 0.08 ÷ 4 = 0.02<br />
    Number of quarters n = 4 years × 4 = 16<br />
    Deposit R = ₹ 8,000 per quarter. Total put in = 16 × 8,000 = ₹ 1,28,000.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Ordinary annuity (end of each quarter)</div>
    (1.02)¹⁶ = 1.3728 to four decimal places (1.372786 unrounded)<br />
    Factor A(16, 2%) = (1.372786 − 1) ÷ 0.02 = 18.6393<br />
    Maturity amount = 8,000 × 18.639285 = <strong>₹ 1,49,114.28</strong><br />
    Interest earned = 1,49,114.28 − 1,28,000 = ₹ 21,114.28
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Annuity due (beginning of each quarter)</div>
    Multiply the ordinary annuity result by (1 + i) = 1.02<br />
    Maturity amount = 1,49,114.28 × 1.02 = <strong>₹ 1,52,096.57</strong><br />
    Depositing three months earlier each time is worth ₹ 2,982.29 more on the same ₹ 1,28,000 —
    exactly one quarter's interest on the whole accumulated fund.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 6 — lease instalments in advance against an outright price (moderate-hard)">
  <p>
    A printing press can be bought outright for ₹ 16,50,000, or leased for five years at
    ₹ 4,00,000 payable at the <strong>beginning</strong> of each year, with the press passing to the
    lessee at the end at no further cost. The firm's cost of capital is 12%. Which is cheaper, and
    by how much?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Identify the annuity</div>
    Five equal payments, at the start of each year, so this is an <strong>annuity due</strong> of
    n = 5, R = ₹ 4,00,000, i = 0.12. The first payment is made today and is not discounted at all.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Ordinary annuity factor first</div>
    (1.12)^(−5) = 0.567427<br />
    P(5, 12%) = (1 − 0.567427) ÷ 0.12 = 0.432573 ÷ 0.12 = <strong>3.6048</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Convert to an annuity due and compare</div>
    Annuity-due factor = 3.604776 × 1.12 = 4.037349<br />
    Present value of the lease = 4,00,000 × 4.037349 = <strong>₹ 16,14,939.74</strong><br />
    Outright price = ₹ 16,50,000<br />
    <strong>Leasing is cheaper by ₹ 35,060.26</strong> in today's money.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — The trap</div>
    Treating the lease as an ordinary annuity gives 4,00,000 × 3.604776 = ₹ 14,41,910.40 and makes
    the lease look ₹ 2,08,090 cheaper than it is. One missing multiplication by 1.12 changes the
    size of the advantage sixfold, though not, here, the decision itself.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 7 — a sinking fund for debenture redemption (moderate)">
  <p>
    A company must redeem ₹ 40,00,000 of debentures at par at the end of 6 years. It will build a
    sinking fund by equal deposits at the end of each year, earning 8.5% per annum compounded
    yearly. Find the yearly deposit and the interest the fund earns over its life.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — The compound factor</div>
    (1.085)² = 1.177225 · (1.085)⁴ = 1.385859 · (1.085)⁶ = 1.385859 × 1.177225 = <strong>1.6315</strong><br />
    Three multiplications, no table needed.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — The annuity factor and the deposit</div>
    A(6, 8.5%) = (1.631468 − 1) ÷ 0.085 = 0.631468 ÷ 0.085 = 7.4290<br />
    Deposit R = 40,00,000 ÷ 7.429030 = <strong>₹ 5,38,428.34 a year</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Where the rest comes from</div>
    Six deposits total 6 × 5,38,428.34 = ₹ 32,30,570.02<br />
    Interest earned inside the fund = 40,00,000 − 32,30,570.02 = <strong>₹ 7,69,429.98</strong><br />
    Had the deposits been made at the beginning of each year, the divisor becomes
    7.429030 × 1.085 = 8.060497 and the deposit falls to ₹ 4,96,247.31.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 8 — a loan amortisation schedule, line by line (hard)">
  <p>
    A firm borrows ₹ 12,00,000 at 9% per annum, repayable in 5 equal yearly instalments, the first
    falling due one year after the loan is taken. Find the instalment, prepare the amortisation
    schedule, and state the amount still outstanding immediately after the third instalment.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — The instalment</div>
    (1.09)^(−5) = 0.649931<br />
    P(5, 9%) = (1 − 0.649931) ÷ 0.09 = 0.350069 ÷ 0.09 = 3.8897<br />
    R = 12,00,000 ÷ 3.889651 = <strong>₹ 3,08,510.95</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — The schedule (interest is always on the opening balance)</div>
    Year 1: opening 12,00,000.00 · interest 1,08,000.00 · principal 2,00,510.95 · closing 9,99,489.05<br />
    Year 2: opening 9,99,489.05 · interest 89,954.01 · principal 2,18,556.93 · closing 7,80,932.12<br />
    Year 3: opening 7,80,932.12 · interest 70,283.89 · principal 2,38,227.06 · closing 5,42,705.06<br />
    Year 4: opening 5,42,705.06 · interest 48,843.46 · principal 2,59,667.49 · closing 2,83,037.57<br />
    Year 5: opening 2,83,037.57 · interest 25,473.38 · principal 2,83,037.57 · closing <strong>0.00</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Balance after three instalments, without the schedule</div>
    Two instalments remain, so the balance is their present value:<br />
    P(2, 9%) = (1 − (1.09)^(−2)) ÷ 0.09 = 1.759111<br />
    Balance = 3,08,510.95 × 1.759111 = <strong>₹ 5,42,705.06</strong> — the same figure the schedule
    produced, reached in one line.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — Totals as a check</div>
    Total paid = 5 × 3,08,510.95 = ₹ 15,42,554.74; of that ₹ 12,00,000 is principal and
    <strong>₹ 3,42,554.74</strong> is interest. The principal column of the schedule adds back to
    exactly ₹ 12,00,000, which is the arithmetic proof that the instalment was correct.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 9 — NPV and profitability index for two projects (hard)">
  <p>
    A firm with a cost of capital of 13% is looking at two proposals. <strong>Project P</strong>
    costs ₹ 10,00,000 today and returns ₹ 4,00,000 at the end of each of 4 years.
    <strong>Project Q</strong> costs ₹ 11,00,000 today and returns ₹ 3,00,000, ₹ 4,00,000,
    ₹ 5,00,000 and ₹ 6,00,000 at the ends of years 1 to 4. Compute the NPV and the profitability
    index of each, and advise (a) if both can be funded and (b) if only one can be.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Discount factors at 13%</div>
    Year 1: 1 ÷ 1.13 = 0.8850 · Year 2: 0.7831 · Year 3: 0.6931 · Year 4: 0.6133<br />
    Their total, 2.9745, is P(4, 13%) — the annuity factor is nothing but the sum of the separate
    factors, which is why Project P needs only one multiplication.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Project P (equal inflows, so use the annuity factor)</div>
    Present value of inflows = 4,00,000 × 2.974471 = ₹ 11,89,788.53<br />
    NPV = 11,89,788.53 − 10,00,000 = <strong>₹ 1,89,788.53</strong><br />
    PI = 11,89,788.53 ÷ 10,00,000 = <strong>1.1898</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Project Q (unequal inflows, so discount one by one)</div>
    3,00,000 × 0.884956 = ₹ 2,65,486.73<br />
    4,00,000 × 0.783147 = ₹ 3,13,258.68<br />
    5,00,000 × 0.693050 = ₹ 3,46,525.09<br />
    6,00,000 × 0.613319 = ₹ 3,67,991.24<br />
    Present value of inflows = ₹ 12,93,261.72<br />
    NPV = 12,93,261.72 − 11,00,000 = <strong>₹ 1,93,261.72</strong>; PI = <strong>1.1757</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — The advice, and why the two measures disagree</div>
    (a) Both NPVs are positive, so if funds allow, <strong>accept both</strong>.<br />
    (b) If the two are mutually exclusive, take <strong>Project Q</strong>: it adds ₹ 3,473 more
    value. Project Q wins on NPV but loses on PI, because it needs ₹ 1,00,000 more capital to earn
    that extra value. The rankings can disagree whenever the outlays differ, and NPV is the measure
    that answers "which single project makes us richer".
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 10 — CAGR and a growing endowment (moderate)">
  <p>
    A firm's revenue grew from ₹ 18,00,000 in 2021-22 to ₹ 32,00,000 in 2025-26. (a) Find the
    compound annual growth rate. (b) The founder now wants to endow a scholarship of ₹ 1,20,000
    payable at the end of next year and rising 4% a year for ever. If the endowment fund earns 9%
    per annum, what capital sum is required?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) Count the years, then take the root</div>
    From 2021-22 to 2025-26 is <strong>4</strong> years, not 5 — count the gaps between the years,
    not the years named.<br />
    Ratio = 32,00,000 ÷ 18,00,000 = 1.777778<br />
    CAGR = (1.777778)^(1/4) − 1 = 1.154701 − 1 = 0.154701 = <strong>15.47% per annum</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Check the answer forwards</div>
    18,00,000 × (1.154701)⁴ = 18,00,000 × 1.777778 = ₹ 32,00,000 ✓<br />
    A wrong year count of 5 would have given 12.19%, an option the examiner will certainly offer.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (b) A growing perpetuity</div>
    R = ₹ 1,20,000 (the payment one year from now), i = 0.09, g = 0.04, and g is less than i, so the
    formula applies.<br />
    Capital required = 1,20,000 ÷ (0.09 − 0.04) = 1,20,000 ÷ 0.05 = <strong>₹ 24,00,000</strong><br />
    A level scholarship of ₹ 1,20,000 for ever would have needed only 1,20,000 ÷ 0.09 = ₹ 13,33,333.
    Protecting the scholarship against a 4% drift in prices nearly doubles the endowment.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 11 — valuing a bond with half-yearly coupons (hard)">
  <p>
    A bond of face value ₹ 1,00,000 carries a coupon of 8% per annum payable half-yearly and is
    redeemable at par at the end of 4 years. Investors in similar bonds now require 10% per annum.
    What is the bond worth today, and why is it not ₹ 1,00,000?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — Convert everything to half-years</div>
    Coupon per half-year = 1,00,000 × 8% ÷ 2 = <strong>₹ 4,000</strong><br />
    Yield per half-year = 10% ÷ 2 = <strong>5%</strong> · Periods n = 4 × 2 = <strong>8</strong><br />
    The coupon uses the coupon rate on the face value; the discounting uses the yield. Two rates,
    two jobs.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — The two factors</div>
    (1.05)⁸ = 1.477455, so the discount factor is 1 ÷ 1.477455 = <strong>0.6768</strong><br />
    P(8, 5%) = (1 − 0.676839) ÷ 0.05 = <strong>6.4632</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — Add the annuity and the single sum</div>
    Present value of the coupons = 4,000 × 6.463213 = ₹ 25,852.85<br />
    Present value of the redemption = 1,00,000 × 0.676839 = ₹ 67,683.94<br />
    Value of the bond = <strong>₹ 93,536.79</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — Why the discount</div>
    The bond pays 8% while the market wants 10%. A buyer will only accept the smaller coupon if the
    price is low enough for the shortfall to be recovered on redemption, so the bond trades ₹ 6,463
    below par. Had the required return been 8%, the two factors would have produced exactly
    ₹ 1,00,000, and at 6% the bond would have sold at a premium.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

- **Using the simple interest formula where compounding was required.** P i t and P[(1 + i)^n − 1]
  agree for one period and never again. If a question mentions "compounded", "converted" or a
  conversion period, the straight-line formula is wrong from period two onward.
- **Leaving the rate as a percentage.** 8% is 0.08 in every formula on this page. An answer a
  hundred times too big, or a factor like 8.0 where 1.08 belonged, is this error.
- **Not adjusting for the conversion period.** Quarterly compounding needs the rate divided by 4
  <em>and</em> the years multiplied by 4. Doing one without the other is the single most productive
  distractor an examiner has in this chapter.
- **Treating an annuity due as an ordinary annuity.** Payments "in advance", "at the beginning" or
  "the first today" need one extra multiplication by (1 + i). Applying it twice is just as wrong.
- **Confusing the two annuity factors.** P(n, i) is always less than n; A(n, i) is always more than
  n. Check your factor against the number of payments before you multiply.
- **Discounting one period too many or too few.** A payment at the end of year 3 carries the factor
  (1 + i)^(−3), not (1 + i)^(−4). For a deferred annuity, the annuity factor already values the
  stream one period before its first payment; discount only the remaining gap.
- **Discounting the initial outlay in an NPV.** Money spent today has a factor of 1.
- **Using the nominal rate where the effective rate was needed**, or comparing two offers on their
  nominal rates alone. Convert both to effective rates and then compare.
- **Using the coupon rate to discount a bond.** The coupon rate produces the rupee coupon; the
  yield does the discounting. Swapping them always returns the face value.
- **Counting years wrongly in a CAGR.** From 2021-22 to 2025-26 is 4 years. Count the intervals,
  not the labels.
- **Averaging growth rates instead of compounding them.** The arithmetic mean of yearly growth
  rates overstates the true CAGR whenever the rates differ.
- **Charging interest on the original loan in an amortisation schedule.** Interest each period is
  on that period's opening balance.
- **Rounding factors too early.** Carry the factor unrounded through the arithmetic and round the
  money at the end. Two-decimal factors on lakh-sized sums land squarely on a wrong option.
- **Forgetting that g must be less than i** in a growing perpetuity, and that the numerator is next
  period's payment, not the last one paid.

## One-page revision summary

**Symbols.** P = principal or present value · A or FV = amount / future value · I = interest ·
i = rate per conversion period as a decimal · r = annual nominal rate · E = effective annual rate ·
n = number of conversion periods · m = conversions per year · R = the periodic annuity payment ·
g = growth rate per period · y = yield per period · e = 2.71828.

**Interest and growth**

<div class="formula">Simple interest: I = P i t · A = P (1 + i t)</div>
<div class="formula">Compound interest: A = P (1 + i)^n · CI = P [(1 + i)^n − 1]</div>
<div class="formula">Conversion periods: i = r ÷ m and n = years × m — always change both together</div>
<div class="formula">Decay: Value after n periods = P (1 − d)^n</div>
<div class="formula">Rate from two values: i = (A ÷ P)^(1/n) − 1 · Time: n = (log A − log P) ÷ log (1 + i)</div>
<div class="formula">Effective rate: E = (1 + r ÷ m)^m − 1 · Nominal from effective: r = m [(1 + E)^(1/m) − 1]</div>
<div class="formula">Continuous compounding: A = P e^(r t) · E = e^r − 1</div>

**Present and future value**

<div class="formula">FV = PV (1 + i)^n · PV = FV (1 + i)^(−n)</div>
<div class="formula">Discount factor = 1 ÷ compound factor</div>

**Annuities** (R each period, i per period, n periods)

<div class="formula">FV of an ordinary annuity: A(n, i) = R [(1 + i)^n − 1] ÷ i — the factor always exceeds n</div>
<div class="formula">PV of an ordinary annuity: P(n, i) = R [1 − (1 + i)^(−n)] ÷ i — the factor is always below n</div>
<div class="formula">Annuity due: take the ordinary annuity value and multiply by (1 + i)</div>
<div class="formula">Perpetuity: PV = R ÷ i · Growing perpetuity: PV = R ÷ (i − g), needing g below i</div>
<div class="formula">Link: P(n, i) × (1 + i)^n = A(n, i)</div>

**Applications**

<div class="formula">Sinking fund deposit: R = S i ÷ [(1 + i)^n − 1]</div>
<div class="formula">Loan instalment: R = Loan × i ÷ [1 − (1 + i)^(−n)]</div>
<div class="formula">Interest for the period = opening balance × i · Principal repaid = instalment − interest</div>
<div class="formula">Balance after k instalments = R × P(n − k, i)</div>
<div class="formula">NPV = PV of inflows − PV of outflows · Accept if NPV is positive</div>
<div class="formula">Profitability index = PV of inflows ÷ PV of the outlay · Accept if above 1</div>
<div class="formula">CAGR = (Ending ÷ Beginning)^(1 ÷ n) − 1, n counted in years between the two figures</div>
<div class="formula">Bond value = Coupon × P(n, y) + Redemption value × (1 + y)^(−n)</div>

**Conventions used on this page.** Factors are quoted to four decimal places and carried unrounded
into the arithmetic; money is rounded at the last step only. A year is 365 days for simple interest,
and months are exact twelfths. An annuity is an ordinary annuity unless the question says the
payments come at the beginning. Interest is added at the end of each conversion period and is not
rounded period by period.

**Direction of every relationship.** More frequent compounding raises the amount · a higher rate or
a longer wait lowers a present value · the annuity-due value always exceeds the ordinary annuity
value · a bond yields above its coupon rate only when it sells below par · compound interest exceeds
simple interest from the second period onward.
