A garment unit at Tiruppur commissions a new stitching line in April. The line produces 1,200 pieces
in its first month, and the supervisor plans for output to rise by a steady 150 pieces every month as
the operators settle in. Two questions land on the owner's desk in the same week. What will the line
make in the twelfth month, and how many pieces will it have made in the whole year?

They look like the same question. They are not. The first asks for **one** month's figure, the
twelfth term of a list. The second asks for the **total** of all twelve figures. The list is a
*sequence*; the running total is a *series*. Getting 1,200 + 11 × 150 = 2,850 pieces for the twelfth
month and 12 ÷ 2 × (1,200 + 2,850) = 24,300 pieces for the year is the whole of this chapter in two
lines.

Almost every mark in Paper 3 on this topic turns on three small decisions: whether the question wants
a term or a total, whether the count of terms is n or n − 1, and whether the pattern grows by adding
a fixed amount or by multiplying by a fixed factor. The arithmetic is light. The slips are all in
those three places, and the option lists are built out of them.

## 1. What a sequence is and how a series differs

A **sequence** is a list of numbers written in a definite order, where each number's position matters.
The numbers are its **terms**. The first term is written a₁ (or simply a), the second a₂, and the term
in position n is aₙ, read as "the nth term" or "the general term".

<div class="formula">1,200, 1,350, 1,500, 1,650, … is a sequence. Its 1st term is 1,200 and its 3rd term is 1,500.</div>

Order is part of the identity of a sequence. The list 5, 9, 13 and the list 13, 9, 5 contain the same
three numbers but are different sequences, because the term in position 1 differs.

A **series** is what you get when you join the terms of a sequence with plus signs — that is, when you
add them. The sum of the first n terms is written Sₙ.

<div class="formula">1,200 + 1,350 + 1,500 + 1,650 is a series. Its value, S₄, is 5,700.</div>

So a sequence is a list and a series is a total. A question asking "what does the line produce in the
twelfth month" wants a term; a question asking "what does the line produce in the first twelve months"
wants a sum. Reading the wrong one is the single most common way to lose a mark here, and the option
list will always contain both numbers.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Three words in a stem decide which formula you need. <strong>"In the nth month", "on the nth day",
    "the nth term"</strong> point to a term. <strong>"Altogether", "in total", "over the first n
    years", "how many in all"</strong> point to a sum. Underline the word before you write anything.
  </p>
</Callout>

A **progression** is a sequence built by a stated rule that lets you write down the next term from the
one before it. Two rules matter at Foundation level, and they are the two ways of changing a number:

- add a fixed quantity each time — an **arithmetic progression (AP)**;
- multiply by a fixed factor each time — a **geometric progression (GP)**.

Every progression is a sequence, but not every sequence is a progression. The list 2, 3, 5, 7, 11 —
the primes — is a perfectly good sequence with no fixed difference and no fixed ratio, so it is
neither an AP nor a GP.

## 2. Finite and infinite sequences and the general term

A sequence with a last term is **finite**; the number of terms in it is its **length**. A sequence
that continues without end is **infinite**, and is written with a trailing ellipsis.

| Sequence | Kind | Length |
|---|---|---|
| 3, 7, 11, …, 99 | finite AP | 25 terms |
| 5, 10, 20, 40, 80 | finite GP | 5 terms |
| 2, 5, 8, 11, … | infinite AP | endless |
| 1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, … | infinite GP | endless |

Where the general term aₙ is given as a formula in n, every term follows by substitution. If
aₙ = 4n − 1 then a₁ = 3, a₂ = 7, a₃ = 11 — an AP with first term 3 and common difference 4. That is
worth remembering in reverse as well: **a general term that is linear in n always describes an AP**,
and the coefficient of n is its common difference.

A general term of the shape aₙ = k·mⁿ describes a GP, and m is its common ratio: aₙ = 3 × 2ⁿ gives
6, 12, 24, 48, which is a GP with first term 6 and ratio 2.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    A formula for the general term is <em>not</em> the same as a formula for the sum. If aₙ = 4n − 1,
    then a₅ = 19, but S₅ = 3 + 7 + 11 + 15 + 19 = 55. An option offering 19 to a question that asked
    for S₅ is there for exactly this reason.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. Arithmetic progression and its nth term

An **arithmetic progression** is a sequence in which each term after the first is obtained by adding
a fixed number to the previous term. That fixed number is the **common difference**, written d, and
it is found by subtracting any term from the one that follows it.

<div class="formula">d = a₂ − a₁ = a₃ − a₂ = … = aₙ − aₙ₋₁</div>

d may be positive (the sequence rises), negative (it falls) or zero (every term is the same). In
1,200, 1,350, 1,500, … the difference is 150. In 40, 33, 26, 19, … it is −7.

Writing the terms out shows the pattern that gives the formula. With first term a and common
difference d, the terms are a, a + d, a + 2d, a + 3d, … The second term carries **one** d, the third
carries **two**, and so the nth term carries **n − 1**:

<div class="formula">Tₙ = a + (n − 1)d</div>

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — the 12th month of the stitching line</div>
  a = 1,200 pieces · d = 150 pieces · n = 12<br />
  T₁₂ = 1,200 + (12 − 1) × 150 = 1,200 + 11 × 150 = 1,200 + 1,650 = <strong>2,850 pieces</strong><br />
  Check by counting: from month 1 to month 12 there are 11 <em>steps</em>, not 12. Using 12 × 150
  gives 3,000, which is the thirteenth month's figure.
</div>

The n − 1 is where marks go. The number of *steps* between the first term and the nth is always one
fewer than the number of *terms*, in the same way that a fence with 12 posts has 11 gaps.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Using <code>a + nd</code> instead of <code>a + (n − 1)d</code> shifts every answer forward by
    exactly one d. In a well-set question that wrong value is <strong>always one of the four
    options</strong>. Before you accept an answer, ask "how many steps did I take from the first
    term?" — the answer must be n − 1.
  </p>
</Callout>

The formula has four letters, so any three of a, d, n and Tₙ determine the fourth. Two shapes appear
constantly:

- **Two terms given.** Subtracting Tₚ = a + (p − 1)d from T_q = a + (q − 1)d gives
  T_q − Tₚ = (q − p)d, so d = (T_q − Tₚ) ÷ (q − p). Find d first, then a.
- **A term's value given, n wanted.** Rearrange to n = (Tₙ − a) ÷ d + 1. The "+ 1" is the same
  n − 1 seen from the other side, and dropping it is the same error as before.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — the 7th term is 43 and the 15th is 91</div>
  T₁₅ − T₇ = (15 − 7)d → 91 − 43 = 8d → 48 = 8d → <strong>d = 6</strong><br />
  T₇ = a + 6d → 43 = a + 36 → <strong>a = 7</strong><br />
  Check T₁₅: 7 + 14 × 6 = 7 + 84 = 91 ✓ — always check with the term you did not use.
</div>

## 4. The sum of n terms of an AP

Write the sum forwards and then backwards, one line under the other:

<div class="formula">Sₙ = a + (a + d) + … + (l − d) + l &nbsp;&nbsp;and&nbsp;&nbsp; Sₙ = l + (l − d) + … + (a + d) + a</div>

Add the two lines column by column. Every column gives the same total a + l, and there are n columns,
so 2Sₙ = n(a + l). That gives the first form of the sum, in which l is the **last term** of the n
terms being added:

<div class="formula">Sₙ = (n ÷ 2) × (a + l)</div>

Substituting l = a + (n − 1)d gives the second form, used when the last term is not known:

<div class="formula">Sₙ = (n ÷ 2) × [2a + (n − 1)d]</div>

The two forms are the same statement. Use the first when the first and last terms are both in front
of you, and the second when you know a, d and how many terms there are.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — a year of output from the stitching line</div>
  a = 1,200 · d = 150 · n = 12, and from §3 the last term l = T₁₂ = 2,850<br />
  First form: S₁₂ = (12 ÷ 2) × (1,200 + 2,850) = 6 × 4,050 = <strong>24,300 pieces</strong><br />
  Second form: S₁₂ = 6 × [2,400 + 11 × 150] = 6 × [2,400 + 1,650] = 6 × 4,050 = 24,300 ✓<br />
  Sanity check: the average month is (1,200 + 2,850) ÷ 2 = 2,025 pieces, and 12 × 2,025 = 24,300 ✓
</div>

That last line is worth keeping. **The mean of an AP's terms is the mean of its first and last term**,
so Sₙ = n × (average term). It gives a fast independent check on every sum you compute.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A useful consequence: <strong>Tₙ = Sₙ − Sₙ₋₁</strong>. If a question hands you a formula for the
    sum, such as Sₙ = 3n² + 5n, the nth term is the difference of two consecutive sums:
    Tₙ = (3n² + 5n) − [3(n−1)² + 5(n−1)] = 6n + 2. Putting n = 1 gives a = 8, and the coefficient of
    n gives d = 6.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. The nth term of an AP counted from the end

Sometimes the question counts backwards: "what is the 5th term from the end of the AP 7, 11, 15, …,
139?" Two methods both work, and knowing both means you never have to remember a formula under
pressure.

**Method 1 — reverse the progression.** Read the AP from its last term. Reversing an AP gives another
AP whose first term is l and whose common difference is −d. So the nth term from the end is

<div class="formula">nth term from the end = l − (n − 1)d</div>

**Method 2 — convert to a position from the front.** If the AP has m terms altogether, the nth term
from the end sits at position m − n + 1 from the front.

<div class="formula">position from the front = m − n + 1</div>

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — the 5th term from the end of 7, 11, 15, …, 139</div>
  a = 7 · d = 4 · l = 139<br />
  Method 1: 139 − (5 − 1) × 4 = 139 − 16 = <strong>123</strong><br />
  Method 2 needs the length first: 139 = 7 + (m − 1) × 4 → 132 = 4(m − 1) → m − 1 = 33 → m = 34<br />
  Position from the front = 34 − 5 + 1 = 30, and T₃₀ = 7 + 29 × 4 = 7 + 116 = <strong>123</strong> ✓
</div>

Both methods give 123. Method 1 is faster when the last term is given; method 2 is safer when the
question also asks how many terms there are.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    "The 5th term from the end" is <strong>not</strong> l − 5d, and it is not the 5th term counted
    after the last. The last term is itself the <em>1st</em> from the end, so only four steps of d
    separate it from the 5th. The off-by-one here produces l − 5d = 119, which will be sitting in the
    option list next to the right answer.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. How many terms of an AP lie in a range

A whole family of questions asks for a **count**: how many multiples of 7 lie between 100 and 500,
how many terms of a given AP are three-digit numbers, how many months pass before output reaches a
target. All of them use the same three steps.

1. Identify the **first** term of the AP that lies inside the range, and the **last**.
2. Find the common difference d, which is the spacing of the pattern.
3. Count with **n = (last − first) ÷ d + 1**.

<div class="formula">Number of terms = (last term − first term) ÷ common difference + 1</div>

The "+ 1" is the fence-post rule again: dividing gives the number of *gaps*, and terms outnumber gaps
by one.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — how many multiples of 7 lie between 100 and 500</div>
  First multiple of 7 above 100: 7 × 15 = 105 (since 100 ÷ 7 = 14.28…)<br />
  Last multiple of 7 below 500: 7 × 71 = 497 (since 500 ÷ 7 = 71.42…)<br />
  d = 7 → n = (497 − 105) ÷ 7 + 1 = 392 ÷ 7 + 1 = 56 + 1 = <strong>57 terms</strong><br />
  Cross-check by index: the multiples are 7 × 15 up to 7 × 71, and 71 − 15 + 1 = 57 ✓
</div>

The words at the edge of the range decide the endpoints, so read them exactly. **"Between 100 and
500"** normally excludes both 100 and 500; **"from 100 to 500"** and **"between 100 and 500, both
inclusive"** include them. Here 100 and 500 are not multiples of 7, so the two readings agree — but in
a question about multiples of 5 between 100 and 500 they differ by two terms, which is precisely why
such a question is set.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Dividing and stopping is the classic error: 392 ÷ 7 = 56 counts the <em>gaps</em>, not the terms.
    A shorter case makes it obvious — the multiples of 7 from 14 to 28 are 14, 21, 28, which is three
    terms, while (28 − 14) ÷ 7 = 2. Whenever a count feels uncertain, test your method on a list short
    enough to write out.
  </p>
</Callout>

A second shape of the same question asks **when** a running quantity first crosses a level. Solve
a + (n − 1)d ≥ target for n and then **round up** to the next whole number, because n counts terms and
must be an integer. Rounding down answers the opposite question.

## 7. Terms equidistant from the two ends

In a finite AP, take the first and last terms and add them. Now take the second and the second-last
and add them. The two totals are equal, and so is every other such pair.

<div class="formula">T₁ + Tₘ = T₂ + Tₘ₋₁ = T₃ + Tₘ₋₂ = … = a + l</div>

The reason is short. The kth term from the front is a + (k − 1)d and the kth from the end is
l − (k − 1)d; adding them cancels the (k − 1)d and leaves a + l for every k.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — the AP 4, 9, 14, 19, 24, 29, 34</div>
  First + last = 4 + 34 = 38<br />
  Second + second-last = 9 + 29 = 38 ✓ · Third + third-last = 14 + 24 = 38 ✓<br />
  The middle term 19 is half of 38, as it must be in an AP with an odd number of terms.
</div>

Two consequences are examined directly. First, in an AP with an **odd** number of terms the middle
term equals (a + l) ÷ 2, so the sum is (number of terms) × (middle term). Second, if a question tells
you that T₄ + T₁₇ = 90 in a 20-term AP, you can read off T₁ + T₂₀ = 90 as well, because 4 and 17 are
equidistant from the ends in the same way 1 and 20 are — and then S₂₀ = 10 × 90 = 900 without ever
finding a or d.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Whenever a stem gives you the <strong>sum of two terms whose positions add to one more than the
    number of terms</strong>, it is handing you a + l directly. Positions 4 and 17 in a 20-term AP add
    to 21 = 20 + 1; so do 1 and 20, and 8 and 13. Spotting that saves two equations of work.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. Arithmetic mean and inserting n arithmetic means

If three numbers a, A and b are in AP, then A is the **arithmetic mean** of a and b. Being in AP means
the two differences match, so A − a = b − A, and rearranging gives

<div class="formula">A = (a + b) ÷ 2</div>

That is the ordinary average, and the test works both ways: **three numbers are in AP exactly when the
middle one is the average of the outer two.** Checking whether 12, 20, 28 are in AP is one line —
(12 + 28) ÷ 2 = 20 ✓.

**Inserting n arithmetic means.** To insert n arithmetic means between two numbers a and b means to
find n numbers A₁, A₂, …, Aₙ such that

<div class="formula">a, A₁, A₂, …, Aₙ, b is a single AP</div>

Count the terms of that AP: the two endpoints plus the n inserted numbers, so **n + 2 terms in all**.
The endpoint b is therefore the (n + 2)th term:

<div class="formula">b = a + (n + 2 − 1)d = a + (n + 1)d, so d = (b − a) ÷ (n + 1)</div>

The denominator is n + 1 because inserting n numbers between two numbers creates n + 1 gaps.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — insert 4 arithmetic means between 6 and 41</div>
  a = 6 · b = 41 · n = 4 → d = (41 − 6) ÷ (4 + 1) = 35 ÷ 5 = <strong>7</strong><br />
  The means are 6 + 7 = 13, 20, 27, 34.<br />
  Full AP: 6, 13, 20, 27, 34, 41 — six terms, and 34 + 7 = 41 ✓<br />
  Using n = 4 in the denominator instead of 5 gives d = 8.75 and a list that misses 41 entirely.
</div>

Two facts follow that are asked directly. The **sum of the n inserted means is n × (a + b) ÷ 2**,
because the inserted terms are themselves an AP with the same overall average. In the example,
13 + 20 + 27 + 34 = 94 and 4 × (6 + 41) ÷ 2 = 4 × 23.5 = 94 ✓.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The two endpoints are <strong>not</strong> among the means. "Insert 4 arithmetic means between 6
    and 41" asks for four numbers, not six, and the AP that contains them has six terms. Answering
    with the whole AP, or dividing by n rather than n + 1, are the two errors the option list is
    built from. See the citations file — this counting convention is one a reviewer should confirm.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Geometric progression and its nth term

A **geometric progression** is a sequence in which each term after the first is obtained by
multiplying the previous term by a fixed non-zero number. That number is the **common ratio**, r, and
it is found by dividing any term by the one before it.

<div class="formula">r = a₂ ÷ a₁ = a₃ ÷ a₂ = … = aₙ ÷ aₙ₋₁</div>

The word is *ratio*, and the operation is division, not subtraction. In 5, 15, 45, 135 the ratio is
15 ÷ 5 = 3, not 15 − 5 = 10. In 80, 40, 20, 10 the ratio is 1/2. In 3, −6, 12, −24 the ratio is −2,
and the alternating signs are the signature of a negative ratio.

Writing the terms out gives the formula in the same way as before. With first term a and ratio r the
terms are a, ar, ar², ar³, … The second term carries **one** factor of r, so the nth carries n − 1:

<div class="formula">Tₙ = a·r ⁿ⁻¹</div>

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — a distributor's units sold grow 20% a year</div>
  Year 1 sales 25,000 units, and each year is 1.2 times the year before, so a = 25,000 and r = 1.2<br />
  Year 5 sales = T₅ = 25,000 × 1.2⁴ = 25,000 × 2.0736 = <strong>51,840 units</strong><br />
  Using r⁵ instead of r⁴ gives 62,208, which is year 6 — the same n − 1 slip as in an AP, wearing a
  different hat.
</div>

A growth of p per cent per period means r = 1 + p/100; a decline of p per cent means r = 1 − p/100.
So 20% growth is r = 1.2 and a 15% fall is r = 0.85.

The same three-of-four rule applies. Dividing one term by another kills a: T_q ÷ Tₚ = r ^(q − p). If
T₃ = 20 and T₇ = 320, then r⁴ = 16 so r = 2 (taking the positive root; r = −2 also satisfies r⁴ = 16,
and a well-set stem says which sign applies or gives positive terms).

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Treating the common ratio as a difference is the most costly single error in the GP half of this
    chapter. In 4, 12, 36, … the ratio is 3; a student who writes d = 8 produces 4, 12, 20, 28 and
    every later answer is wrong. Test the pattern on <strong>two</strong> consecutive pairs before
    deciding whether it is an AP or a GP.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. The sum of n terms of a GP

Write the sum and then r times the sum, one under the other:

<div class="formula">Sₙ = a + ar + ar² + … + arⁿ⁻¹ &nbsp;&nbsp;and&nbsp;&nbsp; rSₙ = ar + ar² + … + arⁿ⁻¹ + arⁿ</div>

Subtract. Everything in the middle cancels and only two terms survive: Sₙ − rSₙ = a − arⁿ, so
Sₙ(1 − r) = a(1 − rⁿ). Dividing gives the sum, written in two arrangements that differ only in which
way round the subtractions run:

<div class="formula">Sₙ = a(1 − rⁿ) ÷ (1 − r), used when r &lt; 1</div>
<div class="formula">Sₙ = a(rⁿ − 1) ÷ (r − 1), used when r &gt; 1</div>

The two are the same formula: multiplying the top and bottom of one by −1 gives the other. Choosing
the arrangement whose numerator and denominator are both positive simply avoids handling two minus
signs. **When r = 1 neither form works**, because the denominator is zero; but then every term equals
a and the sum is simply na.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — total units over the distributor's first 5 years</div>
  a = 25,000 · r = 1.2 · n = 5, and r &gt; 1 so use the second arrangement<br />
  1.2⁵ = 2.48832 → S₅ = 25,000 × (2.48832 − 1) ÷ (1.2 − 1) = 25,000 × 1.48832 ÷ 0.2<br />
  = 25,000 × 7.4416 = <strong>1,86,040 units</strong><br />
  Check by adding the five terms: 25,000 + 30,000 + 36,000 + 43,200 + 51,840 = 1,86,040 ✓
</div>

That check is worth doing whenever n is small. **Building the five terms and adding them is a genuinely
independent test**; re-substituting into the same formula only repeats whatever slip you made.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — sum of 6 terms of 96, 48, 24, … (r &lt; 1)</div>
  a = 96 · r = 1/2 · n = 6 → use a(1 − rⁿ) ÷ (1 − r)<br />
  (1/2)⁶ = 1/64 → S₆ = 96 × (1 − 1/64) ÷ (1 − 1/2) = 96 × (63/64) ÷ (1/2) = 96 × 63/32 = <strong>189</strong><br />
  Check term by term: 96 + 48 + 24 + 12 + 6 + 3 = 189 ✓
</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    An alternative form is often quicker when the last term is known:
    <strong>Sₙ = (l·r − a) ÷ (r − 1)</strong>, where l is the last term. It follows by writing
    l = arⁿ⁻¹ so that arⁿ = lr. In the second example, l = 3 and
    (3 × ½ − 96) ÷ (½ − 1) = (−94.5) ÷ (−0.5) = 189 ✓
  </p>
</Callout>

## 11. The sum of an infinite GP

Add the terms of 96, 48, 24, 12, … without stopping. The partial sums are 96, 144, 168, 180, 186, 189,
190.5, … creeping upwards but never past 192. That ceiling is the **sum to infinity**.

It exists only when the terms shrink, and the terms shrink exactly when the ratio is smaller than 1 in
size. Formally, rⁿ tends to 0 as n grows if and only if |r| &lt; 1, and putting rⁿ = 0 in the finite
formula leaves

<div class="formula">S∞ = a ÷ (1 − r), valid only when |r| &lt; 1</div>

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — the sum to infinity of 96, 48, 24, …</div>
  a = 96 · r = 1/2, and |1/2| &lt; 1 so the sum exists<br />
  S∞ = 96 ÷ (1 − 1/2) = 96 ÷ (1/2) = <strong>192</strong><br />
  The partial sums above approach 192 from below and never reach it. The finite sum S₆ = 189 is
  already within 3 of the limit.
</div>

The condition is the whole examinable point, so read it carefully.

| Common ratio | Behaviour of the terms | Sum to infinity |
|---|---|---|
| \|r\| &lt; 1, for example 1/3 or −0.4 | shrink towards zero | exists, equal to a ÷ (1 − r) |
| r = 1 | every term equals a | does not exist — the partial sums are na and grow without limit |
| r = −1 | terms alternate a, −a, a, −a | does not exist — the partial sums oscillate between a and 0 |
| \|r\| &gt; 1, for example 2 or −3 | grow in size | does not exist — the partial sums grow without limit |

Note the sign of the ratio does not decide anything by itself: r = −0.4 gives a perfectly good sum,
because |−0.4| = 0.4 is less than 1. It is the **size** of r that matters.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Applying S∞ = a ÷ (1 − r) when |r| ≥ 1 produces a number, and the number looks plausible, which is
    what makes it dangerous. For 5, 10, 20, 40, … it gives 5 ÷ (1 − 2) = −5 — a negative total for a
    sum of positive growing terms, which is impossible on its face. Check |r| &lt; 1 <em>before</em>
    you divide, and if a stem asks for the sum to infinity of a growing GP, the answer is that no such
    sum exists.
  </p>
</Callout>

A recurring decimal is the standard application. 0.4̄ means 0.4 + 0.04 + 0.004 + …, a GP with
a = 4/10 and r = 1/10, so its value is (4/10) ÷ (9/10) = 4/9. The same method converts any recurring
decimal into a fraction, which is why the topic is examined here rather than in arithmetic.

## 12. The nth term of a GP counted from the end

The same two methods as for an AP, with multiplication in place of addition.

**Method 1 — reverse the progression.** Reading a finite GP backwards gives a GP with first term l and
ratio 1/r. So

<div class="formula">nth term from the end = l ÷ r ⁿ⁻¹ = l × (1/r)ⁿ⁻¹</div>

**Method 2 — convert the position.** In a GP of m terms, the nth from the end is the (m − n + 1)th
from the front.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — the 4th term from the end of 3, 6, 12, …, 1536</div>
  a = 3 · r = 2 · l = 1,536<br />
  Method 1: 1,536 ÷ 2⁴⁻¹ = 1,536 ÷ 8 = <strong>192</strong><br />
  Method 2: 1,536 = 3 × 2^(m−1) → 2^(m−1) = 512 = 2⁹ → m = 10 terms<br />
  Position from the front = 10 − 4 + 1 = 7, and T₇ = 3 × 2⁶ = 3 × 64 = <strong>192</strong> ✓
</div>

The off-by-one warning repeats exactly: the last term is the **1st** from the end, so the 4th from the
end is only three divisions back, l ÷ r³. Dividing by r⁴ gives 96, which is the 5th from the end.

## 13. Geometric mean and inserting n geometric means

If three numbers a, G and b are in GP, then G is the **geometric mean** of a and b. Being in GP means
the two ratios match, so G ÷ a = b ÷ G, hence G² = ab and

<div class="formula">G = √(ab), taken as the positive root when a and b are positive</div>

The test again works both ways: **three numbers are in GP exactly when the square of the middle one
equals the product of the outer two.** Checking 4, 10, 25: 10² = 100 and 4 × 25 = 100 ✓.

**Inserting n geometric means.** To insert n geometric means between a and b is to find G₁ … Gₙ such
that a, G₁, …, Gₙ, b is a single GP. As before the GP has n + 2 terms, so b is its (n + 2)th term:

<div class="formula">b = a·r ⁿ⁺¹, so r = (b ÷ a)^[1 ÷ (n + 1)]</div>

Again the exponent's denominator is n + 1, the number of gaps.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — insert 3 geometric means between 5 and 405</div>
  a = 5 · b = 405 · n = 3 → r⁴ = 405 ÷ 5 = 81 → r = <strong>3</strong> (positive root)<br />
  The means are 5 × 3 = 15, 45, 135.<br />
  Full GP: 5, 15, 45, 135, 405 — five terms, and 135 × 3 = 405 ✓<br />
  Using r³ = 81 instead of r⁴ gives r = 4.327 and a list that never lands on 405.
</div>

A companion result, asked directly: the **product of the n inserted geometric means is (ab)^(n/2)**,
which is Gⁿ where G is the geometric mean of a and b. In the example, 15 × 45 × 135 = 91,125, and
(5 × 405)^(3/2) = 2,025^1.5 = 45³ = 91,125 ✓.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    A geometric mean is a <strong>square root of a product</strong>, never an average. The GM of 4 and
    25 is √100 = 10, not (4 + 25) ÷ 2 = 14.5. When a stem says "in continued proportion", "in GP" or
    "the mean proportional", the operation is multiplication. The arithmetic mean will be one of the
    four options every time.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 14. The relation between the AM and the GM of two numbers

For two **positive** numbers a and b, write A = (a + b) ÷ 2 and G = √(ab). These two are never in
random order:

<div class="formula">A ≥ G, with equality only when a = b</div>

The proof is one line. A − G = (a + b) ÷ 2 − √(ab) = (√a − √b)² ÷ 2, and a square is never negative.
It is zero only when √a = √b, that is when a = b.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — a and b are 8 and 50</div>
  A = (8 + 50) ÷ 2 = 29 · G = √(8 × 50) = √400 = 20<br />
  29 &gt; 20 ✓, and the gap (√8 − √50)² ÷ 2 = (2.828 − 7.071)² ÷ 2 = 18.0 ÷ 2 = 9 = 29 − 20 ✓<br />
  With a = b = 20 instead: A = 20 and G = √400 = 20, so the two coincide.
</div>

Two further relations are worth carrying, because they turn a pair of AM and GM values back into the
original numbers:

<div class="formula">a + b = 2A &nbsp;·&nbsp; ab = G² &nbsp;·&nbsp; (a − b)² = 4A² − 4G² = 4(A + G)(A − G)</div>

So if the AM of two numbers is 29 and the GM is 20, then a + b = 58 and ab = 400, and the two numbers
are the roots of x² − 58x + 400 = 0, namely 8 and 50. That is the standard way these questions are
set: give A and G, ask for the numbers.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    The inequality is a fast sanity check. If a computed geometric mean comes out <em>larger</em> than
    the arithmetic mean of the same two positive numbers, something is wrong — almost always a square
    root not taken, or the sum used where the product belonged. A stem stating "the AM of two numbers
    is 15 and their GM is 17" describes numbers that cannot exist.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 15. Convenient symmetrical forms for three or four terms

When a question says "three numbers are in AP and their sum is 27", writing them as a, a + d, a + 2d
gives a sum of 3a + 3d — two unknowns in one equation. Shifting the labels so that the middle term is
the unknown makes the d terms cancel:

<div class="formula">Three terms in AP: a − d, a, a + d &nbsp;→&nbsp; sum = 3a</div>
<div class="formula">Four terms in AP: a − 3d, a − d, a + d, a + 3d &nbsp;→&nbsp; sum = 4a</div>

In the four-term form the common difference is **2d**, not d. That is the price of the symmetry, and
forgetting it is the standard error: the terms are spaced 2d apart, so if the working gives d = 2 the
progression steps by 4.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — three numbers in AP sum to 27 and their product is 585</div>
  Write them a − d, a, a + d. Sum: 3a = 27 → <strong>a = 9</strong><br />
  Product: (9 − d)(9)(9 + d) = 585 → 9(81 − d²) = 585 → 81 − d² = 65 → d² = 16 → d = ±4<br />
  d = 4 gives <strong>5, 9, 13</strong>; d = −4 gives 13, 9, 5, the same three numbers reversed.<br />
  Check: 5 + 9 + 13 = 27 ✓ and 5 × 9 × 13 = 585 ✓
</div>

The GP versions use division and multiplication in place of subtraction and addition, so that the
**product** of the terms collapses:

<div class="formula">Three terms in GP: a ÷ r, a, ar &nbsp;→&nbsp; product = a³</div>
<div class="formula">Four terms in GP: a ÷ r³, a ÷ r, ar, ar³ &nbsp;→&nbsp; product = a⁴, common ratio r²</div>

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — three numbers in GP have product 1,728 and sum 84</div>
  Write them a/r, a, ar. Product: a³ = 1,728 → <strong>a = 12</strong><br />
  Sum: 12/r + 12 + 12r = 84 → 12/r + 12r = 72 → divide by 12: 1/r + r = 6<br />
  Multiply by r: r² − 6r + 1 = 0 → r = (6 ± √32) ÷ 2 = 3 ± 2√2<br />
  Those are irrational, so re-read the stem: with sum 84 the answer is genuinely irrational. Had the
  sum been 63, then 1/r + r = 17/4, giving 4r² − 17r + 4 = 0, r = 4 or 1/4, and the numbers
  <strong>3, 12, 48</strong>. Check: 3 × 12 × 48 = 1,728 ✓ and 3 + 12 + 48 = 63 ✓
</div>

Choose the symmetrical form when the stem gives you a **sum** (for an AP) or a **product** (for a GP)
of three or four terms. When it gives neither, the plain a, a + d, a + 2d form is simpler.

## 16. The special series sum of n sum of n squared and sum of n cubes

Three closed forms cover the series that are not themselves progressions. Each is a formula for the
sum of the first n whole numbers raised to a power.

<div class="formula">1 + 2 + 3 + … + n = Σn = n(n + 1) ÷ 2</div>
<div class="formula">1² + 2² + 3² + … + n² = Σn² = n(n + 1)(2n + 1) ÷ 6</div>
<div class="formula">1³ + 2³ + 3³ + … + n³ = Σn³ = [n(n + 1) ÷ 2]² = (Σn)²</div>

The first is just an AP with a = 1 and d = 1, so it needs no new memory. The third is the square of the
first, which is the easiest of the three to remember and the easiest to check.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — the three sums for n = 6</div>
  Σn = 6 × 7 ÷ 2 = 21, and 1+2+3+4+5+6 = 21 ✓<br />
  Σn² = 6 × 7 × 13 ÷ 6 = 91, and 1+4+9+16+25+36 = 91 ✓<br />
  Σn³ = 21² = 441, and 1+8+27+64+125+216 = 441 ✓
</div>

Most examined series reduce to these three by splitting the general term into pieces. The rules used
are that a sum splits across plus signs, and that a constant multiplier comes outside:

<div class="formula">Σ(pk² + qk + c) = p·Σk² + q·Σk + c·n, since Σ of a constant c over n terms is cn</div>

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — sum to n terms of 1×4 + 2×7 + 3×10 + …</div>
  The kth term is k × (3k + 1) = 3k² + k — check: k = 1 gives 4, k = 2 gives 14 = 2 × 7 ✓<br />
  Sum = 3Σk² + Σk = 3 × n(n+1)(2n+1)/6 + n(n+1)/2 = n(n+1)(2n+1)/2 + n(n+1)/2<br />
  = [n(n+1) ÷ 2] × [(2n + 1) + 1] = [n(n+1) ÷ 2] × (2n + 2) = <strong>n(n + 1)²</strong><br />
  Check at n = 3: formula gives 3 × 16 = 48, and 4 + 14 + 30 = 48 ✓
</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Σn³ is <strong>(Σn)²</strong>, not (Σn²) and not Σn × Σn². For n = 6 that is 441, while Σn² = 91
    and 91 × 21 = 1,911 — both wrong values are commonly offered. And Σn² is <em>not</em> the square
    of Σn: squaring 21 gives 441, which is the cube sum, not the square sum.
  </p>
</Callout>

A last habit for this section: after deriving a closed form in n, **test it at n = 1 and n = 2**
against the terms you can see. A formula that fails at n = 1 is wrong, and the test costs ten seconds.

## 17. Business applications of progressions

Progressions describe two of the commonest patterns in business data: a quantity that changes by a
**fixed amount** each period, and a quantity that changes by a **fixed percentage** each period. The
first is an AP, the second a GP. Deciding which is which is most of the work.

<div class="formula">"rises by ₹ 400 a year" → AP, d = 400 &nbsp;·&nbsp; "rises by 8% a year" → GP, r = 1.08</div>

**Production and capacity build-up.** A line that makes a fixed number more each month is an AP. The
output in month n is a + (n − 1)d and the cumulative output over n months is the AP sum. Questions ask
for either, and sometimes for the month in which cumulative output first crosses a target — that is
the counting problem of §6.

**Salary increments.** A fixed annual increment of ₹ 15,000 on a starting salary of ₹ 4,80,000 makes
the annual salaries an AP. A fixed **percentage** increment makes them a GP. Both are asked, often in
the same question, so that the two answers can sit side by side as options.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — two increment schemes over 5 years</div>
  Start ₹ 4,80,000. Scheme A: flat ₹ 30,000 a year. Scheme B: 5% of the previous year's salary.<br />
  Scheme A, year 5: 4,80,000 + 4 × 30,000 = <strong>₹ 6,00,000</strong><br />
  Scheme A, five-year total: (5 ÷ 2) × (4,80,000 + 6,00,000) = 2.5 × 10,80,000 = <strong>₹ 27,00,000</strong><br />
  Scheme B, year 5: 4,80,000 × 1.05⁴ = 4,80,000 × 1.21550625 = <strong>₹ 5,83,443</strong> (nearest rupee)<br />
  Scheme B is behind in year 5, because 5% of a salary near ₹ 5,00,000 is about ₹ 25,000, less than the
  flat ₹ 30,000.
</div>

**Straight-line quantities.** A machine whose book value falls by a fixed rupee amount each year has
book values in AP with a negative d. (A machine written down by a fixed *percentage* each year has
book values in GP — the arithmetic is the same GP arithmetic taught here, and the finance chapter
takes such compounding applications further.)

**Seating rows, stacked stock and tiered layouts.** A hall whose first row has 18 seats and each later
row 3 more is an AP: the nth row has 18 + 3(n − 1) seats and the hall's capacity to n rows is the AP
sum. The same shape counts logs stacked in a tapering pile, bricks in a stepped wall, or displays in a
tiered rack.

**Population, subscribers and unit sales growing by a fixed ratio.** A customer base rising by a fixed
percentage each year is a GP. The base in year n is a·rⁿ⁻¹ and the total over n years is the GP sum.
A base *falling* by a fixed percentage has r &lt; 1, and if the question asks for the total of an
endless decline, the infinite sum a ÷ (1 − r) applies.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — a subscriber base growing 25% a year</div>
  Year 1: 8,000 subscribers, r = 1.25<br />
  Year 4 base = 8,000 × 1.25³ = 8,000 × 1.953125 = <strong>15,625</strong><br />
  Four-year total = 8,000 × (1.25⁴ − 1) ÷ 0.25 = 8,000 × (2.44140625 − 1) ÷ 0.25 = 8,000 × 5.765625 =
  <strong>46,125</strong><br />
  Check by listing: 8,000 + 10,000 + 12,500 + 15,625 = 46,125 ✓
</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    "Increases by 20% <strong>of the first year's figure</strong>" is an AP with d = 0.2a, while
    "increases by 20% <strong>a year</strong>" is a GP with r = 1.2. The two phrasings differ by four
    words and give different answers from the third year onwards. Read the base the percentage is
    taken on before you choose a formula.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 18. Negative marking and when to leave a question

Paper 3 is entirely objective and carries **negative marking of 0.25 mark for each wrong answer**. A
correct answer earns 1 mark, a wrong one costs 0.25, and an unattempted one scores 0.

The arithmetic of that is calm and worth knowing. A pure guess among four options is right one time in
four: on average four such guesses earn 1 mark and lose 3 × 0.25 = 0.75, a net gain of 0.25 marks
across four questions. Blind guessing is therefore slightly positive but close to neutral.

**Elimination changes the picture, and this chapter makes elimination unusually easy.** The distractors
here are built from named errors, and several of them can be discarded on sight: an infinite-GP answer
offered for a ratio bigger than 1, a "sum" that is smaller than the largest term being added, a
geometric mean larger than the arithmetic mean of two positive numbers. Rule out one option and a
guess among three is worth about 0.5 marks per four attempts; rule out two and a guess between the
last two is worth about 1.5.

A sensible working rule: **answer when you can eliminate at least one option, and consider leaving the
question when you cannot narrow the field at all.** Nothing in that rule needs to be applied
anxiously. The marks at stake either way are small, and the time saved by leaving one genuinely opaque
question is usually worth more than the fraction of a mark it carries.

## Fully worked examples

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — a production schedule as an AP (easy)">
  <p>
    A packaging unit at Vapi makes 640 cartons in its first week of operation and raises output by 45
    cartons every week. Find (a) the output in week 20, (b) the total output over the first 20 weeks,
    and (c) the first week in which weekly output reaches 1,500 cartons.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — identify the progression</div>
    Output rises by a fixed <em>quantity</em>, so the weekly outputs are an AP.<br />
    a = 640 cartons · d = 45 cartons
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (a) the 20th term</div>
    T₂₀ = a + (20 − 1)d = 640 + 19 × 45 = 640 + 855 = <strong>1,495 cartons</strong><br />
    Nineteen steps, not twenty: week 1 has taken no steps at all.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (b) the sum of 20 terms</div>
    S₂₀ = (20 ÷ 2) × (640 + 1,495) = 10 × 2,135 = <strong>21,350 cartons</strong><br />
    Check with the other form: 10 × [2 × 640 + 19 × 45] = 10 × [1,280 + 855] = 10 × 2,135 ✓<br />
    Check by averaging: the mean week is (640 + 1,495) ÷ 2 = 1,067.5, and 20 × 1,067.5 = 21,350 ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — (c) the first week reaching 1,500</div>
    640 + (n − 1) × 45 ≥ 1,500 → 45(n − 1) ≥ 860 → n − 1 ≥ 19.11 → n ≥ 20.11<br />
    n must be a whole number of weeks, so round <strong>up</strong>: <strong>week 21</strong><br />
    Confirm: T₂₀ = 1,495 (short of 1,500) and T₂₁ = 1,540 (past it) ✓ — rounding down would name
    week 20, whose output is still below the target.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — recovering a and d from two terms (easy-moderate)">
  <p>
    In an AP the 9th term is 74 and the 21st term is 158. Find (a) the common difference, (b) the
    first term, (c) the 30th term, (d) the sum of the first 30 terms, and (e) which term of the
    progression equals 361.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) subtract the two term equations</div>
    T₂₁ − T₉ = (21 − 9)d → 158 − 74 = 12d → 84 = 12d → <strong>d = 7</strong><br />
    Dividing 84 by 21 − 9 = 12, not by 21, is the point: it is the <em>gap</em> in positions that
    multiplies d.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) back-substitute for a</div>
    T₉ = a + 8d → 74 = a + 56 → <strong>a = 18</strong><br />
    Check in the other equation: 18 + 20 × 7 = 18 + 140 = 158 ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (c) and (d)</div>
    T₃₀ = 18 + 29 × 7 = 18 + 203 = <strong>221</strong><br />
    S₃₀ = (30 ÷ 2) × (18 + 221) = 15 × 239 = <strong>3,585</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — (e) solve for the position</div>
    18 + (n − 1) × 7 = 361 → 7(n − 1) = 343 → n − 1 = 49 → <strong>n = 50</strong><br />
    The "+ 1" matters: 343 ÷ 7 = 49 is the number of steps, and the term sits at position 50.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — counting terms in a range and summing them (moderate)">
  <p>
    (a) How many three-digit numbers are divisible by 13? (b) What is their total? (c) How many terms
    of the AP 9, 16, 23, … lie between 100 and 400?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) find the two endpoints first</div>
    Smallest three-digit multiple of 13: 100 ÷ 13 = 7.69…, so 13 × 8 = 104<br />
    Largest: 999 ÷ 13 = 76.8…, so 13 × 76 = 988<br />
    n = (988 − 104) ÷ 13 + 1 = 884 ÷ 13 + 1 = 68 + 1 = <strong>69 numbers</strong><br />
    Index check: the multiples run from 13 × 8 to 13 × 76, and 76 − 8 + 1 = 69 ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) sum them with the first-and-last form</div>
    S₆₉ = (69 ÷ 2) × (104 + 988) = 34.5 × 1,092 = <strong>37,674</strong><br />
    Alternative: 13 × (8 + 9 + … + 76) = 13 × [(76 × 77 ÷ 2) − (7 × 8 ÷ 2)] = 13 × (2,926 − 28)
    = 13 × 2,898 = 37,674 ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (c) the same method on a given AP</div>
    The AP is 9, 16, 23, … with d = 7, so its terms are 9 + 7(n − 1) = 7n + 2.<br />
    First term above 100: 7n + 2 &gt; 100 → n &gt; 14 → n = 15, giving 107<br />
    Last term below 400: 7n + 2 &lt; 400 → n &lt; 56.85 → n = 56, giving 394<br />
    Count = 56 − 15 + 1 = <strong>42 terms</strong><br />
    Or directly: (394 − 107) ÷ 7 + 1 = 287 ÷ 7 + 1 = 41 + 1 = 42 ✓
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — counting from the end in an AP and in a GP (moderate)">
  <p>
    (a) Find the 8th term from the end of the AP 5, 12, 19, …, 208. (b) Find the 5th term from the end
    of the GP 2, 6, 18, …, 4374. (c) In part (a), what is the sum of the 8th term from the start and
    the 8th term from the end?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) reverse the AP</div>
    a = 5 · d = 7 · l = 208<br />
    8th from the end = l − (8 − 1)d = 208 − 7 × 7 = 208 − 49 = <strong>159</strong><br />
    Seven steps back from the last term, because the last term is the 1st from the end.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (a) confirm by converting the position</div>
    Length: 208 = 5 + (m − 1) × 7 → 203 = 7(m − 1) → m − 1 = 29 → m = 30 terms<br />
    8th from the end sits at 30 − 8 + 1 = 23rd from the front.<br />
    T₂₃ = 5 + 22 × 7 = 5 + 154 = <strong>159</strong> ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (b) reverse the GP by dividing</div>
    a = 2 · r = 3 · l = 4,374<br />
    5th from the end = l ÷ r⁵⁻¹ = 4,374 ÷ 3⁴ = 4,374 ÷ 81 = <strong>54</strong><br />
    Length check: 4,374 = 2 × 3^(m−1) → 3^(m−1) = 2,187 = 3⁷ → m = 8 terms, and the 5th from the end
    is the 8 − 5 + 1 = 4th from the front: T₄ = 2 × 3³ = 54 ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — (c) use the equidistant property</div>
    T₈ from the start = 5 + 7 × 7 = 54, and the 8th from the end is 159.<br />
    54 + 159 = <strong>213</strong>, which is exactly a + l = 5 + 208 = 213 ✓<br />
    The property means the answer was available without computing either term.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — inserting arithmetic means (easy-moderate)">
  <p>
    (a) Insert 5 arithmetic means between 11 and 65, and state their sum. (b) The arithmetic mean of
    two numbers is 37 and one of them is 22. Find the other. (c) How many arithmetic means must be
    inserted between 4 and 76 so that the common difference of the resulting AP is 8?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) count the gaps, not the means</div>
    Inserting 5 means gives an AP of 5 + 2 = 7 terms, so 65 is the 7th term.<br />
    65 = 11 + (7 − 1)d → 54 = 6d → <strong>d = 9</strong><br />
    The means are 20, 29, 38, 47, 56. Full AP: 11, 20, 29, 38, 47, 56, 65 ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (a) the sum of the five means</div>
    20 + 29 + 38 + 47 + 56 = <strong>190</strong><br />
    By formula: n × (a + b) ÷ 2 = 5 × (11 + 65) ÷ 2 = 5 × 38 = 190 ✓<br />
    Note the sum of the whole seven-term AP is 7 × 38 = 266, which is 190 + 11 + 65 — the endpoints
    are not part of the means.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (b) a single arithmetic mean</div>
    (22 + x) ÷ 2 = 37 → 22 + x = 74 → <strong>x = 52</strong><br />
    Subtracting instead of doubling gives 15, an answer whose average with 22 is 18.5, not 37.

  <p>
    (a) The arithmetic mean of two positive numbers is 25 and their geometric mean is 20. Find the two
    numbers. (b) Show that for these numbers A &gt; G, and state when the two means would have been

  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) turn the means into a sum and a product</div>
    A = 25 → a + b = 2 × 25 = <strong>50</strong><br />
    G = 20 → ab = 20² = <strong>400</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (a) build the quadratic and solve it</div>

    Split −50 into −40 and −10 (product 400): x² − 40x − 10x + 400 = 0 → (x − 40)(x − 10) = 0<br />
    <strong>The numbers are 10 and 40.</strong> Check: (10 + 40) ÷ 2 = 25 ✓ and √(10 × 40) = 20 ✓

## Common mistakes and examiner traps

- **Using n where n − 1 belongs.** Tₙ = a + (n − 1)d and Tₙ = arⁿ⁻¹. Writing a + nd or arⁿ shifts
  every answer one term forward, and that shifted value is always one of the four options.
- **Answering a term when the sum was asked, or the reverse.** The stem's wording decides: "in the
  12th month" is a term, "over the first 12 months" is a sum. Both numbers appear in the options.
- **Counting terms without the "+ 1".** (last − first) ÷ d counts the gaps. Terms outnumber gaps by
  one, always. Test any counting method on a three-term list you can write out.
- **Misreading the endpoints of a range.** "Between 100 and 500" normally excludes both ends; "from
  100 to 500" includes them. When the boundary value is itself in the pattern, the two readings differ
  by one or two terms.
- **The nth term from the end taken as l − nd.** The last term is the 1st from the end, so the nth is
  l − (n − 1)d, and in a GP it is l ÷ rⁿ⁻¹, not l ÷ rⁿ.
- **Treating the common ratio as a difference.** In 4, 12, 36 the ratio is 3, not the difference 8.
  Check two consecutive pairs before deciding whether a sequence is an AP or a GP.
- **Using the infinite-GP sum when |r| ≥ 1.** a ÷ (1 − r) is meaningless unless |r| &lt; 1. For a
  growing GP it returns a negative or absurd value that still looks like an answer.
- **Judging convergence by the sign of r.** r = −0.4 converges; r = 1.2 does not. The test is on
  |r|, the size, not the sign.
- **Forgetting that r = 1 breaks the sum formula.** With r = 1 the denominator is zero and the sum is
  simply na, not an application of the formula.
- **Using the arithmetic mean where the geometric mean was required.** The GM of 4 and 25 is
  √100 = 10, not 14.5. "Mean proportional", "in continued proportion" and "in GP" all mean the
  geometric mean.
- **Claiming G &gt; A for two positive numbers.** AM ≥ GM always, with equality only when the two
  numbers are equal. A computed GM above the AM signals a slip in the working.
- **Dividing by n instead of n + 1 when inserting means.** Inserting n means creates n + 1 gaps, so
  d = (b − a) ÷ (n + 1) and r = (b ÷ a)^[1 ÷ (n + 1)]. Counting the endpoints among the means is the
  same error in another guise.
- **Forgetting that the four-term symmetrical AP steps by 2d.** In a − 3d, a − d, a + d, a + 3d the
  common difference is 2d. Reporting d as the common difference halves the spacing.
- **Confusing Σn³ with (Σn²) or with Σn × Σn².** Σn³ = (Σn)². At n = 6 that is 441, while Σn² = 91.
- **Rounding the wrong way on a "when does it first exceed" question.** n counts periods and must be
  a whole number, so round **up**. Rounding down names the last period *before* the target is met.
- **Mixing a fixed amount with a fixed percentage.** "Rises by ₹ 400 a year" is an AP; "rises by 8% a
  year" is a GP; "rises by 20% of the first year's figure" is an AP again. The three phrasings agree
  in year 2 and separate afterwards.
- **Applying an AP sum to a GP, or the reverse.** A quick test: in a GP the ratio of successive terms
  is constant, so dividing two adjacent terms should give the same number twice.
- **Answering with r or d when the question asked for a term.** After finding the common ratio, one
  more line of arithmetic is usually still needed.

## One-page revision summary

**Symbols used throughout.** a = first term · d = common difference of an AP · r = common ratio of a
GP · n = number of terms · Tₙ = the nth term · l = the last term of the n terms considered · Sₙ = the
sum of the first n terms · S∞ = the sum of an infinite GP · A = arithmetic mean · G = geometric mean.

**Sequence and series.** A sequence is an ordered list of terms; a series is the sum of a sequence's
terms. A progression is a sequence built by a fixed rule: add d each time (AP) or multiply by r each
time (GP). Finite sequences have a last term; infinite ones do not.

**Arithmetic progression.**

<div class="formula">Tₙ = a + (n − 1)d &nbsp;·&nbsp; d = Tₙ − Tₙ₋₁ &nbsp;·&nbsp; T_q − Tₚ = (q − p)d</div>
<div class="formula">Sₙ = (n ÷ 2)(a + l) &nbsp;·&nbsp; Sₙ = (n ÷ 2)[2a + (n − 1)d] &nbsp;·&nbsp; Tₙ = Sₙ − Sₙ₋₁</div>

**Counting and positions in an AP.** Number of terms = (last − first) ÷ d + 1. The nth term from the
end = l − (n − 1)d, and it sits at position m − n + 1 from the front in an AP of m terms. Terms
equidistant from the two ends have the constant sum a + l; with an odd number of terms the middle term
is (a + l) ÷ 2 and Sₙ = n × (middle term).

**Arithmetic mean.** A = (a + b) ÷ 2, and three numbers are in AP exactly when the middle is the
average of the other two. Inserting n arithmetic means between a and b gives an AP of n + 2 terms with
d = (b − a) ÷ (n + 1); the n means sum to n(a + b) ÷ 2.

**Geometric progression.**

<div class="formula">Tₙ = a·r ⁿ⁻¹ &nbsp;·&nbsp; r = Tₙ ÷ Tₙ₋₁ &nbsp;·&nbsp; T_q ÷ Tₚ = r ^(q − p)</div>
<div class="formula">Sₙ = a(1 − rⁿ) ÷ (1 − r) for r &lt; 1 &nbsp;·&nbsp; Sₙ = a(rⁿ − 1) ÷ (r − 1) for r &gt; 1</div>
<div class="formula">Sₙ = na when r = 1 &nbsp;·&nbsp; Sₙ = (l·r − a) ÷ (r − 1) when the last term l is known</div>

**Infinite GP.** S∞ = a ÷ (1 − r), and it exists **only when |r| &lt; 1**. For |r| ≥ 1 no sum to
infinity exists: r = 1 gives partial sums na, r = −1 gives partial sums oscillating between a and 0,
and |r| &gt; 1 gives partial sums growing without limit. A recurring decimal is the standard
application: 0.4̄ = (4/10) ÷ (9/10) = 4/9.

**Positions in a GP.** The nth term from the end = l ÷ rⁿ⁻¹, at position m − n + 1 from the front in a
GP of m terms.

**Geometric mean.** G = √(ab) for positive a and b, and three numbers are in GP exactly when the
square of the middle equals the product of the outer two. Inserting n geometric means between a and b
gives a GP of n + 2 terms with r = (b ÷ a)^[1 ÷ (n + 1)]; the n means multiply to (ab)^(n/2).

**AM and GM together.** For positive a and b:

<div class="formula">A ≥ G, with A = G only when a = b &nbsp;·&nbsp; a + b = 2A &nbsp;·&nbsp; ab = G²</div>
<div class="formula">(a − b)² = 4(A² − G²), and a, b are the roots of x² − 2Ax + G² = 0</div>

**Symmetrical forms.** Three in AP: a − d, a, a + d (sum 3a). Four in AP: a − 3d, a − d, a + d,
a + 3d (sum 4a, common difference **2d**). Three in GP: a/r, a, ar (product a³). Four in GP: a/r³,
a/r, ar, ar³ (product a⁴, common ratio **r²**).

**Special series.**

<div class="formula">Σn = n(n + 1) ÷ 2 &nbsp;·&nbsp; Σn² = n(n + 1)(2n + 1) ÷ 6 &nbsp;·&nbsp; Σn³ = [n(n + 1) ÷ 2]² = (Σn)²</div>
<div class="formula">Σ(pk² + qk + c) = p·Σk² + q·Σk + cn — split the general term, then apply the three forms</div>

Test any closed form you derive at n = 1 and n = 2 against the visible terms before using it.

**Applications.** A fixed rupee change per period is an AP; a fixed percentage change per period is a
GP with r = 1 ± p/100. Production schedules, salary increments with a flat rise, straight-line
reductions in book value and seating rows are APs. Population, subscribers, unit sales and any
compounding quantity are GPs — the Mathematics of Finance chapter applies the same GP machinery to
interest and instalment problems.

**Marking.** 1 mark correct, −0.25 wrong, 0 unattempted. Blind guessing among four is close to
neutral; eliminating even one option makes an attempt clearly worthwhile, and in this chapter an
option can often be eliminated on sight.
