At a wedding in Hubli, a guest points across the hall and says, "That man dancing is the only son of
my grandmother's only child." The person next to her frowns, works it through, and then smiles: the
dancer is the guest's own brother, or the guest himself if the guest is a man. One sentence, one
small chain of relatives, one answer — that is the whole of this chapter.

Paper 3 asks the same move in three costumes. Sometimes the relatives are named directly ("A is the
brother of B, B is the daughter of C"). Sometimes a person points at a photograph and describes the
face in it. Sometimes the relationship is hidden behind a symbol, so that "P + Q" means "P is the
father of Q". The information is small and the arithmetic is almost nothing. Marks are lost by
assuming a name tells you the gender, by counting a generation twice, and by reading "in-law"
relationships as if they were blood ones.

The reliable method never changes. Turn the words into a small picture — a family tree — and read the
answer off the picture instead of holding five relatives in your head at once. This chapter builds
that picture step by step.

## 1. The vocabulary of relationships

Every puzzle is built from a short list of relationship words. Get them exact and half the work is
done before you draw anything.

**The direct line (your own blood).** These pass straight up and down the generations:

- **Parents and children** — father, mother, son, daughter.
- **Grandparents and grandchildren** — grandfather, grandmother, grandson, granddaughter. Two steps
  up or down.
- **Great-grandparents and great-grandchildren** — three steps.
- **Siblings** — brother and sister, who share the same parents.

**Paternal versus maternal.** A relative reached through your **father** is *paternal*; a relative
reached through your **mother** is *maternal*. The difference is tested often:

- **Paternal grandfather** — your father's father. **Maternal grandfather** — your mother's father.
- **Paternal uncle** — your father's brother. **Maternal uncle** — your mother's brother. In everyday
  Indian usage the maternal uncle is "mama".
- **Paternal aunt** — your father's sister (bua). **Maternal aunt** — your mother's sister (mausi).

**Uncles, aunts, nephews, nieces and cousins.**

- **Uncle** — a brother of either parent, *or* the husband of a parent's sister.
- **Aunt** — a sister of either parent, *or* the wife of a parent's brother.
- **Nephew** — the son of your brother or sister. **Niece** — the daughter of your brother or sister.
- **Cousin** — the child of your uncle or aunt. In English "cousin" carries **no gender**, which the
  examiner uses to set traps.

**Relations by marriage (in-laws).** These are joined to you by a wedding, not by blood, and mixing
the two is the single most common error in the chapter.

- **Husband and wife** — the married couple themselves.
- **Father-in-law, mother-in-law** — the parents of your spouse.
- **Son-in-law** — your daughter's husband. **Daughter-in-law** — your son's wife.
- **Brother-in-law** — your spouse's brother, or your sister's husband. **Sister-in-law** — your
  spouse's sister, or your brother's wife.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A relationship word tells you three things at once: the <strong>direction</strong> (up, down or
    across a generation), the <strong>gender</strong> of the person named, and whether the link is by
    <strong>blood or by marriage</strong>. When a question feels tangled, write those three tags
    beside each relative. "Daughter-in-law" is: same generation as your child, female, joined by
    marriage — and that reading alone answers most in-law questions.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    "Brother-in-law" and "sister-in-law" each cover <strong>two different people</strong>. Your
    sister-in-law may be your wife's sister or your brother's wife, and the two sit in different
    places on the tree. When a puzzle uses the word, keep both readings alive until another clue rules
    one out. Collapsing it to a single meaning too early is how a clean chain reaches the wrong final
    answer.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. Drawing a family tree

The picture is worth more than any mnemonic. Use four symbols and nothing else.

<div class="formula">□ a male &nbsp;·&nbsp; ○ a female &nbsp;·&nbsp; = a marriage (drawn horizontally) &nbsp;·&nbsp; | a parent-to-child link (drawn vertically)</div>

Place people of the **same generation on the same horizontal level**. Draw a marriage as an equals
sign between the two partners, and draw descent as a vertical line dropping from the couple down to
their children. A single generation gap is one step down the page; grandparents sit two levels above
their grandchildren.

Here is a three-generation family drawn with the symbols. A and B are a married couple; their son C
married D; C and D have a daughter E and a son F.

```
        A (□) = B (○)          ← generation 1 (grandparents)
             |
        C (□) = D (○)          ← generation 2 (parents)
             |
       ┌─────┴─────┐
      E (○)       F (□)        ← generation 3 (children)
```

Reading the tree back into words is now mechanical: E is the granddaughter of A and B; F is E's
brother; D is the daughter-in-law of A and B (joined by marriage, female, one generation down); C is
the son-in-law of nobody shown, because his own parents A and B are above him, not his wife's parents.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Keep <strong>gender out of the letters and inside the symbols</strong>. Write "C (□)" and
    "D (○)", never lean on the letter looking male or female. The examiner chooses letters precisely
    so they carry no hint, and a tree that records gender in a symbol beside each node cannot be
    fooled by the name.
  </p>
</Callout>

A few drawing habits save marks:

- **One fact per stroke.** Add relatives one clue at a time, in the order the sentence gives them.
  Do not try to place the whole family from a single reading.
- **Leave gender blank when it is unknown.** If a clue says "C is the child of D", draw C without a
  box or circle until a later clue fixes the gender. Guessing early is the trap in §6.
- **Mark marriages before descent.** Put the couples together first, then drop the children. A child
  hanging off the wrong parent is the commonest drawing slip.

## 3. Reading a pointing or photograph puzzle

The classic Paper 3 line is "Pointing to a photograph / a person, X said, …". The speaker describes
someone **else** through a chain of relatives, and you must name how that someone is related to the
speaker. Two disciplines make these reliable.

**Read the sentence from the far end inwards.** The description is a chain, and the last-named person
is usually the speaker themselves. Start at the innermost relative and walk outwards.

Take "Pointing to a man, a woman said, *He is the son of the only brother of my mother.*" Work from
the inside:

```
  my mother          → the speaker's mother (○)
  the only brother of my mother   → the speaker's maternal uncle (□)
  the son of that brother         → the maternal uncle's son
```

The son of your mother's brother is your **maternal cousin**. So the man is the woman's cousin.
Because the puzzle names him "the son", the man is specifically her cousin brother, but the plain
answer "cousin" is what the option list will carry.

**The speaker is a person too.** When the description ends at the speaker ("…is my father", "…is
me"), place the speaker on the tree like any other relative. A very common short form is a *photograph
of the speaker's own relative*, where the last link folds back onto the speaker.

<div class="formula">"The father of that boy is the only son of my grandfather." → the boy's father is the speaker's father (or the speaker, if male) → the boy is the speaker's son or the speaker's brother's son</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Do not attach the relationship to the wrong end of the chain. In "the son of the only brother of
    my mother", the answer is how <strong>the son</strong> relates to the speaker, not how the
    brother or the mother does. Underline the very first noun of the sentence — that is the person you
    must finally place — and keep everything else as the route to reach them.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Coded relations shown by symbols

Some questions replace the relationship words with arithmetic symbols and give you a key. A typical
key reads:

<div class="formula">P + Q means P is the father of Q &nbsp;·&nbsp; P − Q means P is the wife of Q &nbsp;·&nbsp; P × Q means P is the brother of Q &nbsp;·&nbsp; P ÷ Q means P is the mother of Q</div>

The symbols carry **no arithmetic meaning at all** — "+" is not addition and does not "outrank" "×".
Each symbol is only a shorthand for a relationship word, and the key is different in every question,
so you must read the key freshly each time.

The reliable method is to translate the coded string into a tiny tree, left to right, exactly as the
key dictates.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — with the key above, what does M × N ÷ O mean, and how is M related to O?</div>
  Read the left symbol first: M × N means <strong>M is the brother of N</strong>.<br />
  Read the next symbol: N ÷ O means <strong>N is the mother of O</strong>.<br />
  Draw it: M (□) and N (○) are brother and sister; N is O's mother.<br />
  So M is the brother of O's mother → M is the <strong>maternal uncle of O</strong>.
</div>

Two cautions turn the coding questions from fiddly to routine.

- **The order of the letters is fixed.** "P + Q means P is the father of Q" does **not** also mean Q
  is the father of P. Read every symbol in the direction the key states, left letter first.
- **A symbol fixes gender.** In the key above, "−" means "is the wife of", so the left person in a
  "−" pair is always female. That is often the only gender clue in the whole string, and it settles
  traps that the letters alone cannot.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Never let the familiar look of "+", "−", "×", "÷" tempt you into arithmetic thinking — there is
    no order of operations here. M × N ÷ O is read strictly left to right as two separate relationship
    links, not as "do the ÷ before the ×". Treating the symbols as sums is the built-in trap of every
    coded-relation question.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Counting generations and members

A second family of questions asks you to **count** rather than to name: how many members, how many
are female, how many generations, how many married couples, how many grandchildren. These are the
questions where a computed answer can be checked exactly, so the examiner keeps the family small and
the counting honest.

**Counting generations.** A generation is one step down the family line. Count the *levels* of your
tree, not the people. A grandparent, a parent and a child is **three** generations, however many
people sit on each level.

```
    grandparents        ← level 1
         |
      parents           ← level 2
         |
      children          ← level 3     →  3 generations
```

**Counting members and sub-groups.** Draw the whole tree, then count the nodes for "how many
members", the ○ symbols for "how many female", the = signs for "how many married couples", and the
lowest level for "how many grandchildren". Because the tree records each person once, nobody is
double-counted.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — a couple's children</div>
  "A man has three sons, and each son has exactly one sister. How many children has the man?"<br />
  Draw the three sons on one level: □ □ □.<br />
  "Each son has one sister" — but the sisters of all three sons are the <strong>same</strong> one
  girl, because they share the same parents.<br />
  So the children are three sons and one daughter → <strong>four children</strong>, not six.
</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The counting trap is the <strong>shared sibling</strong>. "Each of the three sons has one sister"
    describes one sister shared among them, not three separate sisters. Reading it as three sisters
    gives six children, and six will be sitting in the option list. Draw the siblings on a single
    level and the sharing is obvious; hold them in your head and it is invisible.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. The gender-assumption trap

The examiner's favourite weapon in this chapter is the **assumption that a name or a role tells you a
gender when it does not.**

- **Letters carry no gender.** In "A is the child of B", A may be a son or a daughter. Draw A with no
  box or circle until a later clue — "A is the mother of C", say — fixes it.
- **Some relationship words carry no gender.** *Cousin*, *child*, *spouse*, *parent*, *sibling* and
  *in-law* are all gender-free. A puzzle that says "the cousin of X" has deliberately withheld
  whether that cousin is male or female.
- **Some words carry gender firmly.** *Son, daughter, father, mother, brother, sister, uncle, aunt,
  nephew, niece, husband, wife* each fix the gender the moment they appear. Use them as your anchors.

The trap works like this: a chain of gender-free links is laid down, and the question then asks
something that would only have one answer if you had assumed a gender. If you did assume, you fall
into the wrong option; if you kept the gender blank, you correctly answer "cannot be determined".

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — when the answer is "cannot be determined"</div>
  "A is the child of B. B is the sister of C. How is A related to C?"<br />
  B is female (sister). C's gender is not given, and neither is A's.<br />
  A is B's child, and B is C's sibling, so A is the <strong>niece or nephew of C</strong> — but which
  one depends on A's gender, which no clue supplies.<br />
  The exact single word (niece <em>or</em> nephew) <strong>cannot be determined</strong>. An option
  that commits to just one is the trap.
</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Before you choose an option, check whether every gender in the chain was actually
    <strong>stated</strong>, not assumed. If the final answer would flip depending on a gender the
    puzzle never gave you, the correct choice is "cannot be determined" — and that phrase is a real
    option in these questions, not a cop-out. Examiners include it precisely to catch the assumer.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. Negative marking and when to leave a question

Paper 3 is entirely objective and carries **negative marking of 0.25 mark for each wrong answer**. A
correct answer earns 1 mark, a wrong one costs 0.25, and an unattempted one scores 0.

Blood-relations questions are unusually kind to elimination. Because the wrong options are built from
named errors — an in-law read as a blood relative, a generation miscounted, a gender assumed — you
can often strike out one or two choices on sight. A "cousin" question whose options include "brother"
and "uncle" has told you those are the traps for the generation-confused; if your tree puts the person
firmly on your own level, both go at once.

The expected-value arithmetic is the same calm sum as elsewhere in the paper. A blind guess among
four options is right one time in four: four such guesses earn on average 1 mark and lose
3 × 0.25 = 0.75, a net of only 0.25 marks. Eliminate one option and a guess among the remaining three
is worth about 0.5 marks per four attempts; eliminate two and the last guess is worth about 1.5.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A sensible working rule for this chapter: <strong>draw the tree, and answer whenever the tree
    lets you strike out even one option.</strong> A fully drawn tree usually settles the question
    outright, so genuine guesses are rare. Leave a question only when the clues are so incomplete
    that you cannot place the key person at all — and even then, check first whether "cannot be
    determined" is itself the intended answer.
  </p>
</Callout>

## Fully worked examples

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — a pointing puzzle read from the inside out (easy)">
  <p>
    Pointing to a lady in a photograph, Mr. Rao said, "Her mother's only son is my father." How is
    the lady related to Mr. Rao?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — start at the innermost relative</div>
    "Her mother" — the lady's mother.<br />
    "Her mother's only son" — the lady's mother's only son, i.e. the lady's only brother.<br />
    That son "is my father" — so the lady's only brother is Mr. Rao's father.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — place Mr. Rao</div>
    Mr. Rao's father is the lady's brother. So the lady is the sister of Mr. Rao's father →
    the lady is Mr. Rao's <strong>paternal aunt</strong> (his father's sister, "bua").<br />
    Check: the lady and Mr. Rao's father share a mother, so they are siblings; Mr. Rao is one
    generation below them, which is exactly the aunt–nephew gap. ✓
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — separating blood from marriage (easy-moderate)">
  <p>
    In a family, Kavya is the wife of Deepak. Deepak is the son of Girish. Girish has one more child,
    a daughter Meera. How is Meera related to Kavya, and how is Kavya related to Girish?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — draw the tree</div>
    Girish (□) is the parent of Deepak (□) and Meera (○), who are therefore brother and sister.<br />
    Kavya (○) = Deepak (□) are married.<br />
    <pre>        Girish (□)
             |
      ┌──────┴──────┐
   Deepak (□)=Kavya(○)   Meera (○)</pre>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — read the two relationships</div>
    Meera is Deepak's sister; Kavya is Deepak's wife. So Meera is Kavya's
    <strong>sister-in-law</strong> (spouse's sister), a relation by marriage, not blood.<br />
    Kavya married into Girish's family through his son, so Kavya is Girish's
    <strong>daughter-in-law</strong>.<br />
    The trap answer is to call Meera Kavya's "sister"; the link is by marriage, so it is
    sister-in-law.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — a coded relation string (moderate)">
  <p>
    In a certain code, "P + Q" means P is the mother of Q; "P − Q" means P is the brother of Q;
    "P × Q" means P is the father of Q; and "P ÷ Q" means P is the sister of Q. Which of the following
    means "A is the maternal uncle of B"?
    Options: (i) A − C + B, (ii) A + C − B, (iii) A × C ÷ B, (iv) A ÷ C + B.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — what "maternal uncle" needs</div>
    A is the maternal uncle of B means: A is the <strong>brother</strong> of B's
    <strong>mother</strong>. So we need "A is the brother of C" and "C is the mother of B", with C the
    linking person.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — test the options against the key</div>
    "A is the brother of C" is A − C (− means brother of). "C is the mother of B" is C + B (+ means
    mother of). Put together: <strong>A − C + B</strong>, which is option (i).<br />
    Check the others: A + C − B makes A the mother of C (wrong, A must be male and a brother); A × C
    makes A the father of C, not the uncle of B. Only (i) builds the exact chain.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — counting the children (moderate)">
  <p>
    A woman introduces the people at a table: "I have as many brothers as sisters, but each of my
    brothers has twice as many sisters as brothers." How many brothers and sisters are there in the
    woman's family, counting the woman herself?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — set up from the woman's view</div>
    Let the family have b brothers and g girls (sisters), the woman being one of the girls.<br />
    "I have as many brothers as sisters" — the woman's brothers number b, and her sisters number
    g − 1 (everyone except herself). So b = g − 1.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — use a brother's view</div>
    A brother's sisters number g, and his brothers number b − 1.<br />
    "Each brother has twice as many sisters as brothers": g = 2(b − 1).<br />
    Substitute b = g − 1: g = 2(g − 2) = 2g − 4 → g = 4, so b = 3.<br />
    <strong>The family has 3 brothers and 4 sisters</strong> — seven children in all. Check: a sister
    sees 3 brothers and 3 sisters (equal ✓); a brother sees 4 sisters and 2 brothers (twice ✓).
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — three generations on one tree (moderate)">
  <p>
    T is the father of U. U is the daughter of V. V is the mother of W. W is the brother of U. X is
    the son of U. Using the family tree, state (a) how X is related to T, and (b) how many generations
    the family spans.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — build the tree one clue at a time</div>
    T (□) and V (○) are the parents of U (○, "daughter of V") and W (□, "brother of U").<br />
    X (□) is the son of U.<br />
    <pre>        T (□) = V (○)          ← generation 1
                |
        ┌───────┴───────┐
       U (○)           W (□)      ← generation 2
        |
       X (□)                       ← generation 3</pre>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — read the answers off the picture</div>
    (a) X is the son of U, and U is the daughter of T, so X is the child of T's daughter → X is the
    <strong>grandson of T</strong>.<br />
    (b) The levels are T/V, then U/W, then X — <strong>three generations</strong>.<br />
    The trap in (a) is to call X the "son" of T by skipping a level; the vertical distance on the tree
    is two steps, which is grandson, not son.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 6 — the gender-assumption trap (moderate-hard)">
  <p>
    Read this exchange. "How is the child on your lap related to you?" a neighbour asks. The reply:
    "The child's father is the only son of my father." From the reply alone, can the speaker's own
    gender and the exact relationship be fixed?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — resolve the chain</div>
    "The only son of my father" is the speaker's father's only son — that is the speaker's brother, or
    the speaker himself if the speaker is male.<br />
    That person "is the child's father".
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — test both genders of the speaker</div>
    If the speaker is <strong>male</strong>: he is his father's only son, so the child's father is the
    speaker himself → the child is the speaker's <strong>own child</strong>.<br />
    If the speaker is <strong>female</strong>: her father's only son is her brother, so the child's
    father is her brother → the child is her <strong>nephew or niece</strong>.<br />
    The relationship therefore <strong>cannot be determined</strong> from the reply alone, because the
    speaker's gender was never stated. Assuming the speaker is male (or female) is exactly the trap.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 7 — counting members of a joint family (moderate)">
  <p>
    A joint family sits for a photograph: a grandfather and grandmother; their two sons; each son's
    wife; and two children of the elder son and one child of the younger son. How many members are in
    the photograph, and how many of them are married couples?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — draw and count the nodes</div>
    Grandfather (□) = Grandmother (○) — 2 people.<br />
    Two sons (□ □) and their two wives (○ ○) — 4 people.<br />
    Elder son's two children and younger son's one child — 3 people.<br />
    Total nodes: 2 + 4 + 3 = <strong>9 members</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — count the marriages</div>
    Marriages (= signs): grandfather–grandmother, elder son–his wife, younger son–his wife.<br />
    That is <strong>three married couples</strong>. The children are unmarried, so they add no "=".<br />
    Counting the grandchildren as if they formed couples, or forgetting one son's wife, are the two
    ways this count goes wrong.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 8 — a longer chain with an in-law twist (hard)">
  <p>
    A is the brother of B. C is the mother of A. D is the sister of C. E is the mother of D. F is the
    son of B. How is F related to E?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — lay the generations out</div>
    E (○) is the mother of D and, since D is the sister of C, of C as well — so E is C's mother.<br />
    C (○) is the mother of A and B (A and B are brother and sibling).<br />
    F (□) is the son of B.<br />
    <pre>            E (○)                 ← generation 1
              |
      ┌───────┴───────┐
     C (○)           D (○)          ← generation 2
      |
   ┌──┴──┐
  A(□)  B              ← generation 3
         |
        F (□)          ← generation 4</pre>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — count the steps from F up to E</div>
    F → B (parent) → C (grandparent) → E (great-grandparent). E is four levels up on the direct
    line.<br />
    So F is the <strong>great-grandson of E</strong>. The distractors "grandson" and "son" come from
    dropping a generation; the tree makes the three upward steps impossible to miscount.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 9 — a coded string with a gender clue (hard)">
  <p>
    In a code, "P × Q" means P is the sister of Q; "P ÷ Q" means P is the husband of Q; "P + Q" means
    P is the son of Q; "P − Q" means P is the daughter of Q. If "K ÷ L × M − N", how is K related to
    N, and what is N's gender?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — read each symbol left to right</div>
    K ÷ L: K is the husband of L → K is male, L is female.<br />
    L × M: L is the sister of M → L and M are siblings.<br />
    M − N: M is the daughter of N → M is female; N is M's parent.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — assemble and read off the answer</div>
    N is the parent of M, and M's sister is L, so N is also L's parent. K is married to L, so K is the
    son-in-law of N. Thus <strong>K is the son-in-law of N</strong>.<br />
    N's gender: nothing in the string states it — "M is the daughter of N" fixes M, not N — so
    <strong>N's gender cannot be determined</strong>. The "÷" symbol fixed K as male; no symbol ever
    fixed N.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes and examiner traps

- **Reading an in-law as a blood relative.** "Sister-in-law" is not "sister"; "daughter-in-law" is
  not "daughter". The link is a marriage, drawn as "=", not a descent line.
- **The two-sided in-law words.** "Brother-in-law" and "sister-in-law" each name two different
  people. Keep both readings until a clue rules one out.
- **Assuming a letter's gender.** A, B, C carry no gender. Leave a node blank until a gendered word
  (son, mother, uncle…) fixes it.
- **Assuming a gender-free word's gender.** *Cousin, child, spouse, sibling, in-law* are gender-free.
  If the answer would change with the gender, the answer is "cannot be determined".
- **Counting a generation twice, or skipping one.** A grandson is two steps down, not one. Count the
  levels on the tree, never guess the distance.
- **The shared-sibling miscount.** "Each of the three brothers has one sister" is one sister, not
  three. Draw siblings on a single level.
- **Attaching the answer to the wrong end of the chain.** In "the son of the brother of my mother",
  you are asked about the son, not the brother. Underline the first noun of the sentence.
- **Treating coded symbols as arithmetic.** "+", "−", "×", "÷" are relationship shorthand with no
  order of operations. Read strictly left to right, and re-read the key for every new question.
- **Reversing the direction of a coded pair.** "P + Q means P is the father of Q" never also means Q
  is the father of P. The left letter comes first.
- **Ignoring "cannot be determined".** It is a genuine answer, included to catch the student who
  assumed a gender the puzzle withheld.

## One-page revision summary

**Vocabulary.** Paternal = through the father; maternal = through the mother. Uncle/aunt = a parent's
sibling or that sibling's spouse. Nephew/niece = a sibling's son/daughter. Cousin = an uncle's or
aunt's child, and it is gender-free. In-laws (father-/mother-/son-/daughter-/brother-/sister-in-law)
are joined by marriage, not blood.

**The tree.** □ male, ○ female, = marriage, | descent. Same generation on the same level; mark
couples first, then drop children; leave gender blank until a clue fixes it.

**Pointing and photograph puzzles.** Read the chain from the innermost relative outwards. The last
link often folds back onto the speaker. Answer for the first noun of the sentence, not the middle of
the chain.

**Coded relations.** Each symbol is a relationship word set by the question's own key — no arithmetic
meaning, no order of operations. Read left to right, left letter first; a symbol that means
"husband/wife of" or "son/daughter of" also fixes a gender.

**Counting.** Generations = the number of levels on the tree. Members = the nodes; female = the ○
symbols; couples = the = signs; grandchildren = the lowest level. Watch the shared sibling: "each son
has one sister" is one girl, shared.

**The gender trap.** Gender-free words are *cousin, child, spouse, sibling, parent, in-law*. If the
single-word answer would change with a gender that the puzzle never stated, choose "cannot be
determined".

**Marking.** 1 mark correct, −0.25 wrong, 0 unattempted. Draw the tree; it usually settles the
question, so real guessing is rare. Eliminating even one option makes an attempt worthwhile.
