A printing press in Coimbatore quotes two ways of doing the same job. Plan A charges a fixed setting
charge of ₹ 18,000 plus ₹ 6 per printed copy. Plan B charges nothing to set up but ₹ 15 per copy.
The owner's question is short: **at what order size do the two plans cost the same?**

Write the two costs as expressions, put an equals sign between them, and the question turns into
`18,000 + 6x = 15x`. Nine words of business become one line of algebra, and the line has exactly one
answer: 2,000 copies. Below 2,000 copies Plan B is cheaper, above it Plan A is. The whole of this
chapter is that move — translate, solve, and read the answer back into the business.

Paper 3 asks for the second step almost every time. You will be given an equation and asked for its
roots, or given a pair of equations and asked for x + y, or told a quadratic's roots and asked to
build the equation back up. The arithmetic is small. The marks are lost on sign slips, on forgetting
the second root, and on answering a different question from the one asked.

## 1. What an equation is and how an identity differs

An **equation** is a statement that two expressions are equal, and it is true only for particular
values of the letter in it. Those values are the **roots** or **solutions**.

<div class="formula">3x − 7 = 11 is true only when x = 6. That single value is the root.</div>

An **identity** looks the same on the page but is true for **every** value of the letter:

<div class="formula">(x + 4)² = x² + 8x + 16 is true for x = 0, x = −3, x = 1,00,000 — for every x</div>

Test it at two convenient values. Put x = 0 into the identity: both sides give 16. Put x = 1: both
sides give 25. An identity never fails. Put x = 0 into `3x − 7 = 11` and the left side gives −7,
which is not 11 — so the statement is an equation, and only the value that repairs it counts.

The practical difference is what happens when you "solve" one. Solve an equation and the letters
cancel down to a value. Try to solve an identity and everything cancels, leaving a true statement
with no letters at all, such as 0 = 0. That is the signal to stop and say "identity — every value
works", not "no solution".

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Examiners test this with a one-liner: "Which of the following is an identity?" The reliable
    method is to substitute <strong>two different easy values</strong>, usually 0 and 1. If both
    sides agree twice, the statement is almost certainly an identity; if they disagree even once, it
    is an equation at best and false at worst.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. Degree and roots: the vocabulary

Six words carry the whole chapter.

- **Variable** — the letter whose value is unknown: x, y, z.
- **Coefficient** — the number multiplying a variable. In 7x² the coefficient of x² is 7.
- **Constant term** — the number standing alone, with no variable attached.
- **Degree** — the highest power of the variable present, once the equation is arranged with every
  term on one side. `4x + 9 = 0` has degree 1; `2x² − 5x + 3 = 0` has degree 2; `x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6 = 0`
  has degree 3.
- **Root** — a value that satisfies the equation. Substituting it makes the two sides equal.
- **Simultaneous equations** — two or more equations that must hold **at the same time**, for the
  same values of the letters.

Degree decides how many roots to expect. A polynomial equation of degree n has exactly **n roots**,
counted with repetition. So a linear equation has one root, a quadratic has two, a cubic has three.
Names follow the degree: degree 1 is **linear** (or simple), degree 2 is **quadratic**, degree 3 is
**cubic**.

| Equation | Degree | Name | Roots to expect |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5x − 20 = 0 | 1 | linear (simple) | 1 |
| 2x² − 7x + 3 = 0 | 2 | quadratic | 2 |
| x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6 = 0 | 3 | cubic | 3 |
| x⁴ − 13x² + 36 = 0 | 4 | biquadratic | 4 |

A last piece of vocabulary. Two equations that must hold together are **simultaneous**; a system
that has at least one solution is **consistent** and one that has none is **inconsistent**; a system
whose equations carry genuinely different information is **independent**, and one whose second
equation is only the first rescaled is **dependent**. Section 6 turns these four words into a test
you can apply in ten seconds.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    "A quadratic has two roots" does not mean the two roots must be different. When the two roots
    coincide, as in x² − 10x + 25 = 0 where x = 5 twice, the equation still has two roots — they are
    <strong>real and equal</strong>. An option saying "only one root" is the trap; an option saying
    "two equal real roots" is the answer.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. Simple linear equations in one unknown

A linear equation in one unknown can always be pushed into the shape `ax + b = 0` with a ≠ 0, and
then it has exactly one root, x = −b ÷ a.

The method is four steps, always in this order:

1. **Clear the fractions.** Multiply every term on both sides by the LCM of the denominators.
2. **Remove the brackets.** Watch the sign in front of each bracket.
3. **Transpose.** Move every term with the unknown to one side and every number to the other. A term
   changes its sign when it crosses the equals sign.
4. **Divide** by the coefficient of the unknown.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — solve (2x − 3)/4 − (x − 5)/6 = 1/2</div>
  LCM of 4, 6 and 2 is 12. Multiply every term by 12:<br />
  3(2x − 3) − 2(x − 5) = 6<br />
  6x − 9 − 2x + 10 = 6 &nbsp;&nbsp;← the minus in front of the second bracket flips both signs inside<br />
  4x + 1 = 6 → 4x = 5 → <strong>x = 5/4</strong><br />
  Check: (2(1.25) − 3)/4 − (1.25 − 5)/6 = (−0.5)/4 − (−3.75)/6 = −0.125 + 0.625 = 0.5 ✓
</div>

Two shapes deserve their own note.

**A letter that cancels.** If the x terms vanish and what is left is false, such as 7 = 9, the
equation has **no solution**. If what is left is true, such as 9 = 9, it is an identity and every
value works.

**Equations with the unknown in a denominator.** Multiply through, solve, and then **reject any root
that makes an original denominator zero**. Such a root is not a root at all — the equation was never
defined there.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — solve 1/(x − 2) + 1/(x + 2) = 4/(x² − 4)</div>
  Notice first that x² − 4 = (x − 2)(x + 2), so that is the common denominator.<br />
  Multiply every term by (x − 2)(x + 2): (x + 2) + (x − 2) = 4<br />
  2x = 4 → x = 2<br />
  But x = 2 makes the first denominator zero, so the original equation has no meaning there.<br />
  <strong>The equation has no solution.</strong> An option offering "x = 2" is the whole point of the
  question.
</div>

**A word on units.** In a business equation the letter always stands for a number of something —
jars, rupees, days. Write down what it stands for before you solve. Half of the wrong answers in the
applications section are right numbers attached to the wrong thing.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The single most expensive slip in the whole chapter is the sign on transposition. A term that
    moves across the equals sign changes sign; a term that moves across a <em>plus</em> sign inside
    the same side does not. Writing <code>4x = 6 + 1</code> instead of <code>4x = 6 − 1</code> in the
    example above gives x = 7/4, and 7/4 will be sitting there as an option.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Two unknowns: solving by elimination

One equation in two unknowns has endlessly many solutions: `x + y = 10` is satisfied by (1, 9),
(2, 8), (4.5, 5.5) and infinitely more. To pin the pair down you need a **second** equation that the
same pair must satisfy. That is a system of **simultaneous** equations, written in the standard form

<div class="formula">a₁x + b₁y = c₁ &nbsp;&nbsp;and&nbsp;&nbsp; a₂x + b₂y = c₂</div>

**Elimination** removes one unknown by adding or subtracting the equations after scaling them so
that one variable has the same coefficient in both.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — solve 3x + 4y = 26 and 5x − 2y = 26</div>
  Choose to remove y, because its coefficients 4 and −2 are already close.<br />
  (i) 3x + 4y = 26 &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; (ii) × 2 → 10x − 4y = 52<br />
  The y terms are now +4y and −4y, so <strong>add</strong> the two lines:<br />
  13x = 78 → <strong>x = 6</strong><br />
  Put x = 6 back into (i): 18 + 4y = 26 → 4y = 8 → <strong>y = 2</strong><br />
  Check in the equation you did <em>not</em> use for back-substitution: 5(6) − 2(2) = 30 − 4 = 26 ✓
</div>

Two habits are worth building. **Add when the signs of the matched terms are opposite; subtract when
they are the same.** And always check in the *other* equation — checking in the one you used to find
the second unknown cannot catch an error. Fractional answers are perfectly normal; a system is not
wrong because its solution is 59/13.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Pick the unknown that is <strong>cheapest to eliminate</strong>. If one variable already has
    equal or opposite coefficients, use it directly. If one coefficient divides the other exactly,
    scale only one equation. Choosing badly costs no marks, only time.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Two unknowns: substitution and cross-multiplication

**Substitution** suits a system in which one unknown already stands almost alone. Make one variable
the subject of one equation and push that expression into the other.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — solve x + 3y = 17 and 4x − y = 3</div>
  The first equation gives x directly: x = 17 − 3y<br />
  Substitute into the second: 4(17 − 3y) − y = 3<br />
  68 − 12y − y = 3 → −13y = −65 → <strong>y = 5</strong><br />
  Then x = 17 − 3(5) = <strong>x = 2</strong> · Check: 4(2) − 5 = 3 ✓
</div>

**Cross-multiplication** is a formula that solves the standard pair in one stroke. Write both
equations with everything on the left:

<div class="formula">a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0 &nbsp;&nbsp;and&nbsp;&nbsp; a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0</div>

<div class="formula">x ÷ (b₁c₂ − b₂c₁) = y ÷ (c₁a₂ − c₂a₁) = 1 ÷ (a₁b₂ − a₂b₁)</div>

So, provided a₁b₂ − a₂b₁ ≠ 0,

<div class="formula">x = (b₁c₂ − b₂c₁) ÷ (a₁b₂ − a₂b₁) &nbsp;&nbsp;and&nbsp;&nbsp; y = (c₁a₂ − c₂a₁) ÷ (a₁b₂ − a₂b₁)</div>

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — the same pair by cross-multiplication</div>
  x + 3y = 17 becomes x + 3y − 17 = 0, so a₁ = 1, b₁ = 3, c₁ = −17<br />
  4x − y = 3 becomes 4x − y − 3 = 0, so a₂ = 4, b₂ = −1, c₂ = −3<br />
  Denominator: a₁b₂ − a₂b₁ = (1)(−1) − (4)(3) = −1 − 12 = −13<br />
  x numerator: b₁c₂ − b₂c₁ = (3)(−3) − (−1)(−17) = −9 − 17 = −26 → x = −26 ÷ −13 = <strong>2</strong><br />
  y numerator: c₁a₂ − c₂a₁ = (−17)(4) − (−3)(1) = −68 + 3 = −65 → y = −65 ÷ −13 = <strong>5</strong>
</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Cross-multiplication fails for exactly one reason, and it is always the same reason: the
    constants were not moved to the left, so c₁ and c₂ kept the wrong sign. In the example above,
    using c₁ = +17 and c₂ = +3 gives x = +26 ÷ −13 = −2 and y = +5 — a perfectly plausible-looking
    pair with both signs wrong. Write the equations in the "= 0" form on paper before you touch the
    formula.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. Unique solution no solution or infinitely many

Everything about a pair of linear equations in two unknowns is decided by three ratios:

<div class="formula">Compare a₁/a₂ with b₁/b₂ with c₁/c₂ (equations written as a₁x + b₁y = c₁ and a₂x + b₂y = c₂)</div>

| Condition | What it means | Solutions | The lines |
|---|---|---|---|
| a₁/a₂ ≠ b₁/b₂ | different slopes | exactly **one** solution — the system is *consistent and independent* | cross at one point |
| a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ ≠ c₁/c₂ | same slope, different intercept | **no** solution — the system is *inconsistent* | parallel, never meet |
| a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂ | the same equation twice over | **infinitely many** solutions — *consistent and dependent* | one line drawn twice |

The picture behind the table is worth holding on to. Each equation in two unknowns draws a straight
line. Two lines can meet at one point, never meet, or lie on top of one another — there is no fourth
possibility, which is why a pair of linear equations can never have exactly two or exactly three
solutions. An option offering "two solutions" for a linear system is always wrong.

The first condition is the one to memorise, because it is equivalent to `a₁b₂ − a₂b₁ ≠ 0` — the
same quantity that sits in the denominator of the cross-multiplication formula. When that quantity
is zero the formula divides by zero, which is the algebra telling you there is no single answer.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Questions of the shape "for what value of k does the system have no solution?" are pure ratio
    work. Set a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ to find the candidate value of k, then <strong>check the third
    ratio</strong>. If c₁/c₂ also matches, the answer is "infinitely many", not "none". Skipping the
    third check is what makes these questions worth asking.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. Three unknowns

Three unknowns need three independent equations. The method does not change — it only repeats.

1. **Choose one variable to remove.** Take the equations in pairs and eliminate the same variable
   from each pair. Three equations give two new equations in the two surviving unknowns.
2. **Solve that pair** by any method from the last two sections.
3. **Back-substitute** into any original equation to recover the third unknown.
4. **Check in all three** original equations, not just the one you used.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — solve x + y + z = 12, 2x − y + 3z = 22, 3x + 2y − z = 5</div>
  Label them (i), (ii), (iii). Remove y first, because (i) and (ii) already carry +y and −y.<br />
  (i) + (ii): 3x + 4z = 34 &nbsp;&nbsp;… (iv)<br />
  (iii) + 2 × (ii): 3x + 2y − z + 4x − 2y + 6z = 5 + 44 → 7x + 5z = 49 &nbsp;&nbsp;… (v)<br />
  Solve (iv) and (v). Multiply (iv) by 7 and (v) by 3 to match the x terms:<br />
  21x + 28z = 238 and 21x + 15z = 147 → subtract: 13z = 91 → <strong>z = 7</strong><br />
  From (iv): 3x + 28 = 34 → <strong>x = 2</strong> · From (i): y = 12 − 2 − 7 = <strong>y = 3</strong><br />
  Check (ii): 4 − 3 + 21 = 22 ✓ &nbsp; Check (iii): 6 + 6 − 7 = 5 ✓
</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Eliminate the <strong>same</strong> variable from both pairs. Removing y from one pair and z
    from the other leaves two equations in different unknowns and gets you nowhere — it is the most
    common way a three-unknown attempt stalls halfway.
  </p>
</Callout>

Three-unknown systems can also be inconsistent or dependent. If eliminating leaves a false statement
such as 0 = 5, the system has **no solution**. If it leaves 0 = 0, one equation was a combination of
the others and there are **infinitely many** solutions.

## 8. Quadratic equations: solving by factorisation

A **quadratic equation** is any equation of degree two. Standard form:

<div class="formula">ax² + bx + c = 0, where a ≠ 0</div>

If a were 0 the x² term would vanish and the equation would be linear — which is why "a ≠ 0" is part
of the definition and not decoration.

Two shapes solve themselves and are worth spotting before any method is applied.

- **Pure quadratic, ax² + c = 0** — no middle term. Then x² = −c/a, so x = ±√(−c/a). The roots are
  always equal in size and opposite in sign, and they are real only when −c/a is not negative.
  `3x² − 27 = 0` gives x = ±3.
- **No constant term, ax² + bx = 0** — take x out as a factor: x(ax + b) = 0, so **one root is
  always 0** and the other is −b/a. `5x² − 20x = 0` gives x = 0 and x = 4. Dividing both sides by x
  to "simplify" destroys the root x = 0 and is the mistake this shape is set to catch.

**Factorisation** rests on one fact: if a product is zero, at least one factor is zero. Split the
middle coefficient b into two parts whose **sum is b** and whose **product is a × c**.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — solve 6x² − 19x + 10 = 0</div>
  a × c = 6 × 10 = 60; we need two numbers with product 60 and sum −19 → −15 and −4<br />
  6x² − 15x − 4x + 10 = 0<br />
  3x(2x − 5) − 2(2x − 5) = 0<br />
  (2x − 5)(3x − 2) = 0<br />
  So 2x − 5 = 0 or 3x − 2 = 0 → <strong>x = 5/2 or x = 2/3</strong><br />
  Check by the root relations: sum = 5/2 + 2/3 = 19/6 = −b/a ✓ ; product = 5/3 = c/a = 10/6 ✓
</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Two errors live here. The first is splitting b using a product of <strong>c alone</strong>
    instead of <strong>a × c</strong> — that works only when a = 1 and quietly fails otherwise. The
    second is reading the factor (2x − 5) as giving the root −5, or 5, instead of solving
    2x − 5 = 0 properly. Set every factor to zero and solve it; never read a root straight off a
    bracket.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Solving by completing the square

Factorisation only works when the factors are rational. **Completing the square** always works, and
it is where the quadratic formula comes from.

The steps, on ax² + bx + c = 0:

1. Divide throughout by a, so the x² term is bare: x² + (b/a)x + c/a = 0.
2. Move the constant to the right: x² + (b/a)x = −c/a.
3. Add the square of **half the coefficient of x** to both sides: add (b/2a)².
4. The left side is now a perfect square, (x + b/2a)². Take the square root of both sides,
   remembering **both** signs.
5. Isolate x.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — solve 2x² + 8x − 10 = 0 by completing the square</div>
  Divide by 2: x² + 4x − 5 = 0 → x² + 4x = 5<br />
  Half the coefficient of x is 2; its square is 4. Add 4 to both sides:<br />
  x² + 4x + 4 = 9 → (x + 2)² = 9<br />
  x + 2 = ±3 &nbsp;&nbsp;← the ± is the whole point; a square root has two values<br />
  x = −2 + 3 = 1 &nbsp;or&nbsp; x = −2 − 3 = −5 → <strong>roots 1 and −5</strong><br />
  Check: sum = −4 = −b/a = −8/2 ✓ ; product = −5 = c/a = −10/2 ✓
</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Writing <code>x + 2 = 3</code> and stopping loses the second root every time. A square root
    taken during solving carries <strong>±</strong>. The distractor "1 only" is put in the option
    list precisely for the student who forgets it.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. The quadratic formula

Run the completing-the-square steps on the general equation and the result is the formula every
Foundation student needs by heart:

<div class="formula">For ax² + bx + c = 0 (a ≠ 0): x = [ −b ± √(b² − 4ac) ] ÷ 2a</div>

Both roots come out of one line: one uses the plus, the other the minus.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — solve 3x² − 11x + 6 = 0 by formula</div>
  a = 3, b = −11, c = 6<br />
  b² − 4ac = (−11)² − 4(3)(6) = 121 − 72 = 49 → √49 = 7<br />
  x = [ 11 ± 7 ] ÷ 6<br />
  x = 18/6 = <strong>3</strong> &nbsp;or&nbsp; x = 4/6 = <strong>2/3</strong><br />
  Check: sum 3 + 2/3 = 11/3 = −b/a ✓ ; product 3 × 2/3 = 2 = c/a = 6/3 ✓
</div>

Three habits keep the formula honest.

- **Rearrange to "= 0" first.** `2x² = 7x − 3` is not ready; `2x² − 7x + 3 = 0` is.
- **Carry the sign into b.** In 3x² − 11x + 6 the value of b is −11, so −b is +11. Using −(11) here
  gives the roots −3 and −2/3, both wrong in sign.
- **Take the square root before dividing.** √(b² − 4ac) must be evaluated as a whole; you cannot
  root b² and 4ac separately, and √49 is 7, not 24.5.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The classic slip is dividing only the −b by 2a. The whole numerator, <strong>including the
    ± term</strong>, is divided. Writing x = −b/2a ± √(b² − 4ac) turns the roots of
    3x² − 11x + 6 = 0 into 11/6 ± 7, which is neither root.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 11. The discriminant and the nature of the roots

The quantity under the square root sign decides everything about the roots without your having to
find them.

<div class="formula">Discriminant D = b² − 4ac</div>

| D | Nature of the roots | Example |
|---|---|---|
| D &gt; 0 and a perfect square | **real, distinct and rational** — the equation factorises over the rationals | x² − 5x + 6 = 0, D = 1, roots 2 and 3 |
| D &gt; 0 but not a perfect square | **real, distinct and irrational**, occurring as a conjugate pair p ± √q | x² − 4x + 1 = 0, D = 12, roots 2 ± √3 |
| D = 0 | **real and equal** — one value repeated twice, x = −b/2a | x² − 6x + 9 = 0, D = 0, root 3 twice |
| D &lt; 0 | **not real** — no real number satisfies the equation; the roots are a conjugate pair of imaginary numbers | x² + x + 4 = 0, D = −15 |

The "perfect square" row assumes a, b and c are rational, which they are in every Foundation
question. Two consequences worth stating plainly:

- **Irrational roots always come in pairs.** If 2 + √3 is a root of a quadratic with rational
  coefficients, 2 − √3 is the other root. The same is true of imaginary roots: if 3 + 2i is a root,
  3 − 2i is the other.
- **"Real and equal" still counts as two roots.** The equation has two roots that happen to have the
  same value.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    "For what value of k are the roots equal?" is a D = 0 question, nothing more. Set b² − 4ac = 0,
    which is itself usually a quadratic in k, and solve it — so expect <strong>two</strong> answers
    for k unless the question narrows it down. "Roots are real" means D ≥ 0, and the ≥ matters: it
    includes the equal-roots case.
  </p>
</Callout>

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — for kx² − 12x + 9 = 0, find k for equal roots, and the range of k for real distinct roots</div>
  Here a = k, b = −12, c = 9, and k ≠ 0 or the equation stops being quadratic.<br />
  D = (−12)² − 4(k)(9) = 144 − 36k<br />
  <strong>Equal roots:</strong> D = 0 → 144 = 36k → <strong>k = 4</strong><br />
  Check: 4x² − 12x + 9 = (2x − 3)², root 3/2 twice ✓<br />
  <strong>Real and distinct:</strong> D &gt; 0 → 144 − 36k &gt; 0 → <strong>k &lt; 4, and k ≠ 0</strong><br />
  <strong>Not real:</strong> D &lt; 0 → k &gt; 4
</div>

One more relationship is worth carrying. Because the equal-roots case is D = 0, the repeated root is
simply

<div class="formula">When D = 0, both roots equal −b ÷ 2a</div>

For 4x² − 12x + 9 = 0 that is 12 ÷ 8 = 3/2, matching the factorised form above without any further
work.

## 12. Sum and product of the roots

If α and β are the roots of ax² + bx + c = 0, then ax² + bx + c = a(x − α)(x − β). Expand the right
side and match coefficients, and two relations drop out:

<div class="formula">α + β = −b/a &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; αβ = c/a</div>

Read them as: the **sum is minus the middle coefficient over the first**, and the **product is the
last over the first**. They let you answer a large family of questions without solving anything.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — for 5x² − 12x + 4 = 0, find α + β, αβ and 1/α + 1/β</div>
  a = 5, b = −12, c = 4<br />
  α + β = −(−12)/5 = <strong>12/5</strong><br />
  αβ = 4/5<br />
  1/α + 1/β = (α + β) ÷ αβ = (12/5) ÷ (4/5) = <strong>3</strong><br />
  No root was ever computed. (For the record they are 2 and 2/5, and 1/2 + 5/2 = 3 ✓)
</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Two sign traps. First, the sum is <strong>−b/a</strong>, not b/a — for 5x² − 12x + 4 = 0 the sum
    is +12/5, and answering −12/5 is the commonest wrong option in the paper. Second, students swap
    the two: quoting the sum as c/a and the product as −b/a gives 4/5 and −12/5, a pair that looks
    respectable and is completely inverted.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 13. Forming an equation from given roots

Run the relations backwards and you can build the equation from its roots.

<div class="formula">x² − (sum of the roots)x + (product of the roots) = 0</div>

The minus sign in front of the sum is part of the formula. Multiply through by any convenient number
to clear fractions — the roots do not change when every term is scaled.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — form the equation whose roots are 3/4 and −2/5</div>
  Sum = 3/4 − 2/5 = (15 − 8)/20 = 7/20<br />
  Product = (3/4)(−2/5) = −6/20 = −3/10<br />
  x² − (7/20)x − 3/10 = 0<br />
  Multiply throughout by 20: <strong>20x² − 7x − 6 = 0</strong><br />
  Check: D = 49 + 480 = 529 = 23², roots = (7 ± 23)/40 = 30/40 and −16/40 = 3/4 and −2/5 ✓
</div>

The same idea builds an equation whose roots are **derived** from another equation's roots. You do
not need the roots themselves — only their new sum and new product.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — α and β satisfy x² − 6x + 4 = 0; form the equation whose roots are 2α and 2β</div>
  From the given equation: α + β = 6, αβ = 4<br />
  New sum = 2α + 2β = 2(α + β) = 12<br />
  New product = (2α)(2β) = 4αβ = 16<br />
  Equation: x² − 12x + 16 = 0
</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A question that gives you one irrational or imaginary root of a rational quadratic has already
    given you both. If one root is 5 − √2, the other is 5 + √2, so the sum is 10 and the product is
    25 − 2 = 23, and the equation is x² − 10x + 23 = 0. You never have to hunt for the second root.
  </p>
</Callout>

Three shifted-root patterns come up often enough to be worth memorising. If α and β are the roots of
ax² + bx + c = 0, with s = α + β = −b/a and p = αβ = c/a:

| New roots | New sum | New product | Resulting equation |
|---|---|---|---|
| kα and kβ | ks | k²p | ax² + kbx + k²c = 0 |
| α + k and β + k | s + 2k | p + ks + k² | replace x by (x − k) in the original |
| 1/α and 1/β | s/p | 1/p | **cx² + bx + a = 0** — reverse the coefficients |
| −α and −β | −s | p | ax² − bx + c = 0 — flip the sign of b |

The reciprocal row is the neatest result in the chapter: to get the equation whose roots are the
reciprocals of another's, simply **write the coefficients backwards**. From 3x² − 11x + 6 = 0, whose
roots are 3 and 2/3, the reciprocal equation is 6x² − 11x + 3 = 0, whose roots are 1/3 and 3/2 ✓

## 14. Symmetric functions of the roots

A **symmetric function** of α and β is any expression that stays the same when α and β swap places.
Every such expression can be written using only the sum s = α + β and the product p = αβ, which is
why they can be evaluated without solving the equation.

The identities worth knowing:

<div class="formula">α² + β² = s² − 2p</div>
<div class="formula">(α − β)² = s² − 4p, so α − β = ±√(s² − 4p)</div>
<div class="formula">1/α + 1/β = s ÷ p &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; 1/α² + 1/β² = (s² − 2p) ÷ p²</div>
<div class="formula">α³ + β³ = s³ − 3ps</div>
<div class="formula">α/β + β/α = (α² + β²) ÷ αβ = (s² − 2p) ÷ p</div>
<div class="formula">α⁴ + β⁴ = (s² − 2p)² − 2p²</div>

Each one is built the same way: write the expression in terms of α + β and αβ using an algebraic
identity, then substitute. α⁴ + β⁴, for instance, is (α² + β²)² − 2α²β², and α² + β² is already
known to be s² − 2p.

Notice that (α − β)² = s² − 4p is the discriminant divided by a²: for ax² + bx + c = 0,
s² − 4p = b²/a² − 4c/a = (b² − 4ac)/a². So a question asking for the **difference** of the roots is
a discriminant question wearing a different hat.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — for 2x² − 9x + 4 = 0, find α² + β², α³ + β³ and α − β</div>
  s = 9/2, p = 4/2 = 2<br />
  α² + β² = s² − 2p = 81/4 − 4 = <strong>65/4</strong><br />
  α³ + β³ = s³ − 3ps = 729/8 − 3(2)(9/2) = 729/8 − 27 = (729 − 216)/8 = <strong>513/8</strong><br />
  (α − β)² = s² − 4p = 81/4 − 8 = 49/4 → α − β = <strong>±7/2</strong><br />
  (The roots are 4 and 1/2; 16 + 0.25 = 16.25 = 65/4 ✓ and 4 − 0.5 = 3.5 = 7/2 ✓)
</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    α² + β² is <strong>not</strong> (α + β)². The cross term 2αβ must be subtracted. Reporting
    (9/2)² = 81/4 above instead of 65/4 is the error the distractor is built from. The same applies
    to α³ + β³, where the correction term is 3ps and not nothing.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 15. Equations reducible to quadratic form

Many equations that are not quadratic become quadratic after a **substitution**. Four families cover
almost everything Paper 3 asks.

**Family 1 — the biquadratic, ax⁴ + bx² + c = 0.** Put y = x². Solve the quadratic in y, then take
square roots to return to x. Each positive y gives two values of x; a negative y gives none that are
real.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — solve x⁴ − 13x² + 36 = 0</div>
  Let y = x²: y² − 13y + 36 = 0 → (y − 4)(y − 9) = 0 → y = 4 or y = 9<br />
  x² = 4 → x = ±2 &nbsp;&nbsp; x² = 9 → x = ±3<br />
  <strong>Four roots: 2, −2, 3, −3</strong> — as a degree-4 equation must have.
</div>

**Family 2 — the reciprocal pattern, a(x + 1/x)² + b(x + 1/x) + c = 0.** Put y = x + 1/x. Solve for
y, then solve x + 1/x = y, which is the quadratic x² − yx + 1 = 0.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — solve 2(x + 1/x)² − 9(x + 1/x) + 10 = 0</div>
  Let y = x + 1/x: 2y² − 9y + 10 = 0<br />
  Split −9 into −4 and −5 (product 2 × 10 = 20): 2y² − 4y − 5y + 10 = 0 → (y − 2)(2y − 5) = 0<br />
  y = 2 or y = 5/2<br />
  y = 2: x + 1/x = 2 → x² − 2x + 1 = 0 → (x − 1)² = 0 → x = 1, twice<br />
  y = 5/2: x + 1/x = 5/2 → 2x² − 5x + 2 = 0 → (2x − 1)(x − 2) = 0 → x = 1/2, x = 2<br />
  <strong>Roots: 1 (repeated), 1/2 and 2</strong> — four in all, as a degree-4 equation requires.
</div>

**Family 3 — equations with a surd.** Isolate the square root on one side and square both sides.
Squaring can manufacture roots that do not satisfy the original equation, so **every root must be
substituted back into the original equation** and any that fails must be discarded. A root thrown up
by squaring that fails the check is an **extraneous root**.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — solve √(3x + 10) = x</div>
  Square both sides: 3x + 10 = x² → x² − 3x − 10 = 0 → (x − 5)(x + 2) = 0 → x = 5 or x = −2<br />
  Test x = 5 in the original: √25 = 5 ✓ — a genuine root<br />
  Test x = −2: √4 = 2, but the right side is −2, and 2 ≠ −2 ✗ — <strong>extraneous</strong><br />
  The equation has <strong>one</strong> root, x = 5.
</div>

**Family 4 — the same expression appearing twice.** In (x² + 3x)² − 8(x² + 3x) + 12 = 0, put
y = x² + 3x, solve y² − 8y + 12 = 0 to get y = 2 or 6, then solve each of x² + 3x = 2 and
x² + 3x = 6 as a separate quadratic.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Two habits go wrong here. Students solve for the substituted letter and stop, giving y = 4 and
    y = 9 as "the roots" instead of returning to x. And students who do return forget that squaring
    is a one-way street: the check against the <strong>original</strong> equation, not the squared
    one, is what separates a root from an extraneous root.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 16. Cubic equations

A cubic ax³ + bx² + cx + d = 0 has three roots. At Foundation level the questions are built so that
**at least one root is a simple rational number**, and the method is:

1. **Find one root by trial.** Test the divisors of the constant term divided by the divisors of the
   leading coefficient — in practice, try ±1, ±2, ±3 and the other small factors of d ÷ a. When a
   value of x makes the whole expression zero, that value is a root.
2. **Divide out the factor.** If x = r is a root, (x − r) is a factor. Divide the cubic by (x − r)
   to leave a quadratic.
3. **Solve the quadratic** by factorisation or formula. Together with r that gives all three roots.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — solve x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6 = 0</div>
  Constant is −6, so try x = 1: 1 − 6 + 11 − 6 = 0 ✓ — x = 1 is a root, (x − 1) is a factor<br />
  Divide: x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6 = (x − 1)(x² − 5x + 6)<br />
  x² − 5x + 6 = (x − 2)(x − 3) → x = 2 and x = 3<br />
  <strong>Roots: 1, 2 and 3</strong><br />
  Check with the cubic relations: sum = 6 = −b/a ✓ ; product = 6 = −d/a ✓
</div>

**How to do the division.** Two routes give the same quotient. Ordinary long division of polynomials
works exactly like long division of numbers. The quicker route at Foundation level is to write the
quotient with unknown coefficients and match terms: if x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6 = (x − 1)(x² + mx + n),
then expanding gives x³ + (m − 1)x² + (n − m)x − n, so −n = −6 → n = 6 and m − 1 = −6 → m = −5. That
recovers x² − 5x + 6 in two lines, and the middle coefficient acts as a free check: n − m = 6 + 5 =
11 ✓

The relations between roots and coefficients extend to cubics. For ax³ + bx² + cx + d = 0 with roots
α, β, γ:

<div class="formula">α + β + γ = −b/a &nbsp;&nbsp; αβ + βγ + γα = c/a &nbsp;&nbsp; αβγ = −d/a</div>

The alternating signs are the pattern: minus, plus, minus. They give a fast check on any cubic you
have just solved.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Trial values are not random. Any rational root of a cubic with integer coefficients must be a
    factor of the constant term divided by a factor of the leading coefficient. For
    2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6 = 0 the candidates are ±1, ±2, ±3, ±6 and their halves — about a dozen
    numbers, and x = 3 works on the third try.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 17. Applications to business problems

Word problems are marked on the translation, not the algebra. Four disciplined steps:

1. **Name the unknown in words first** — "let x be the number of units sold in a month". A letter
   with no stated meaning is where most wrong answers begin.
2. **Write one equation for each independent fact** in the question. Count the facts; you need as
   many equations as unknowns.
3. **Solve.**
4. **Answer the question that was asked.** If the question asks for the *selling price* and you
   solved for the *number of units*, one more line of arithmetic is still needed.

**Cost, revenue and break-even.** With a fixed cost F, a variable cost v per unit and a selling
price p per unit:

<div class="formula">Total cost = F + vx &nbsp;&nbsp; Total revenue = px &nbsp;&nbsp; Profit = (p − v)x − F</div>
<div class="formula">Break-even quantity = F ÷ (p − v), where p − v is the contribution per unit</div>

At break-even the profit is zero, so the two lines cross. The press in the opening paragraph is the
same idea with two cost lines instead of a cost line and a revenue line.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — the printing press from the opening paragraph</div>
  Let x be the number of copies printed.<br />
  Plan A costs 18,000 + 6x · Plan B costs 15x<br />
  The plans cost the same when 18,000 + 6x = 15x → 18,000 = 9x → <strong>x = 2,000 copies</strong><br />
  At 2,000 copies each plan costs ₹ 30,000. Below 2,000 copies Plan B is cheaper (its saving on the
  setting charge outweighs the higher rate); above 2,000 copies Plan A is cheaper.
</div>

A **target-profit** question is the same equation with the profit written in instead of zero:
quantity = (fixed cost + target profit) ÷ contribution per unit.

**Demand, supply and equilibrium.** A demand equation falls as price rises, a supply equation rises
with price, and the **equilibrium** price is where the two quantities are equal. Set the two
right-hand sides equal, solve for p, then put p back into either equation to get the quantity.

**Mixtures and alligation-style splits.** Two grades at different rates blended into one lot: let x
be the quantity of the first grade, write a **quantity** equation and a **value** equation, and
solve them together.

<div class="formula">x + y = total quantity &nbsp;&nbsp; r₁x + r₂y = (blend rate) × (total quantity)</div>

**Work and time.** Work rates add. If A finishes a job in a days, A does 1/a of it per day.

<div class="formula">1/a + 1/b = 1/t, where t is the time taken together</div>

**Digits.** A two-digit number with tens digit t and units digit u is **10t + u**, never tu.
Reversing the digits gives 10u + t, and the difference between the two is always 9(t − u).

**Ages.** Write the present ages as the unknowns, and express past or future ages by subtracting or
adding the same number of years **from every person's age**. "Five years ago" means x − 5 and
y − 5 together, not x − 5 and y.

**Cost, profit and percentages.** A percentage is a multiplier, so "sold at a profit of 20% on cost"
means selling price = 1.2 × cost, while "a margin of 20% on the selling price" means cost = 0.8 ×
selling price. Both translate into linear equations, and the two produce different answers from the
same 20% — read which base the percentage is quoted on before you write the equation.

**How many equations do you need?** One for each unknown, and they must be independent. If a word
problem gives you three facts about two unknowns, one of the three is either a repetition or a check
— use two to solve and the third to verify. If it gives you two facts about three unknowns, the
question must be asking for a combination such as x + y + z rather than for the individual values.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    In an age or digit problem, a quadratic often produces one negative or one non-integral root.
    That root is <strong>rejected on the facts</strong> — an age cannot be −3 and a digit cannot be
    7.5 — and rejecting it is part of the answer, not an oversight. But do not reject a negative root
    reflexively: in a profit-and-loss or a temperature question a negative value can be perfectly
    meaningful.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 18. Negative marking and when to leave a question

Paper 3 is entirely objective and carries **negative marking of 0.25 mark for each wrong answer**. A
correct answer earns 1 mark, a wrong one costs 0.25 and an unattempted one scores 0.

The arithmetic of that is worth knowing calmly. A pure guess among four options is right one time in
four: on average, four such guesses earn 1 mark and lose 3 × 0.25 = 0.75, a net gain of 0.25 marks
for four questions. So blind guessing is very slightly positive but close to neutral, and its spread
is wide.

What changes the picture is **elimination**. Rule out one option and a guess among the remaining
three is right one time in three, worth about 0.5 marks per four attempts. Rule out two and a guess
between the last two is worth about 1.5 marks per four. In this chapter elimination is unusually
easy, because the distractors are built from named errors: an option carrying the wrong sign on a
sum of roots, or a single root where a quadratic must have two, can often be discarded on sight.

A sensible working rule for this chapter: **answer when you can eliminate at least one option;
consider leaving it when you cannot narrow the field at all**. Nothing about that rule needs to be
followed anxiously — the marks at stake in either direction are small, and time spent on a question
you can actually finish is worth more than either choice.

## Fully worked examples

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — break-even and the target-profit quantity (easy)">
  <p>
    Sharada Foods packs one variety of pickle. Fixed costs are ₹ 2,40,000 a month; the variable cost
    is ₹ 180 a jar and the selling price is ₹ 300 a jar. Find (a) the break-even quantity, (b) the
    quantity needed for a monthly profit of ₹ 90,000, and (c) the new break-even quantity if
    competition forces the price down to ₹ 270 while costs stay the same.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — set up the profit equation</div>
    Let x be the number of jars sold in a month.<br />
    Revenue = 300x · Total cost = 2,40,000 + 180x<br />
    Contribution per jar = 300 − 180 = <strong>₹ 120</strong><br />
    Profit = 120x − 2,40,000
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (a) break-even: profit is zero</div>
    120x − 2,40,000 = 0 → 120x = 2,40,000 → <strong>x = 2,000 jars</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (b) profit of ₹ 90,000</div>
    120x − 2,40,000 = 90,000 → 120x = 3,30,000 → <strong>x = 2,750 jars</strong><br />
    The extra 750 jars carry ₹ 120 of contribution each: 750 × 120 = ₹ 90,000 ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — (c) price cut to ₹ 270</div>
    New contribution = 270 − 180 = ₹ 90 · 2,40,000 ÷ 90 = 2,666.67<br />
    Jars are whole, and 2,666 jars still leave a small loss, so the firm must sell
    <strong>2,667 jars</strong> to break even. A 10% price cut raised the break-even volume by a
    third — that is the leverage the contribution per unit carries.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — two unknowns by elimination and by cross-multiplication (easy-moderate)">
  <p>
    A stationery wholesaler in Nashik sells registers and files. One retailer buys 3 registers and
    4 files for ₹ 1,010. Another buys 5 registers and returns 2 files against a credit note, paying
    ₹ 860 net. Find the price of each item, and confirm the answer by cross-multiplication.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — translate</div>
    Let r be the price of a register and f the price of a file, both in rupees.<br />
    (i) 3r + 4f = 1,010 &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; (ii) 5r − 2f = 860
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — elimination</div>
    (ii) × 2 → 10r − 4f = 1,720. The f terms are now +4f and −4f, so add:<br />
    13r = 2,730 → <strong>r = ₹ 210</strong><br />
    From (i): 4f = 1,010 − 630 = 380 → <strong>f = ₹ 95</strong><br />
    Check in (ii): 5(210) − 2(95) = 1,050 − 190 = 860 ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — the same pair by cross-multiplication</div>
    Rewrite with everything on the left: 3r + 4f − 1,010 = 0 and 5r − 2f − 860 = 0<br />
    a₁ = 3, b₁ = 4, c₁ = −1,010 · a₂ = 5, b₂ = −2, c₂ = −860<br />
    Denominator a₁b₂ − a₂b₁ = (3)(−2) − (5)(4) = −26<br />
    r = (b₁c₂ − b₂c₁) ÷ (−26) = [(4)(−860) − (−2)(−1,010)] ÷ (−26) = (−3,440 − 2,020) ÷ (−26) = <strong>210</strong><br />
    f = (c₁a₂ − c₂a₁) ÷ (−26) = [(−1,010)(5) − (−860)(3)] ÷ (−26) = (−5,050 + 2,580) ÷ (−26) = <strong>95</strong>
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — consistency and the value of k (moderate)">
  <p>
    For the system 3x + ky = 12 and 6x + 8y = 30, find the value of k for which the system has
    (a) no solution and (b) a unique solution. Then say what happens if the second equation's
    constant is 24 instead of 30.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — write down the three ratios</div>
    a₁/a₂ = 3/6 = 1/2 &nbsp;·&nbsp; b₁/b₂ = k/8 &nbsp;·&nbsp; c₁/c₂ = 12/30 = 2/5
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (a) no solution</div>
    No solution needs a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ but c₁/c₂ different.<br />
    k/8 = 1/2 → <strong>k = 4</strong><br />
    Third ratio check: 2/5 ≠ 1/2, so the ratios really do split → the system is inconsistent and has
    <strong>no solution when k = 4</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (b) unique solution</div>
    A unique solution needs a₁/a₂ ≠ b₁/b₂, that is k/8 ≠ 1/2 → <strong>k ≠ 4</strong>. Any other
    value of k, positive or negative, gives one crossing point.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — the constant changed to 24</div>
    Now c₁/c₂ = 12/24 = 1/2. At k = 4 all three ratios equal 1/2, so the second equation is simply
    the first doubled: the system has <strong>infinitely many solutions</strong>. The same k, the
    opposite conclusion — which is exactly why the third ratio must always be checked.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — three unknowns from three invoices (moderate)">
  <p>
    A college store's three purchase bills read: 2 pens, 3 notebooks and 1 calculator for ₹ 470;
    1 pen, 2 notebooks and 3 calculators for ₹ 840; 4 pens, 1 notebook and 2 calculators for ₹ 640.
    Find the unit price of each item.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — translate</div>
    Let x, y and z be the prices of a pen, a notebook and a calculator, in rupees.<br />
    (i) 2x + 3y + z = 470 &nbsp; (ii) x + 2y + 3z = 840 &nbsp; (iii) 4x + y + 2z = 640
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — eliminate x from two pairs</div>
    2 × (ii) − (i): (2x + 4y + 6z) − (2x + 3y + z) = 1,680 − 470 → y + 5z = 1,210 … (iv)<br />
    (iii) − 4 × (ii): (4x + y + 2z) − (4x + 8y + 12z) = 640 − 3,360 → −7y − 10z = −2,720,
    that is 7y + 10z = 2,720 … (v)
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — solve the pair</div>
    From (iv): y = 1,210 − 5z. Substitute in (v):<br />
    7(1,210 − 5z) + 10z = 2,720 → 8,470 − 35z + 10z = 2,720 → −25z = −5,750 → <strong>z = ₹ 230</strong><br />
    y = 1,210 − 5(230) = <strong>₹ 60</strong> · From (i): 2x = 470 − 180 − 230 = 60 → <strong>x = ₹ 30</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — check in all three</div>
    (i) 60 + 180 + 230 = 470 ✓ &nbsp; (ii) 30 + 120 + 690 = 840 ✓ &nbsp; (iii) 120 + 60 + 460 = 640 ✓
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — a quadratic from a trading situation (moderate)">
  <p>
    A dealer spent ₹ 8,000 buying identical steel tiffin boxes. Had he negotiated and bought 20 more
    boxes for the same total outlay, each box would have cost ₹ 20 less. How many boxes did he buy,
    and at what price each?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — name the unknown and write the two prices</div>
    Let x be the number of boxes actually bought.<br />
    Actual price per box = 8,000 ÷ x · Hypothetical price = 8,000 ÷ (x + 20)<br />
    The hypothetical price is ₹ 20 lower: 8,000/x − 8,000/(x + 20) = 20
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — clear the fractions</div>
    Multiply by x(x + 20): 8,000(x + 20) − 8,000x = 20x(x + 20)<br />
    1,60,000 = 20x² + 400x → divide by 20: x² + 20x − 8,000 = 0
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — solve and reject on the facts</div>
    D = 400 + 32,000 = 32,400 → √32,400 = 180<br />
    x = (−20 ± 180) ÷ 2 → x = 80 or x = −100<br />
    A count of boxes cannot be negative, so <strong>x = 80 boxes</strong>, at 8,000 ÷ 80 =
    <strong>₹ 100 each</strong>.<br />
    Check the story: 100 boxes would have cost 8,000 ÷ 100 = ₹ 80 each, which is ₹ 20 less ✓
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 6 — working entirely through sum and product of roots (moderate-hard)">
  <p>
    The two roots of 2x² − 11x + k = 0 differ by 3/2. Find k. Then, without solving the equation,
    find α² + β² and form the equation whose roots are α + 2 and β + 2.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — the difference is a discriminant question</div>
    s = α + β = −(−11)/2 = 11/2 &nbsp;·&nbsp; p = αβ = k/2<br />
    (α − β)² = s² − 4p = 121/4 − 2k<br />
    The difference is 3/2, so (α − β)² = 9/4:<br />
    121/4 − 2k = 9/4 → 2k = 112/4 = 28 → <strong>k = 14</strong><br />
    Check: 2x² − 11x + 14 = (2x − 7)(x − 2), roots 7/2 and 2, difference 3/2 ✓
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — α² + β² without the roots</div>
    p = k/2 = 7 · α² + β² = s² − 2p = 121/4 − 14 = <strong>65/4</strong><br />
    (The roots are 7/2 and 2: 49/4 + 4 = 65/4 ✓)
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — build the shifted equation</div>
    New sum = (α + 2) + (β + 2) = s + 4 = 11/2 + 4 = 19/2<br />
    New product = (α + 2)(β + 2) = p + 2s + 4 = 7 + 11 + 4 = 22<br />
    x² − (19/2)x + 22 = 0 → multiply by 2 → <strong>2x² − 19x + 44 = 0</strong><br />
    Check against the shifted roots 11/2 and 4: sum 19/2 ✓, product 22 ✓
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 7 — reducible to quadratic form: a surd and a substitution (moderate-hard)">
  <p>
    Solve (a) √(2x + 7) − x = 2 and (b) (x² + 2x)² − 11(x² + 2x) + 24 = 0.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) isolate the surd, then square</div>
    √(2x + 7) = x + 2<br />
    Square both sides: 2x + 7 = x² + 4x + 4 → x² + 2x − 3 = 0 → (x + 3)(x − 1) = 0<br />
    Candidates: x = 1 and x = −3
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (a) test both against the ORIGINAL equation</div>
    x = 1: √9 − 1 = 3 − 1 = 2 ✓ — a genuine root<br />
    x = −3: √1 − (−3) = 1 + 3 = 4, and 4 ≠ 2 ✗ — <strong>extraneous</strong>, created by the squaring<br />
    <strong>The only root is x = 1.</strong> Squaring turned "√(2x+7) = x + 2" into a statement that
    also accepts √(2x+7) = −(x + 2), and x = −3 satisfies that second, unwanted equation.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (b) substitute the repeated block</div>
    Let y = x² + 2x. Then y² − 11y + 24 = 0 → (y − 3)(y − 8) = 0 → y = 3 or y = 8
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 4 — (b) return to x, twice</div>
    y = 3: x² + 2x − 3 = 0 → (x + 3)(x − 1) = 0 → x = −3, 1<br />
    y = 8: x² + 2x − 8 = 0 → (x + 4)(x − 2) = 0 → x = −4, 2<br />
    <strong>Four roots: −4, −3, 1 and 2</strong> — the equation is of degree 4 in x, so four is right.
    Stopping at y = 3 and y = 8 answers a question nobody asked.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 8 — a cubic by locating one rational root (hard)">
  <p>
    Solve 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6 = 0 and verify the answer with the cubic root relations.

  <p>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 10 — three short translations: work, digits and ages (easy-moderate)">
  <p>

    (b) The digits of a two-digit number add to 11, and reversing the digits increases the number by
    45. Find the number. (c) A father is three times as old as his son; eight years ago he was five
    times as old. Find both present ages.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) rates add, times do not</div>
    In one hour A fills 1/12 of the tank and B fills 1/18.<br />
    Together per hour: 1/12 + 1/18 = 3/36 + 2/36 = 5/36<br />
    Time = 36/5 = <strong>7.2 hours, that is 7 hours 12 minutes</strong><br />
    Adding the times to get 30 hours, or averaging them to get 15, both contradict common sense: two
    pipes must be faster than either alone.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) a two-digit number is 10t + u</div>
    Let t be the tens digit and u the units digit. The number is 10t + u; reversed it is 10u + t.<br />
    (i) t + u = 11<br />

    Add (i) and (ii): 2u = 16 → u = 8, so t = 3 → the number is <strong>38</strong><br />
    Check: 83 − 38 = 45 ✓ and 3 + 8 = 11 ✓

## Common mistakes and examiner traps

- **The sign on transposition.** A term changes sign only when it crosses the equals sign. Half the
  wrong options in a linear-equation question are the answer you get from one flipped sign.
- **Losing the second root.** Every square root taken while solving carries **±**. `(x + 2)² = 9`
  gives x = 1 *and* x = −5, never x = 1 alone.
- **Splitting the middle term using c instead of a × c.** The two numbers must multiply to
  **a × c** and add to b. Using c alone works only when a = 1, which is exactly why the examiner
  sets a ≠ 1.
- **Reading a root straight off a bracket.** The factor (2x − 5) gives x = 5/2, not 5 and not −5.
  Set each factor to zero and solve it.
- **Dividing only part of the numerator by 2a.** The whole of −b ± √(b² − 4ac) is divided by 2a.
- **Forgetting the sign of b inside the formula.** For 3x² − 11x + 6 = 0, b = −11 so −b = +11.
  Treating b as +11 flips both roots' signs.
- **Sum and product swapped, or the sum's sign dropped.** Sum = **−b/a**, product = **c/a**. For
  5x² − 12x + 4 = 0 that is +12/5 and 4/5; the options will contain −12/5 and the swapped pair.
- **Taking the root of the discriminant before finishing it.** √(b² − 4ac) is one square root of one
  number. It is not √(b²) − √(4ac), and √49 is 7 not 24.5.
- **"D = 0 means one root."** It means **two equal real roots**. Similarly "the roots are real"
  includes the equal case, so the condition is D ≥ 0, not D &gt; 0.
- **Answering the substituted letter.** In x⁴ − 13x² + 36 = 0 the answer is x = ±2, ±3, not
  y = 4, 9. Always come back to the original variable.
- **Keeping an extraneous root.** After squaring, every candidate must be tested in the **original**
  equation. In √(2x + 7) − x = 2 the value −3 passes the squared equation and fails the real one.
- **Skipping the third ratio in a consistency question.** a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ alone does not mean "no
  solution"; if c₁/c₂ matches too, the answer is "infinitely many".
- **Cross-multiplication with the constants left on the right.** The formula needs the "= 0" form.
  Wrong signs on c₁ and c₂ produce a neat pair of answers with both signs inverted.
- **Eliminating a different variable from each pair in a three-unknown system.** Remove the same
  variable from both pairs, or the two new equations will not share their unknowns.
- **Rejecting a negative root by reflex.** A count of boxes cannot be −100 and an age cannot be −3,
  so those go. But a negative root is a perfectly good answer for a temperature, a loss, or a change
  in demand — reject only for a stated reason.
- **Ageing one person and not the other.** "Eight years ago" applies to every age in the problem at
  the same time.
- **Writing a two-digit number as tu.** It is 10t + u. The product form is the most costly single
  translation error in the applications section.

## One-page revision summary

**Equation vs identity.** An equation is true for particular values (its roots); an identity is true
for every value. Test with two easy substitutions, usually x = 0 and x = 1.

**Degree and root count.** Degree n means n roots counted with repetition. Linear 1, quadratic 2,
cubic 3.

**Linear in one unknown.** ax + b = 0, a ≠ 0 → x = −b/a. Clear fractions by the LCM, remove
brackets, transpose (signs change), divide. Reject any root that makes an original denominator zero.

**Two unknowns.** a₁x + b₁y = c₁ and a₂x + b₂y = c₂. Methods: elimination (scale, then add if the
matched signs are opposite, subtract if the same), substitution, and cross-multiplication. Writing
both as a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0:

<div class="formula">x ÷ (b₁c₂ − b₂c₁) = y ÷ (c₁a₂ − c₂a₁) = 1 ÷ (a₁b₂ − a₂b₁)</div>

**Consistency.** a₁/a₂ ≠ b₁/b₂ → unique solution (consistent, independent). a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ ≠ c₁/c₂ →
no solution (inconsistent, parallel lines). a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂ → infinitely many (dependent, the
same line). The unique-solution test is the same as a₁b₂ − a₂b₁ ≠ 0.

**Three unknowns.** Eliminate the *same* variable from two pairs, solve the resulting two-unknown
system, back-substitute, and check in all three originals. A leftover 0 = 5 means no solution; a
leftover 0 = 0 means infinitely many.

**Quadratic, ax² + bx + c = 0, a ≠ 0.** Factorise by splitting b into two parts with sum b and
product a × c; or complete the square; or use

<div class="formula">x = [ −b ± √(b² − 4ac) ] ÷ 2a</div>

**Discriminant D = b² − 4ac.** D &gt; 0 and a perfect square → real, distinct, rational. D &gt; 0 not a
perfect square → real, distinct, irrational, in conjugate pairs p ± √q. D = 0 → real and equal, both
roots −b/2a. D &lt; 0 → no real roots; the roots are a conjugate pair of imaginary numbers.

**Roots and coefficients (quadratic).** α + β = −b/a; αβ = c/a. Build an equation from roots with
x² − (sum)x + (product) = 0, then scale to clear fractions.

**Symmetric functions**, with s = α + β and p = αβ:

<div class="formula">α² + β² = s² − 2p &nbsp;·&nbsp; (α − β)² = s² − 4p &nbsp;·&nbsp; α³ + β³ = s³ − 3ps</div>
<div class="formula">1/α + 1/β = s/p &nbsp;·&nbsp; 1/α² + 1/β² = (s² − 2p)/p² &nbsp;·&nbsp; α/β + β/α = (s² − 2p)/p</div>

**Roots and coefficients (cubic ax³ + bx² + cx + d = 0).** α + β + γ = −b/a; αβ + βγ + γα = c/a;
αβγ = −d/a. Solve by testing candidate rational roots (factors of d over factors of a), dividing out
the linear factor, and solving the quadratic that remains.

**Reducible to quadratic.** ax⁴ + bx² + c = 0 → put y = x², solve, then take both square roots of
each positive y. A repeated block → substitute the block. A surd → isolate and square, then test
every candidate in the original equation and discard extraneous roots.

**Applications.** Break-even quantity = fixed cost ÷ (price − variable cost); profit =
(price − variable cost) × quantity − fixed cost. Equilibrium: set demand quantity equal to supply
quantity, solve for price, substitute back. Mixtures: one quantity equation plus one value equation.
Work: rates add, 1/a + 1/b = 1/t. Two-digit number = 10t + u; reversed = 10u + t; difference
= 9(t − u). Ages: apply the same shift in years to every person. Reject a root only when the
situation forbids it.

**Marking.** 1 mark correct, −0.25 wrong, 0 unattempted. Guessing among four is close to neutral;
eliminating even one option makes an attempt clearly worthwhile.
