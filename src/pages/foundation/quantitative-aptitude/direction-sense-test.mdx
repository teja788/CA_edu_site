A delivery rider in Pune leaves the depot and rides 4 km east to a warehouse, turns and rides
3 km north to a shop, and stops. His manager, tracking him on a map, asks a single question: **how
far is the rider from the depot in a straight line?** Not how far he rode — that is 7 km — but how
far apart the two points are.

Draw the two legs on paper and a right-angled triangle appears, with the two rides as its short
sides and the straight-line gap as its longest side. The gap is √(4² + 3²) = √25 = **5 km**. The
whole of this chapter is that one move: turn a described walk into a picture, read the picture as
right triangles and straight lines, and answer the exact question asked.

Paper 3 asks these in Part B, Logical Reasoning, and they are among the most reliable marks on the
paper — the arithmetic is small and every answer is checkable. Marks are lost not on the geometry but
on three things: mixing up left and right after the walker has turned, confusing the total distance
walked with the straight-line distance from the start, and naming the wrong final direction. This
chapter fixes each of those in turn.

## 1. The compass and the eight directions

Every direction question is read against a **compass rose**. The four **cardinal** directions are
North, East, South and West. Fix them by the rising sun: face the sunrise and you face **East**;
North is then to your left, South to your right, West behind you.

<div class="formula">Clockwise from the top: North → East → South → West → back to North</div>

Between each pair of cardinals sits an **ordinal** (or intercardinal) direction, named by joining
the two cardinals it lies between, the North–South part always spoken first:

- **North-East (NE)** — between North and East
- **South-East (SE)** — between South and East
- **South-West (SW)** — between South and West
- **North-West (NW)** — between North and West

That gives the **eight-point compass**. Two facts about it carry most of the easy marks.

**Adjacent cardinals are a right angle apart.** North to East is 90°, East to South is another 90°,
and so on around the circle of 360°. Each ordinal sits exactly halfway, so North to North-East is
45°, and North to South-East is 135°.

**Opposite directions are a straight 180° apart.** The opposite of North is South; the opposite of
East is West; the opposite of North-East is **South-West**; the opposite of North-West is South-East.
To find any opposite, flip **both** letters: NE → SW, SE → NW.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    A quick table settles the left/right questions before any walking starts. If a person <strong>faces
    North</strong>, then East is on the right hand, West on the left, South behind. Rotate the whole
    table with the walker: <strong>facing East</strong>, the right hand points South and the left hand
    North; <strong>facing South</strong>, the right hand points West; <strong>facing West</strong>, the
    right hand points North. The rule in one line: your right hand is always 90° clockwise from the way
    you face.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    "Left" and "right" are the walker's, not the reader's, and they turn with the walker. When a
    person walking South turns left, they turn towards the <strong>East</strong>, not the West — even
    though East is on the reader's right side of the page. Always re-read left and right from where the
    walker is now facing, never from the map.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. Drawing the path on paper

The single habit that makes this chapter easy is to **draw**, not to picture in the head. Keep to one
convention and never vary it:

<div class="formula">North points up the page · East points right · South points down · West points left</div>

Then follow four steps for every question.

1. **Mark the start** with a dot and label it. Every "how far from the start" answer is measured back
   to this dot.
2. **Draw each leg** in turn as an arrow the right way, and write its length on it. A turn is a
   corner between two arrows.
3. **Find the finish** — the tip of the last arrow.
4. **Answer the exact question** — the straight-line distance from start to finish, or the direction
   of the finish from the start, or the direction the walker now faces. These are three different
   questions and a single picture answers all three.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — walk East 6, North 8, and read the picture</div>
  Start at the dot. Arrow 1: 6 units right (East). Arrow 2: from that tip, 8 units up (North).<br />
  Finish is 6 right and 8 up from the start.<br />
  Straight-line distance = √(6² + 8²) = √(36 + 64) = √100 = <strong>10 units</strong><br />
  Direction of the finish from the start: up and to the right, so <strong>North-East</strong><br />
  Facing at the finish: the last arrow pointed North, so the walker <strong>faces North</strong>.
</div>

A coordinate grid makes the arithmetic mechanical, and the verifier behind this chapter uses exactly
this. Put the start at (0, 0). **East adds to the across-value (x); West subtracts. North adds to the
up-value (y); South subtracts.** After every leg the finish is a single point (x, y). Everything else
is read off that point.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Distances along the <strong>same line add or cancel before any triangle is drawn</strong>. A walk
    of 10 m East then 4 m West is not two sides of a triangle — it is a net 6 m East, because the two
    moves are along one line. Collapse every such pair first; only genuinely perpendicular legs make a
    right triangle.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. Net displacement and the shortest distance from start

The **distance walked** is the sum of the leg lengths — it is the length of the whole crooked path.
The **displacement**, or shortest distance from the start, is the straight line from the first dot to
the last point. The two are equal only when the walk never turns. Every "how far is he from the
starting point" question asks for the **displacement**, and the trap answer is the distance walked.

Reduce the finish to a point (x, y) as in Section 2. Then:

<div class="formula">Net East–West gap = x (East positive, West negative) · Net North–South gap = y (North positive, South negative)</div>
<div class="formula">Shortest distance from start = √(x² + y²)</div>

The formula is the **Pythagoras theorem**: the net East–West gap and the net North–South gap are the
two perpendicular sides of a right triangle, and the shortest distance is its hypotenuse.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — East 3, North 4</div>
  Finish point: x = 3 (East), y = 4 (North)<br />
  Shortest distance = √(3² + 4²) = √(9 + 16) = √25 = <strong>5 units</strong><br />
  The 3–4–5 triangle is the one every examiner reaches for first.
</div>

A short list of **Pythagorean triples** turns almost every distance question into instant recall.
When you see two of the three numbers, the third is fixed:

| Two perpendicular legs | Straight-line distance |
|---|---|
| 3 and 4 | 5 |
| 6 and 8 | 10 |
| 5 and 12 | 13 |
| 9 and 12 | 15 |
| 8 and 15 | 17 |
| 7 and 24 | 25 |
| 20 and 21 | 29 |
| 12 and 35 | 37 |

Multi-leg walks reduce to a triple once the same-line moves are cancelled.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — North 10, East 6, South 2</div>
  Net up–down: 10 North − 2 South = 8 North, so y = 8<br />
  Net across: 6 East, so x = 6<br />
  Shortest distance = √(6² + 8²) = √100 = <strong>10 units</strong>, and the finish lies
  <strong>North-East</strong> of the start.
</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    Adding the legs to answer "how far from the start" is the most common error in the chapter. In the
    walk above the walker covered 10 + 6 + 2 = 18 units of road, but is only 10 units from the start.
    An option reading "18" is placed there for exactly this slip. Read whether the question asks
    <strong>how far he walked</strong> or <strong>how far he is from the start</strong>, and answer that
    one.
  </p>
</Callout>

When the two net gaps are equal — say 5 East and 5 North — the finish is due **North-East** and the
distance is 5√2, about 7.07. Foundation questions are usually built so the answer is a whole number,
so a √2 answer is a signal to re-check the numbers, not usually the intended value.

## 4. Turns: left, right, clockwise and anticlockwise

A **turn** changes the direction the walker faces; it does not move the walker. Two independent facts
must be tracked through a walk: **where the walker is** (the point) and **which way the walker faces**
(the heading). Confusing the two is where turn questions are lost.

A turn is always measured from the **current heading**, not from North.

- **Turn right** = turn 90° **clockwise**. Facing North and turning right leaves you facing East.
- **Turn left** = turn 90° **anticlockwise**. Facing North and turning left leaves you facing West.

Chain the turns around the compass in the turning direction:

<div class="formula">Right (clockwise): North → East → South → West → North</div>
<div class="formula">Left (anticlockwise): North → West → South → East → North</div>

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — face North, turn right, walk 12, turn left, walk 5</div>
  Start facing North at the dot.<br />
  Turn right → now facing East. Walk 12 → finish so far (12, 0).<br />
  Turn left → from East, left is North → now facing North. Walk 5 → finish (12, 5).<br />
  Shortest distance = √(12² + 5²) = √169 = <strong>13 units</strong>; the walker now
  <strong>faces North</strong>.
</div>

Some questions give the turns in **degrees** rather than as "left" or "right". Convert on the same
clock face: 90° is a quarter turn, 180° is an about-face to the opposite direction, 270° one way is
the same as 90° the other, and 360° returns to the start.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — face East, turn 270° clockwise</div>
  270° clockwise = three quarter-turns clockwise: East → South → West → North.<br />
  The walker now faces <strong>North</strong>. The same end-point is reached by a single 90°
  anticlockwise (left) turn — a useful shortcut when the number is 270.
</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    A turn changes the heading only; it does not add distance. Reading "turns right" as "moves right by
    one unit" invents a leg that is not there. Equally, after two or more turns the words left and right
    must be re-read from the <strong>new</strong> heading each time — a left turn after a right turn does
    not cancel to "straight on" unless the walker had turned right immediately before.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Count turns in one currency. Convert every "left" to −90° and every "right" to +90°, add them, and
    reduce the total modulo 360°. Facing North, a walk of right, right, left is +90 +90 −90 = +90°, a
    single right turn, so the walker ends facing East — no need to track each step around the compass.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Shadows and the sun

The sun rises in the **East** and sets in the **West**. A shadow always falls on the side **away from
the sun**, so the shadow points opposite to the sun's position in the sky.

- **Morning, just after sunrise** — the sun is in the East, so every shadow falls towards the
  **West**.
- **Evening, near sunset** — the sun is in the West, so every shadow falls towards the **East**.
- **Around noon** — the sun is nearly overhead, shadows are very short and unreliable, and a good
  question will avoid asking a direction from a midday shadow. Treat "at noon, the shadow points…" as
  a warning that the intended answer is "cannot be determined" or that the question means the general
  midday sun, slightly to the South at Indian latitudes.

A shadow's direction fixes the sun, and the sun with the time of day fixes everything else. The usual
question gives you a person, the time of day and the side their shadow falls on, and asks which way
the person faces.

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Worked line by line — early morning, a girl's shadow falls to her right; which way does she face?</div>
  Morning sun is in the East, so the shadow falls towards the <strong>West</strong>.<br />
  The shadow is on her right, so her right hand points West.<br />
  The right hand is 90° clockwise from the facing, so the facing is 90° anticlockwise from West:
  West → South.<br />
  She faces <strong>South</strong>.
</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    The shadow points <strong>away</strong> from the sun, not towards it. In the morning the sun is
    East and the shadow is West; answering "the shadow falls East in the morning" reverses the whole
    chain and turns every following direction the wrong way. Fix the sun first, then place the shadow
    opposite it.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    Two people <strong>facing each other</strong> have their left and right hands swapped relative to
    one another, but a shadow falls the same way in the world for both — towards the West in the
    morning. So "the shadow was to A's left and to B's right" is consistent with A and B facing
    opposite ways, and is a favourite way to test whether you are reading the world or the person.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. Mirror images and opposite-direction traps

Two families of trap appear once a chapter is comfortable with plain walks.

**Opposite directions.** When two people start from the same point and walk in opposite directions,
each is in the opposite direction from the other, and the distance between them is the **sum** of the
two distances, not the difference. If A walks 6 km North and B walks 4 km South from the same start,
B is due South of A, and they are 6 + 4 = 10 km apart.

**Relative direction.** "P is East of Q; Q is North of R; where is R from P?" is answered by
sketching, not by guessing. Place Q at the origin: P is East of Q so P is to the right; R is South of
Q (because Q is North of R) so R is below. Then R is **down and to the left of P**, that is R is
**South-West** of P. Reverse a stated relation carefully: if P is East of Q, then Q is West of P.

**Mirror reflection.** A plane mirror reverses the direction facing it while leaving the perpendicular
directions unchanged. The workable rule for these puzzles: a mirror swaps a direction for its
**opposite along the line facing the mirror**. Face a mirror while walking North and your reflection
walks South towards you; the reflection of a movement to the East, seen in a mirror on the North wall,
appears to move to the West. Read the plane of the mirror first, then flip the one direction that
crosses it.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    For people walking in opposite directions from one point, the gap is the <strong>sum</strong> of
    the distances. Subtracting — treating 6 km North and 4 km South as a 2 km gap — is the built-in
    trap. Subtraction is right only when both walk the <strong>same</strong> way along one line, where
    one is simply ahead of the other.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. Negative marking and when to leave a question

Paper 3 is entirely objective and carries **negative marking of 0.25 mark for each wrong answer**. A
correct answer earns 1 mark, a wrong one costs 0.25, and an unattempted one scores 0.

Direction questions are unusually safe ground for this rule, for two reasons. First, the answer is
**checkable**: you can redraw the path and confirm the finish point in seconds, so a finished question
is rarely a guess. Second, the distractors are built from **named errors** — the distance walked
instead of the displacement, a left/right swap, the opposite final direction — and once you know the
error a wrong option is testing, you can often discard two or three options on sight.

The expected-value arithmetic is the same as for the rest of the paper. A blind guess among four
options gains, on average, ¼(1) − ¾(0.25) = 0.0625 mark — barely positive. Eliminate one option and a
guess among three is worth about ⅓(1) − ⅔(0.25) ≈ 0.17 mark; eliminate two and the guess between the
last pair is worth ½(1) − ½(0.25) = 0.375 mark. In this chapter elimination is easy, so most attempted
questions are clearly worth attempting.

A calm working rule: **redraw, answer, and move on where the picture is clear; attempt on elimination
where it is not; leave only the rare question you cannot narrow at all.** The marks at stake either way
are small, and a clean drawing settles most questions faster than the decision to skip them.

## Fully worked examples

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 1 — the shortest distance from the start (easy)">
  <p>
    Meera walks 8 m towards the East from her gate, then turns left and walks 6 m. How far is she from
    her gate, and in which direction does the gate lie from her?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — draw and place the finish</div>
    Start at (0, 0). East 8 → (8, 0). Turn left from East → now facing North. North 6 → (8, 6).<br />
    Finish point: x = 8 (East), y = 6 (North).
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — apply Pythagoras</div>
    Distance from the gate = √(8² + 6²) = √(64 + 36) = √100 = <strong>10 m</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — direction of the gate from Meera</div>
    Meera is 8 East and 6 North of the gate, so the gate is 8 West and 6 South of Meera — down and to
    the left — that is <strong>South-West</strong> of her. Note that the gate's direction from Meera is
    the exact opposite of Meera's direction from the gate.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 2 — distance walked versus displacement (easy-moderate)">
  <p>
    A cyclist rides 12 km North, then 5 km East, then 12 km South. (a) What total distance did he ride?
    (b) How far is he from his starting point, and in which direction?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — (a) the distance ridden is the sum of the legs</div>
    12 + 5 + 12 = <strong>29 km</strong> of road covered.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (b) collapse the same-line moves first</div>
    Up–down: 12 North − 12 South = 0, so y = 0. Across: 5 East, so x = 5.<br />
    The finish is 5 km due East of the start: displacement = <strong>5 km, East</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — the point of the question</div>
    He rode 29 km but ends only 5 km from home, because the two 12 km legs are along one North–South
    line and cancel exactly. "29" and "12" are both offered as distractors; the question asks for the
    straight-line gap, which is 5.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 3 — a walk with several turns (moderate)">
  <p>
    Starting from his office and facing North, Arjun walks 3 km, turns right and walks 4 km, turns
    right and walks 3 km, then turns left and walks 6 km. How far is he from the office in a straight
    line?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — track heading and point together</div>
    Facing North, walk 3 → (0, 3).<br />
    Turn right → facing East, walk 4 → (4, 3).<br />
    Turn right → facing South, walk 3 → (4, 0).<br />
    Turn left → from South, left is East → facing East, walk 6 → (10, 0).
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — read the finish</div>
    Net up–down: 3 North − 3 South = 0, so y = 0. Net across: 4 + 6 = 10 East, so x = 10.<br />
    Straight-line distance = √(10² + 0²) = <strong>10 km</strong>, due East of the office.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — the trap</div>
    The North and South legs are both 3 km and cancel, which is easy to miss in the middle of four
    turns. Collapsing the up–down moves to zero before reaching for Pythagoras is what keeps the
    working short.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 4 — a genuine right triangle from a multi-leg walk (moderate)">
  <p>
    A ranger leaves camp and walks 10 m East, 4 m North, 2 m West, then 8 m North. How far is she from
    camp?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — net across and net up</div>
    Across: 10 East − 2 West = 8 East → x = 8.<br />
    Up: 4 North + 8 North = 12 North → y = 12.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — Pythagoras on the net gaps</div>
    Distance = √(8² + 12²)? Check for a triple: 8 and 12 are not a standard pair, so compute:
    √(64 + 144) = √208 ≈ 14.42 m. Because it is not a whole number, re-read the numbers — here they are
    as given, so the intended answer is √208 = 4√13 m, about <strong>14.42 m</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — the lesson</div>
    Not every walk lands on a clean triple. When the net gaps are 8 and 12, the honest answer is
    4√13, and an option offering a tidy "14" would be a rounding trap. Most Foundation questions are
    built to give whole numbers; when they do not, carry the surd rather than forcing a round figure.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 5 — degrees of turning (moderate)">
  <p>
    A man is facing South-West. He turns 90° clockwise, then 45° anticlockwise, then 180°. Which
    direction is he facing at the end?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — work in 45° steps around the compass</div>
    The eight points are 45° apart. Clockwise order: N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW, back to N.<br />
    Start: SW.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — apply the turns in order</div>
    90° clockwise from SW = two steps clockwise: SW → W → NW.<br />
    45° anticlockwise from NW = one step back: NW → W.<br />
    180° from W = four steps: W → NW → N → NE → E.<br />
    He faces <strong>East</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — the net-angle check</div>
    Total turn = +90 − 45 + 180 = +225° clockwise. From SW, 225° clockwise is 225 ÷ 45 = 5 steps:
    SW → W → NW → N → NE → E ✓ — the same answer by adding the angles first.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 6 — a shadow question (moderate)">
  <p>
    Two friends, Ravi and Sameer, stand facing each other one early morning shortly after sunrise.
    Ravi's shadow falls exactly to his left. (a) Which direction does Ravi face? (b) Which direction
    does Sameer face?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — fix the sun and the shadow</div>
    Early morning: the sun is in the East, so every shadow falls towards the <strong>West</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (a) turn the shadow into a facing for Ravi</div>
    The shadow is to Ravi's left, so his left hand points West. The left hand is 90° anticlockwise from
    the facing, so the facing is 90° clockwise from West: West → North.<br />
    Ravi faces <strong>North</strong>.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (b) Sameer faces the opposite way</div>
    They stand face to face, so Sameer faces the opposite of North, which is <strong>South</strong>.
    Check: for Sameer, facing South, the West-pointing shadow is on his right — consistent with two
    people facing each other having their hands swapped.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 7 — opposite directions and the gap between two people (moderate)">
  <p>
    From the same bus stop, Priya walks 9 m towards the North and Nikhil walks 12 m towards the West.
    (a) How far apart are they? (b) In which direction is Priya from Nikhil?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — place both from one origin</div>
    Bus stop at (0, 0). Priya: 9 North → (0, 9). Nikhil: 12 West → (−12, 0).
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (a) distance between the two points</div>
    Across gap = 0 − (−12) = 12; up gap = 9 − 0 = 9.<br />
    Distance = √(12² + 9²) = √(144 + 81) = √225 = <strong>15 m</strong> (the 9–12–15 triple).
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (b) direction of Priya from Nikhil</div>
    From Nikhil at (−12, 0) to Priya at (0, 9): 12 to the East and 9 to the North — up and to the
    right — so Priya is <strong>North-East</strong> of Nikhil.
  </div>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Worked example 8 — relative direction from a chain of clues (hard)">
  <p>
    A school is East of the library. The library is North of the market. The bank is West of the
    market. Where is the bank relative to the school?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — build a coordinate sketch from the clues</div>
    Put the market at (0, 0). Library is North of the market → library (0, 1).<br />
    School is East of the library → school (1, 1). Bank is West of the market → bank (−1, 0).
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — compare the two points</div>
    From the school (1, 1) to the bank (−1, 0): the bank is 2 to the West and 1 to the South — down and
    to the left.
  </div>

    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — name the direction</div>
    Down and to the left is the <strong>South-West</strong> quadrant, so the bank is
    <strong>South-West</strong> of the school. Because the two gaps are unequal (2 West, 1 South), it is
    "roughly South-West"; when a question wants only the eight-point answer, South-West is the choice.
  </div>

  <p>
    Facing East, a drone flies 7 m forward, turns left and flies 24 m, turns left and flies 7 m.
    (a) How far is it from the launch point? (b) Which direction does it now face, and (c) in which
    direction is the launch point from the drone?
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — fly it leg by leg</div>
    Facing East, forward 7 → (7, 0).<br />
    Turn left → from East, left is North → facing North, forward 24 → (7, 24).<br />
    Turn left → from North, left is West → facing West, forward 7 → (0, 24).
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — (a) distance from launch</div>
    Finish (0, 24): x = 0, y = 24. The two East–West legs of 7 cancel.<br />
    Distance = √(0² + 24²) = <strong>24 m</strong>, due North of the launch point.
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — (b) and (c)</div>
    (b) The last turn left the drone facing <strong>West</strong> — the heading, unrelated to where the
    launch point lies.<br />
    (c) The drone is 24 m North of the launch, so the launch is 24 m <strong>South</strong> of the
    drone. Facing West while the launch lies South is the whole point: heading and relative direction
    are two separate answers.
  </div>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes and examiner traps

- **Distance walked instead of displacement.** "How far from the start" wants the straight-line gap,
  √(x² + y²), not the sum of the legs. The sum is always offered as a distractor.
- **Not cancelling same-line moves.** 10 East then 4 West is a net 6 East, one leg, not two sides of a
  triangle. Collapse every North–South pair and every East–West pair before reaching for Pythagoras.
- **Reading left and right from the map, not the walker.** Left and right turn with the walker. A
  walker facing South who turns left turns towards the East.
- **Confusing heading with relative direction.** The way a walker faces at the finish and the
  direction of the start from the finish are two different questions. A walker can face West while the
  start lies due South.
- **Reversing a relative clue.** "P is East of Q" means Q is West of P. Flipping this the wrong way
  turns the whole sketch inside out.
- **Getting the opposite direction wrong.** The opposite of North-East is South-West, found by
  flipping both letters. NE → NW is a half-done flip and a common wrong option.
- **Subtracting for opposite-direction walkers.** Two people walking opposite ways from one point are
  apart by the sum of their distances, not the difference.
- **Putting the shadow towards the sun.** A shadow falls away from the sun: West in the morning, East
  in the evening. Placing it towards the sun reverses every following direction.
- **Trusting a midday shadow.** Near noon the sun is overhead and the shadow is too short to give a
  reliable direction; a well-set question expects "cannot be determined".
- **Forcing a round distance.** When the net gaps are not a Pythagorean pair, the answer is a surd
  such as 4√13. A suspiciously tidy option may be a rounding trap; most Foundation walks are built to
  land on a whole number, so an ugly figure is a cue to re-check the legs first.
- **Inventing a leg from a turn.** "Turns right" changes the heading only; it adds no distance.

## One-page revision summary

**The compass.** Four cardinals N, E, S, W, 90° apart; four ordinals NE, SE, SW, NW halfway between,
45° from each cardinal. Opposite = flip both letters (NE ↔ SW, NW ↔ SE). Facing North: right hand
East, left hand West. The right hand is always 90° clockwise from the facing.

**Draw everything.** North up, East right, South down, West left. Start at (0, 0). East +x, West −x,
North +y, South −y. The finish is a single point (x, y).

**Distance walked vs displacement.** Distance walked = sum of the legs. Displacement (shortest
distance from start) = √(x² + y²) by Pythagoras, where x is the net East–West gap and y the net
North–South gap. "How far from the start" always means displacement.

**Pythagorean triples to recognise:** 3-4-5, 6-8-10, 5-12-13, 9-12-15, 8-15-17, 7-24-25, 20-21-29,
12-35-37. Equal net gaps give a distance of (gap)√2 and a diagonal (ordinal) direction.

**Turns.** Right = 90° clockwise, left = 90° anticlockwise, measured from the current heading.
Clockwise chain N→E→S→W→N; anticlockwise N→W→S→E→N. Track heading and position separately. Add turns
as +90 (right) and −90 (left), reduce modulo 360. In degrees: 90° quarter, 180° opposite, 270°
clockwise = 90° anticlockwise, 360° full circle.

**Shadows.** Sun rises East, sets West. Shadow falls away from the sun: West in the morning, East in
the evening. Midday shadows are unreliable. From a shadow, fix the sun, place the shadow opposite,
then read the person's facing from which hand the shadow is on.

**Relative direction and opposites.** Sketch from clues on a grid; reverse a stated relation with
care. Opposite-direction walkers from one point are apart by the sum of their distances, and each is
in the opposite direction from the other.

**Marking.** 1 mark correct, −0.25 wrong, 0 unattempted. Answers here are checkable by redrawing and
distractors are built from named errors, so elimination is easy and most attempted questions are
worth attempting.
