Chandravali Foods Limited is registered on 12 May 2026. Its Board meets on 20 June and appoints a
chartered accountant as first auditor. Nobody notices that the appointee's brother is the company's
Chief Financial Officer, that the appointee already signs the audit reports of twenty-two operating
companies, and that no notice of the appointment ever reaches the Registrar. Three separate
provisions have been broken before the company has issued a single invoice — one about a **clock**,
one about a **disqualification**, and one about a **filing**.

That is the shape of this chapter. Sections 139 to 148 are not a discussion of audit technique; they
are a code about **who holds the office, for how long, who may take it away, what he must not be, what
he must report and what happens when he gets it wrong**. Marks come from four things, in this order:
the **appointing authority** (Board · members · Comptroller and Auditor-General · Central Government ·
Tribunal), the **number of days**, the **event the days run from**, and the **rupee threshold**. Learn
those and the descriptive questions write themselves.

## 1. Appointment at the first AGM — section 139(1)

**Section 139(1)** is the main rule and it has one shape worth memorising. Every company shall, **at
the first annual general meeting**, appoint an individual or a firm as auditor **to hold office from
the conclusion of that meeting till the conclusion of its sixth annual general meeting** and
thereafter **till the conclusion of every sixth meeting**.

<div class="formula">
<strong>The term is not "five years" loosely stated — it is five AGMs deep.</strong><br />
Appointed at the <strong>1st</strong> AGM → holds office till the conclusion of the <strong>6th</strong>
AGM. Five annual accounts are audited (FY1 to FY5); the office ends at the meeting that adopts the
fifth of them.
</div>

Three things must happen **before** the appointment, and one **after**:

| Step | Requirement | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| Before | **Written consent** of the auditor | obtained by the company |
| Before | A **certificate** that the appointment, if made, will be in accordance with the prescribed conditions, indicating whether the auditor satisfies the **s. 141** criteria | obtained by the company |
| Before | Where the company must have an **audit committee** (s. 177), the appointment is on the committee's **recommendation** | before the Board proposes it |
| After | The company **informs the auditor** of his appointment and **files notice with the Registrar** in the prescribed form (**ADT-1**) | **within 15 days of the meeting** |

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>There is no annual ratification any more.</strong> The proviso to s. 139(1) requiring the
    appointment to be <em>placed for ratification at every annual general meeting</em> was
    <strong>omitted by the Companies (Amendment) Act 2017</strong>. Once appointed for the five-AGM
    term, the auditor holds office for the whole term unless he resigns, is removed under s. 140, or
    incurs a disqualification under s. 141(4). Any option that says "subject to ratification by the
    members at each annual general meeting" is a <strong>pre-2018 answer</strong> and is wrong. Older
    coaching notes and older question banks still carry it.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Two clean-up rules sit at the end of the section.** Under **s. 139(9)** a retiring auditor may be
re-appointed at an AGM if he is not disqualified, has not given the company a notice of unwillingness,
and no special resolution has been passed appointing somebody else or providing expressly that he
shall not be re-appointed. Under **s. 139(10)**, where at any AGM **no auditor is appointed or
re-appointed**, the **existing auditor continues** in office. The office does not fall vacant merely
because the members forget to fill it.

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 1 — the ADT-1 clock, and what it is not">
  <p>
    Chandravali Foods Limited holds its first annual general meeting on <strong>25 August 2026</strong>
    and appoints M/s Kalyani &amp; Associates as auditors. The engagement letter is signed on 2
    September; the audit committee had recommended the firm on 30 July.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The notice to the Registrar runs from the meeting</strong> — 15 days from 25 August 2026,
    so by <strong>9 September 2026</strong>. Not from the engagement letter, not from the audit
    committee's recommendation, and not from the date the auditor's written consent was obtained. The
    <em>consent and the eligibility certificate</em> had to exist <em>before</em> the appointment; the
    <em>filing</em> is a consequence of it. The term runs from the conclusion of the 2026 meeting to
    the conclusion of the annual general meeting held in <strong>2031</strong> — the sixth meeting,
    counting the 2026 meeting as the first.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. The first auditor and casual vacancies — sections 139(5) to 139(8)

Four different clocks live here and the examiner mixes them deliberately. The distinguishing question
is always the same: **is this a government company, and is this a first appointment or a vacancy?**

**Section 139(5) — the C&amp;AG's companies.** In the case of a **government company**, or any other
company **owned or controlled, directly or indirectly, by the Central Government, by any State
Government or Governments, or partly by the Central Government and partly by one or more State
Governments**, the **Comptroller and Auditor-General of India** appoints the auditor **within 180 days
from the commencement of the financial year**, and that auditor holds office **till the conclusion of
the annual general meeting**.

**The appointment clocks, side by side:**

| Situation | Who appoints | Period | Runs from | If he fails |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **First auditor**, company other than a government company (**s. 139(6)**) | **Board** | **30 days** | **date of registration** | Board informs the members, who appoint at an **EGM within 90 days**; auditor holds office till the conclusion of the **first AGM** |
| **First auditor**, government company (**s. 139(7)**) | **C&amp;AG** | **60 days** | **date of registration** | **Board** within the **next 30 days**; on the Board's failure, members at an **EGM within 60 days**; holds office till the **first AGM** |
| **Subsequent auditor**, government company (**s. 139(5)**) | **C&amp;AG** | **180 days** | **commencement of the financial year** | — |
| **Casual vacancy**, non-C&amp;AG company (**s. 139(8)(i)**) | **Board** | **30 days** | the vacancy | if the vacancy arose by **resignation**, the appointment must also be **approved by the members in general meeting convened within 3 months of the Board's recommendation** |
| **Casual vacancy**, C&amp;AG company (**s. 139(8)(ii)**) | **C&amp;AG** | **30 days** | the vacancy | **Board** within the **next 30 days** |

<div class="formula">
<strong>Memorise the pair, not the numbers.</strong><br />
Non-government first auditor: <strong>30 → 90</strong> (Board, then members).<br />
Government first auditor: <strong>60 → 30 → 60</strong> (C&amp;AG, then Board, then members).<br />
Government subsequent auditor: <strong>180 days from 1 April</strong>.<br />
Casual vacancy: <strong>30 days</strong> in both cases; the government company gets a further
<strong>30</strong> for the Board.
</div>

A **casual vacancy** is a vacancy arising otherwise than by efflux of time — death, resignation,
disqualification incurred after appointment (**s. 141(4)** deems that vacation to be a casual vacancy),
or a Tribunal order under **s. 140(5)** (Explanation I to that sub-section deems it a casual vacancy
too). A vacancy arising because the term simply ran out is **not** casual; it is filled by an ordinary
appointment under s. 139(1). An auditor appointed to fill a casual vacancy holds office only **till
the conclusion of the next annual general meeting**.

<div class="working-note">
<strong>Working note — a first-auditor clock, dated.</strong><br />
Chandravali Foods Limited is registered on <strong>12 May 2026</strong>.<br />
Board's window under s. 139(6): 30 days from the date of <em>registration</em> → 12 May + 30 days =
<strong>11 June 2026</strong>. The Board's actual meeting on 20 June is <em>nine days late</em>.<br />
Had the Board simply failed and informed the members on, say, <strong>20 June 2026</strong>, the
members' extraordinary general meeting would have to appoint within 90 days of that information →
<strong>18 September 2026</strong>.<br />
Note the trap: the 90 days is a <em>fresh</em> window for the members, not the balance of the
Board's 30 days, and the first auditor so appointed still holds office only till the conclusion of the
first AGM.
</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>Only the resignation-caused casual vacancy needs members' approval.</strong> Section
    139(8)(i) gives the Board 30 days in every case. The <em>additional</em> requirement — a general
    meeting within <strong>three months of the Board's recommendation</strong> — attaches only where
    the vacancy is <strong>a result of the resignation of an auditor</strong>. A vacancy caused by
    death or by a disqualification incurred later is filled by the Board alone. And note where the
    three months are counted from: the <strong>Board's recommendation</strong>, not the date of
    resignation and not the date the Board filled the vacancy.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. Rotation of auditors — section 139(2) to 139(4)

**Section 139(2)** bars a **listed company** and companies of the **prescribed classes** from
appointing or re-appointing:

- **an individual** as auditor for **more than one term of five consecutive years**; and
- **an audit firm** as auditor for **more than two terms of five consecutive years**.

| Auditor | Maximum tenure | Cooling-off before re-appointment **in the same company** |
|---|---|---|
| **Individual** | **one term** of 5 consecutive years | **5 years** from the completion of his term |
| **Audit firm** (including an LLP) | **two terms** of 5 consecutive years — **10 years** | **5 years** from the completion of the two terms |

<div class="formula">
<strong>5 + 5 + 5.</strong> An individual: 5 on, 5 off. A firm: 5 + 5 on, then 5 off. The cooling-off
period is <strong>five years for both</strong> — it is the <em>tenure</em> that differs, not the
cooling-off.
</div>

**Three anti-avoidance rules stop the obvious dodges:**

1. **Common partner.** As on the date of appointment, **no audit firm having a common partner or
   partners** with an outgoing audit firm **whose tenure has expired** in that company in the
   immediately preceding financial year **shall be appointed** as auditor of the same company for
   **five years**. Re-badging the same partners under a new firm name does not restart the clock.
2. **The network rule.** Where the outgoing auditor and the proposed incoming auditor belong to the
   **same network of audit firms** — firms operating or marketed under a **common brand name** or
   under **common control** — the incoming firm is caught by the same bar. So is the firm that a
   **retiring partner in charge of the audit**, who also certified the financial statements, joins
   after leaving the outgoing firm.
3. **Joint auditors.** Where two or more persons are appointed as **joint auditors**, the rotation is
   applied so that **all of them do not complete their terms in the same year**. The point of
   rotation is continuity of knowledge alongside change of eye; retiring both at once defeats it.

**Section 139(3)** lets the **members** go further than the Act requires, by resolving that (a) in the
audit firm appointed, the **auditing partner and his team shall be rotated** at such intervals as the
members resolve, or (b) the **audit shall be conducted by more than one auditor**. This is an internal
tightening, resolved by the members — not something the Board may impose and not something the
Registrar directs. **Section 139(4)** empowers the **Central Government** to prescribe the manner of
rotation.

**The transition.** Every company existing on or before the commencement of the Act and required to
comply with s. 139(2) was given **three years from the date of commencement** to comply. The window
is long closed, but it remains a favourite one-mark point.

<Callout kind="amendment" label="AMENDMENT-CHECK · the classes are rules-level, not Act-level">
  <p>
    Section 139(2) itself names only <strong>listed companies</strong> and "such class or classes of
    companies as may be prescribed". The prescribed classes sit in <strong>Rule 5, Companies (Audit
    and Auditors) Rules 2014</strong> and are the numbers examiners test: all <strong>unlisted public
    companies</strong> having paid-up share capital of <strong>₹10 crore or more</strong>; all
    <strong>private limited companies</strong> having paid-up share capital of <strong>₹50 crore or
    more</strong>; and <strong>all companies</strong> having <strong>public borrowings from financial
    institutions, banks or public deposits of ₹50 crore or more</strong>. One-person companies and
    small companies are outside the rule. These thresholds have been revised before and are
    <strong>rules-level</strong>: state them as "prescribed classes, currently ₹10 crore / ₹50 crore /
    ₹50 crore" and re-verify for your attempt.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 2 — counting a rotation term and its cooling-off">
  <p>
    Nairit Alloys Limited is a listed company. M/s Deshmukh &amp; Co, an audit firm, was appointed at
    the annual general meeting held in <strong>2016</strong> for a term of five years and re-appointed
    at the meeting held in <strong>2021</strong> for a second term of five years. Two of its partners
    then float a new firm, M/s Deshmukh Vaidya LLP, which offers itself for appointment at the
    <strong>2026</strong> meeting.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>1 · The tenure is exhausted.</strong> Two terms of five consecutive years is the ceiling
    for a firm; Deshmukh &amp; Co completes it at the conclusion of the 2026 meeting.
    <strong>2 · The cooling-off.</strong> The firm is ineligible for re-appointment in Nairit Alloys
    for <strong>five years</strong> from the completion of the two terms — so the earliest annual
    general meeting at which it may be re-appointed is the one held in <strong>2031</strong>.
    <strong>3 · The new firm is caught too.</strong> M/s Deshmukh Vaidya LLP has partners in common
    with the outgoing firm whose tenure expired in the immediately preceding financial year, so the
    common-partner bar keeps it out for five years as well; the network rule would reach it
    independently if the two firms share a brand or common control. <strong>4 · What would not
    help.</strong> A members' resolution cannot waive s. 139(2); s. 139(3) lets members make rotation
    <em>stricter</em>, never looser.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Removal, resignation and the special-notice route — section 140

Four different ways an auditor may leave office before his term ends. They have different movers and
different approvers, and confusing them is the single most common error in this chapter.

| Route | Who initiates | What is needed | Provision |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Removal before expiry of term** | the **company** | **Board resolution** → **previous approval of the Central Government** (application in **ADT-2 within 30 days** of the Board resolution) → **special resolution within 60 days of receipt of the approval** → the auditor **given a reasonable opportunity of being heard** | **s. 140(1)** |
| **Resignation** | the **auditor** | statement in **ADT-3 filed within 30 days** of the date of resignation with the **company and the Registrar**, and additionally with the **C&amp;AG** for a government company, giving the reasons and relevant facts | **s. 140(2)–(3)** |
| **Not re-appointing the retiring auditor at the AGM** | a **member** | **special notice** of the resolution; the retiring auditor's right to representation | **s. 140(4)** |
| **Change directed on fraud** | **Tribunal**, suo motu or on application by the Central Government or any person concerned | direction to the company to change its auditors | **s. 140(5)** |

<div class="formula">
<strong>Removal under s. 140(1) — the three-step chain, in order.</strong><br />
Board resolution → <strong>ADT-2 to the Central Government within 30 days</strong> of it →
approval → <strong>special resolution within 60 days</strong> of receiving the approval.<br />
Miss the order and the removal fails: the special resolution <em>follows</em> the Central Government's
approval; it never precedes it.
</div>

**Section 140(2) and (3) — resignation.** The auditor who has resigned **shall** file the statement
within **30 days from the date of resignation**. This is the auditor's own statutory duty, not the
company's, and it is one of the few places in the chapter where the Act penalises the auditor for a
purely procedural default: failure attracts a **penalty of ₹50,000 or an amount equal to his
remuneration, whichever is less**, with a **further penalty for each day of continuing failure**,
subject to a prescribed maximum.

**Section 140(4) — the special-notice regime.** Where it is proposed at an annual general meeting to
appoint **a person other than the retiring auditor**, or to provide expressly that the **retiring
auditor shall not be re-appointed**, a **special notice** is required. On receiving it the company
**forthwith sends a copy to the retiring auditor**, who may make a **written representation of
reasonable length** and ask that it be notified to the members. The company must then state the fact
of the representation in the notice of the meeting and send a copy to every member; if it is received
too late or the company defaults, the auditor may require it to be **read out at the meeting**, and a
copy is filed with the Registrar. Where the **Tribunal** is satisfied that the right is being **abused
to secure needless publicity for defamatory matter**, it may order that the representation need not be
sent or read out.

Two carve-outs are worth a mark each. The special notice is **not** required where the retiring auditor
has **completed his consecutive tenure of five or ten years** under s. 139(2) — the rotation rule
already ends his office, so no member need move anything. And the section is about the **procedure at
the meeting**; it is not a removal under s. 140(1) and needs **no Central Government approval**.

**Section 140(5) — the fraud route.** The **Tribunal**, either **suo motu** or on an application by the
**Central Government** or by **any person concerned**, may direct the company to **change its
auditors** if satisfied that the auditor has, **directly or indirectly, acted in a fraudulent manner or
abetted or colluded in any fraud** by or in relation to the company or its directors or officers. Where
the **Central Government** applies and the Tribunal is satisfied, it may **within 15 days of receipt of
the application** order that the auditor shall not function as auditor, and the Central Government may
appoint another auditor in his place. The vacancy so created is **deemed a casual vacancy**. Where a
firm is involved, the liability is that of the **firm and of the partner or partners concerned who
acted in the fraudulent manner**. And the sting: an auditor against whom a **final order** is passed is
**not eligible to be appointed as auditor of any company for five years** from the date of the order,
and is **also liable under s. 447**.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Keep the five-year debarments apart.</strong> The <strong>s. 139(2)</strong> cooling-off is
    five years <em>in the same company</em> and is a consequence of nothing worse than completing a
    term. The <strong>s. 140(5)</strong> debarment is five years from being appointed auditor of
    <strong>any company at all</strong>, and follows a finding of fraud. An option that applies the
    rotation cooling-off across all companies, or the fraud debarment to one company only, is
    inverting the two.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 3 — a removal, dated, with the trap in the middle">
  <p>
    The Board of Suvarna Ceramics Limited resolves on <strong>18 May 2026</strong> to remove its
    auditor, whose five-AGM term still has two years to run. The company applies to the Central
    Government and the approval is <strong>received on 8 June 2026</strong>. The company convenes a
    general meeting for 30 September 2026 and passes an ordinary resolution removing the auditor. The
    auditor is not called.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>1 · The application.</strong> ADT-2 had to be filed within <strong>30 days of the Board
    resolution</strong> — by 17 June 2026. That was met. <strong>2 · The resolution clock.</strong>
    The special resolution had to be passed <strong>within 60 days of receipt of the approval</strong>
    — 8 June + 60 days = <strong>7 August 2026</strong>. The 30 September meeting is out of time.
    <strong>3 · The wrong resolution.</strong> An <em>ordinary</em> resolution cannot remove an auditor
    under s. 140(1); a <strong>special</strong> resolution is mandatory. <strong>4 · The hearing.</strong>
    Not giving the auditor a reasonable opportunity of being heard is an independent breach of the
    proviso to s. 140(1). <strong>5 · Result.</strong> The removal is ineffective on three separate
    grounds; the auditor remains in office.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Eligibility, qualifications and disqualifications — section 141

**Section 141(1)** — a person is eligible for appointment as auditor of a company **only if he is a
chartered accountant**. A **firm** may be appointed **by its firm name** where **a majority of its
partners practising in India** are qualified for appointment. **Section 141(2)** — where a firm
**including a limited liability partnership** is appointed as auditor, **only the partners who are
chartered accountants** are authorised to **act and sign** on behalf of the firm.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>The LLP point, stated precisely.</strong> A <strong>body corporate cannot be an
    auditor</strong> under s. 141(3)(a) — <em>except</em> a limited liability partnership registered
    under the LLP Act 2008. So an LLP <strong>may</strong> be appointed. But s. 141(2) then bites: only
    those partners of the LLP who are <strong>chartered accountants</strong> may act and sign. And
    s. 141(1) still applies to the appointment itself: a <strong>majority of the partners practising
    in India</strong> must be chartered accountants. Three different tests, often compressed into a
    single wrong option. A company, a private limited company or an "audit company" is
    <strong>never</strong> eligible.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 141(3) — the disqualifications.** These are the highest-yield lines in the chapter, because
each one carries a testable boundary.

| Clause | Disqualified person | Boundary that decides the question |
|---|---|---|
| **(a)** | a **body corporate** | **except an LLP** registered under the LLP Act 2008 |
| **(b)** | an **officer or employee** of the company | includes a director; "officer" is defined in s. 2(59) |
| **(c)** | a **partner of, or a person in the employment of**, an officer or employee of the company | reaches one step beyond (b) |
| **(d)(i)** | a person, **his relative or partner**, holding any **security of or interest in** the company, its subsidiary, its holding or associate company, or a subsidiary of such holding company | a **relative** may hold securities of **face value not exceeding ₹1,00,000**; if a relative **acquires** securities above that, **corrective action within 60 days** saves the appointment. The **auditor himself and his partner** get **no** relief — not even one share |
| **(d)(ii)** | a person, his relative or partner **indebted** to any of those companies | **in excess of ₹5,00,000** |
| **(d)(iii)** | a person, his relative or partner who has given a **guarantee or provided security** in connection with the **indebtedness of a third person** to any of those companies | **exceeding ₹1,00,000** |
| **(e)** | a person or firm having, **directly or indirectly**, a **business relationship** with those companies | **excludes** professional services permitted under the Companies Act or the Chartered Accountants Act, and **commercial transactions in the ordinary course of business at arm's length price** (a hotel room, an airline ticket, a telecom connection) |
| **(f)** | a person whose **relative is a director**, or is in the employment of the company as a **director or key managerial personnel** | the relative's role, not the auditor's |
| **(g)** | a person in **full-time employment elsewhere**; or a person, or a partner of a firm, already holding appointment as auditor of **more than 20 companies** | the **counting exclusions** are the examined part — see below |
| **(h)** | a person **convicted of an offence involving fraud**, where **10 years** have not elapsed from the conviction | ten years, from the date of conviction |
| **(i)** | a person whose **subsidiary, associate company or other entity** is engaged, as on the date of appointment, in the **consulting and specialised services under s. 144** | links s. 141 to s. 144 |

<div class="formula">
<strong>The three money lines, in ascending order.</strong><br />
Relative's securities: face value up to <strong>₹1,00,000</strong> permitted (60-day cure).<br />
Guarantee or security for a third person's debt: up to <strong>₹1,00,000</strong> permitted.<br />
Indebtedness: up to <strong>₹5,00,000</strong> permitted.<br />
All three are <em>rules-level</em> figures under Rule 10 and all three are tested by giving you a
number just above or just below the line.
</div>

<Callout kind="amendment" label="AMENDMENT-CHECK · the 20-audit ceiling and what is excluded from it">
  <p>
    Section 141(3)(g) caps a person — and each <strong>partner</strong> of a firm — at
    <strong>twenty companies</strong>. What is examined is not the twenty but the
    <strong>exclusions</strong>, which sit in the rules and were widened by amendment: in reckoning the
    limit, exclude <strong>one-person companies</strong>, <strong>dormant companies</strong>,
    <strong>small companies</strong>, and <strong>private companies having paid-up share capital of
    less than ₹100 crore</strong>. The ₹100 crore figure replaced an earlier ₹100 crore-free position
    and is <strong>rules-level</strong>. Note also the arithmetic of a firm: the ceiling attaches to
    <strong>each partner</strong>, so a five-partner firm may hold up to 100 audits in total, and
    audits signed on behalf of the firm count against the signing partner.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 141(4)** closes the loop: where a person appointed as auditor **incurs any of these
disqualifications after his appointment**, he **shall vacate his office**, and that vacation is
**deemed to be a casual vacancy**. He does not wait to be removed and the company does not need
Central Government approval; the office simply falls vacant and is filled under s. 139(8).

<div class="working-note">
<strong>Working note — testing three thresholds on one set of facts.</strong><br />
CA Meghna is offered the audit of Tarangini Structures Limited. Facts:<br />
(i) her <strong>brother</strong> holds 1,200 equity shares of face value ₹100 each in the company =
<strong>₹1,20,000</strong> face value. Limit for a relative is ₹1,00,000, so the holding is
<strong>₹20,000 over</strong> → at least <strong>200 shares</strong> must go before the appointment,
or corrective action must be taken within <strong>60 days</strong> of the relative acquiring them.<br />
(ii) she owes the company's <strong>subsidiary</strong> ₹4,80,000. The ₹5,00,000 line applies to the
company, its subsidiary, its holding and associate companies alike, so she is inside the line — but any
further borrowing above <strong>₹20,000</strong> from that group disqualifies her.<br />
(iii) she has guaranteed a supplier's ₹90,000 debt to the company. ₹90,000 does not exceed ₹1,00,000,
so clause (d)(iii) is not attracted.<br />
<strong>Conclusion:</strong> only the relative's shareholding stands in the way, and it is curable.
</div>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 4 — counting the twenty">
  <p>
    CA Rajan currently signs the audit reports of: 9 unlisted public companies; 4 listed companies;
    3 one-person companies; 2 dormant companies; 5 small companies; and 6 private companies, of which
    <strong>one</strong> has a paid-up share capital of ₹140 crore and the rest have paid-up capital
    below ₹100 crore. He is offered three more public-company audits.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>Count only what the rules count.</strong> Public unlisted 9 + listed 4 = 13. One-person,
    dormant and small companies are excluded outright — 3, 2 and 5 fall away. Of the six private
    companies, five have paid-up capital below ₹100 crore and are excluded; the ₹140 crore private
    company <strong>counts</strong>. Total counted = <strong>14</strong>. The ceiling is 20, so he may
    accept <strong>6</strong> more — the three offered are safely within the limit.
    <strong>The variation:</strong> had he been a partner in a firm, the twenty would still be
    <em>his</em> ceiling as a partner; the firm's aggregate is the sum of its partners' ceilings, not a
    separate cap of twenty.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. Remuneration of auditors — section 142

**Section 142(1)** — the remuneration of the auditor **shall be fixed in the general meeting** or in
such manner as may be **determined therein**. The **proviso** is the exception the examiner wants: the
**Board may fix the remuneration of the first auditor**.

**Section 142(2)** defines what remuneration includes: in addition to the **fee payable**, it includes
the **expenses incurred by the auditor in connection with the audit** and any **facility extended to
him** — but it **does not include** any remuneration paid to him for **any other service rendered at
the company's request**.

<div class="formula">
<strong>Fee + audit expenses + facilities = remuneration.</strong> Payment for other services (those
s. 144 permits) is <strong>outside</strong> it — which matters because s. 147(2) measures the auditor's
fine as a multiple of "the remuneration of the auditor".
</div>

## 7. Powers and duties of auditors — section 143

**Section 143(1) — the right of access.** Every auditor has a **right of access at all times to the
books of account and vouchers** of the company, **whether kept at the registered office or at any other
place**, and is entitled to **require from the officers** of the company such **information and
explanation** as he may consider necessary. The **proviso** extends the right: the auditor of a
**holding company** also has access to the **records of all its subsidiaries and associate companies**,
so far as it relates to the **consolidation of financial statements**. Access is a **right**, not a
concession the Board may qualify, and it is not confined to the registered office.

**The six inquiry matters in s. 143(1)** must be *inquired into*, and are reported only if the answer
is unsatisfactory. Learn them as a list — they are a standing 4-mark question:

1. whether **loans and advances made on the basis of security** have been **properly secured**, and
   whether the terms are **prejudicial** to the interests of the company or its members;
2. whether **transactions represented merely by book entries** are prejudicial to the company's
   interests;
3. where the company is **not an investment company or a banking company**, whether so much of its
   assets as consist of **shares, debentures and other securities** have been **sold at a price less
   than that at which they were purchased**;
4. whether **loans and advances made** by the company have been shown as **deposits**;
5. whether **personal expenses** have been charged to **revenue account**; and
6. where it is stated in the books that **shares have been allotted for cash**, whether **cash has
   actually been received**, and if not, whether the position as stated in the books and the balance
   sheet is **correct, regular and not misleading**.

**Section 143(2) — the report to the members.** The auditor makes his report **to the members** — not
to the Board, not to the management — on the accounts examined by him and on every financial statement
required to be laid before the company in general meeting. He reports after taking into account the
provisions of the Act, the **accounting and auditing standards** and the prescribed matters, and states
whether, to the best of his information and knowledge, the accounts give a **true and fair view** of
the state of affairs at the end of the financial year, of the profit or loss and of the cash flows.

**Section 143(3) — what the report must state.** Ten clauses, each independently examinable:

| Clause | The report shall state whether |
|---|---|
| (a) | he has **sought and obtained** all information and explanations necessary |
| (b) | **proper books of account** as required by law have been kept, and proper **returns adequate for the audit** received from branches not visited by him |
| (c) | the **branch auditor's report** under s. 143(8) has been **sent to him** and how he has dealt with it |
| (d) | the **balance sheet and profit and loss account** dealt with in the report are **in agreement with the books and returns** |
| (e) | the financial statements **comply with the accounting standards** |
| (f) | his **observations or comments** on financial transactions or matters having an **adverse effect on the functioning** of the company |
| (g) | any **director is disqualified** from being appointed as a director under **s. 164(2)** |
| (h) | there is any **qualification, reservation or adverse remark** relating to the maintenance of accounts and other connected matters |
| (i) | the company has **adequate internal financial controls with reference to financial statements** in place and their **operating effectiveness** |
| (j) | such **other matters as may be prescribed** — **Rule 11** |

**Section 143(4)** — where any of these is answered **in the negative or with a qualification**, the
report must **state the reasons**. A qualified opinion without reasons is itself a default.

**Going concern** is not a separate clause of s. 143(3); it enters the report through the **auditing
standards** the auditor must comply with under s. 143(9), through the **true and fair view** in
s. 143(2), and through clause (f) where it has an adverse effect on the functioning of the company.
Say it that way in an answer rather than inventing a sub-clause.

**Sections 143(5) to (7) — the government-company overlay.** Where the C&amp;AG appoints the auditor,
three additional powers follow:

- **s. 143(5)** — the **C&amp;AG directs the manner** in which the accounts are to be audited, and the
  auditor submits a **copy of the audit report to the C&amp;AG**, including the **directions issued**,
  the **action taken** on them and their **impact** on the accounts and financial statement.
- **s. 143(6)** — the C&amp;AG has, **within 60 days from the date of receipt of the audit report**,
  the right to **conduct a supplementary audit** and to **comment upon or supplement** the audit
  report. Any such comments are **sent to the company** and **placed before the annual general meeting
  at the same time and in the same manner as the audit report**.
- **s. 143(7)** — the C&amp;AG may, **by order**, cause a **test audit** of the accounts to be
  conducted.

**Section 143(8) — branch audit.** The accounts of a **branch office** are audited either by the
**company's auditor** or by **another person qualified for appointment as auditor** of the company and
appointed as such **under s. 139**; where the branch is **outside India**, by the company's auditor, or
by an accountant, or by a person **duly qualified to act as an auditor under the laws of that
country**. The **branch auditor prepares a report and sends it to the company's auditor**, who **deals
with it in his own report** in such manner as he considers necessary. The company's auditor does not
audit the branch again; he is **not relieved of responsibility for his own report** either.

**Sections 143(9) and (10) — auditing standards.** Every auditor **shall comply with the auditing
standards**. The **Central Government** prescribes them, as **recommended by the Institute of Chartered
Accountants of India**, in **consultation with and after examination of the recommendations of the
National Financial Reporting Authority**. Until standards are so notified, the **standards of auditing
specified by the ICAI are deemed to be the auditing standards**.

## 8. Reporting of fraud — section 143(12) to 143(15)

**Section 143(12)** splits on a single number. Where the auditor, **in the course of the performance of
his duties as auditor**, has **reason to believe** that an **offence of fraud** involving the prescribed
amount **is being or has been committed** in the company **by its officers or employees**, he reports it
to the **Central Government**. Below that amount, he reports to the **audit committee** under s. 177, or
to the **Board** where there is no audit committee.

| Fraud | Report to | First step | Then |
|---|---|---|---|
| **₹1 crore or more** (individual fraud) | **Central Government** | report to the **Board or audit committee** immediately and **not later than 2 days** of knowledge, **seeking their reply within 45 days** | on receipt of the reply, forward the **report, the reply and his comments** to the Central Government **within 15 days of receipt of the reply**; if **no reply** is received within the 45 days, forward the report **with a note** that no reply was received, within **15 days of the expiry** of the 45 days — in **Form ADT-4**, to the Secretary, Ministry of Corporate Affairs |
| **Less than ₹1 crore** | **audit committee**, or the **Board** where there is none | report immediately and **not later than 2 days** of knowledge, stating the **nature of the fraud with a description, the approximate amount involved and the parties involved** | the **Board's report** must disclose the nature of the fraud with a description, the approximate amount, the parties involved **if remedial action is not taken**, and the **remedial action taken** |

<div class="formula">
<strong>The s. 143(12) clock: 2 → 45 → 15.</strong><br />
2 days to the Board or audit committee · 45 days for their reply · 15 days from the reply
<strong>or from the expiry of the 45 days</strong> to reach the Central Government.<br />
Longest possible route = <strong>2 + 45 + 15 = 62 days</strong> from the date of knowledge.
</div>

<div class="working-note">
<strong>Working note — the fraud clock, dated.</strong><br />
The auditor of Hemadri Polymers Limited forms the reason to believe on <strong>4 May 2026</strong> that
officers have committed a fraud of <strong>₹1.6 crore</strong>.<br />
Step 1 — report to the Board or audit committee within 2 days: by <strong>6 May 2026</strong>.<br />
Step 2 — reply sought within 45 days of that report: 6 May + 45 days = <strong>20 June 2026</strong>.<br />
Step 3 — no reply is received. Forward to the Central Government within 15 days of the expiry:
20 June + 15 days = <strong>5 July 2026</strong>.<br />
If the Board <em>had</em> replied on, say, 2 June, the 15 days would run from <strong>that</strong>
receipt (→ 17 June), not from the expiry of the 45 days. The clock always runs from whichever of the
two events happens.
</div>

**Section 143(13)** protects the auditor: **no duty to which he is subject is regarded as contravened**
by reason of his reporting under s. 143(12), **if the reporting is done in good faith**. Confidentiality
is no defence to silence and no exposure for speaking.

**Section 143(14)** extends the whole of s. 143(12) **mutatis mutandis** to the **cost accountant
conducting a cost audit under s. 148** and the **company secretary in practice conducting a secretarial
audit under s. 204**. **Section 143(15)** penalises non-compliance with s. 143(12): a **penalty of
₹5,00,000 for a listed company** and **₹1,00,000 for any other company**, on the auditor, cost
accountant or company secretary in practice who failed to report.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Three traps in one sub-section.</strong> First, the ₹1 crore test is applied to the
    <strong>individual fraud</strong>, not to the aggregate of several unrelated frauds. Second, the
    auditor <strong>never reports directly to the Central Government first</strong> — even for a
    ₹50 crore fraud the first step is the Board or audit committee within two days. Third, s. 143(12)
    catches fraud <strong>by officers or employees of the company</strong>; a fraud committed on the
    company by an outsider is not within it, though it may well affect the true and fair view and be
    reportable under s. 143(2) and (3)(f).
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 5 — two frauds, two routes">
  <p>
    During the audit of Hemadri Polymers Limited (unlisted, with an audit committee) the auditor finds
    two things: a purchase manager has diverted <strong>₹78 lakh</strong> through fictitious vendors,
    and, separately, the finance head has routed <strong>₹1.35 crore</strong> through a related shell.
    The company's cost auditor independently notices the second.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>₹78 lakh — below the line.</strong> Report to the <strong>audit committee within 2
    days</strong>, describing the fraud, the approximate amount and the parties. Nothing goes to the
    Central Government; instead the <strong>Board's report</strong> must disclose it, including the
    parties if remedial action is not taken. <strong>₹1.35 crore — above the line.</strong> Report to
    the audit committee within 2 days seeking a reply in 45 days, then to the <strong>Central
    Government</strong> in Form ADT-4 within 15 days of the reply or of the expiry of the 45 days.
    <strong>The cost auditor.</strong> Section 143(14) makes the same obligation his, independently —
    he cannot rely on the statutory auditor having reported. <strong>Failure.</strong> Each of them
    faces a penalty of <strong>₹1,00,000</strong> under s. 143(15), the company being unlisted; it
    would be ₹5,00,000 for a listed company.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Services an auditor must not render — section 144

An auditor may render **only such other services as are approved by the Board or the audit committee**,
and **never** the following — **whether rendered directly or indirectly**, and whether to the
**company, its holding company or its subsidiary company**:

1. **accounting and book keeping** services;
2. **internal audit**;
3. **design and implementation of any financial information system**;
4. **actuarial services**;
5. **investment advisory services**;
6. **investment banking services**;
7. **rendering of outsourced financial services**; and
8. **management services**;

together with **any other kind of services as may be prescribed** — the residuary limb.

**"Directly or indirectly" is defined**, and the definition is what makes this section hard to dodge.
For an **individual** auditor it covers services rendered **himself**, or **through his relative**, or
through **any other person connected or associated with him**, or through **any entity in which he has
significant influence or control**, or **whose name, trade mark or brand he uses**. For a **firm** it
covers services rendered **by the firm itself**, or **through any of its partners**, or through its
**parent, subsidiary or associate entity**, or through **any entity in which the firm or any partner
has significant influence or control**, or **whose name, trade mark or brand is used** by the firm or
any partner.

**The transition proviso** required an auditor or audit firm performing any of these non-audit services
on or before the commencement of the Act to comply **before the closure of the first financial year
after the commencement**.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>Section 144 is wired into two other sections.</strong> Under <strong>s. 141(3)(i)</strong> a
    person whose <strong>subsidiary, associate company or other entity</strong> is engaged in these
    services as on the date of appointment is <strong>disqualified from appointment</strong> — so the
    conflict is tested at the door, not only during the term. And under <strong>s. 147(2)</strong>
    contravention of s. 144 is one of the four sections (139, 143, 144, 145) that expose the auditor
    personally to a fine. Taxation services, and certification work permitted under the Chartered
    Accountants Act, are <strong>not</strong> in the prohibited list — but they still need Board or
    audit committee approval.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. Signing, reading and attendance — sections 145 and 146

**Section 145** — the person appointed as auditor **signs the auditor's report**, or signs or certifies
any other document of the company, **in accordance with s. 141(2)** (in a firm, only the chartered
accountant partners). The **qualifications, observations or comments on financial transactions or
matters which have any adverse effect on the functioning of the company** must be **read before the
company in general meeting** and are **open to inspection by any member**. The unqualified body of the
report is not read out; the **adverse matter** is.

**Section 146** — **all notices of, and other communications relating to, any general meeting** must be
**forwarded to the auditor**. He **shall**, unless **otherwise exempted by the company**, **attend**
the meeting **either himself or through an authorised representative who is himself qualified to be an
auditor**, and has the **right to be heard** on any part of the business **which concerns him as
auditor**.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Attendance is a duty as well as a right, and the exemption comes from the company.</strong>
    Students routinely say the auditor "may attend if he wishes". The section says he
    <strong>shall</strong> attend unless the <strong>company</strong> exempts him. Equally, his right
    to be heard is confined to <strong>business which concerns him as the auditor</strong> — he has no
    right to speak on a dividend proposal or a director's re-appointment as such. And the representative
    who attends for him must himself be <strong>qualified to be an auditor</strong>; a clerk or an
    articled assistant will not do.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 11. Punishment for contravention — section 147

| Who | Contravention | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **The company** | any of **ss. 139 to 146** | **fine of ₹25,000 to ₹5,00,000** |
| **Every officer in default** | any of **ss. 139 to 146** | **fine of ₹10,000 to ₹1,00,000** |
| **The auditor** | **s. 139, s. 143, s. 144 or s. 145** | **fine of ₹25,000 to ₹5,00,000, or four times his remuneration, whichever is less** |
| **The auditor, acting knowingly or wilfully with intent to deceive** the company, its shareholders, creditors or tax authorities | same sections | **imprisonment up to 1 year** **and** **fine of ₹50,000 to ₹25,00,000, or eight times his remuneration, whichever is less** |

<div class="formula">
<strong>The two limbs of s. 147(2) are not alternatives you choose — they are triggered by
intent.</strong><br />
Ordinary contravention → <strong>fine only</strong>, capped at the <strong>lower</strong> of ₹5,00,000
and <strong>4×</strong> remuneration.<br />
Knowing or wilful, with intent to deceive → <strong>imprisonment up to 1 year <em>and</em>
fine</strong>, capped at the lower of ₹25,00,000 and <strong>8×</strong> remuneration.
</div>

**Section 147(3) — what follows a conviction under s. 147(2).** The auditor **shall**:

- **refund the remuneration** received by him to the company; and
- **pay damages** to the **company, to statutory bodies or authorities, or to members or creditors**
  for loss arising out of **incorrect or misleading statements of particulars** made in his audit
  report.

**Section 147(4)** requires the Central Government to notify a body, authority or officer to ensure
**prompt payment of those damages**, which then reports to the Central Government on the payment.

**Section 147(5) — the firm.** Where the audit is conducted by an **audit firm** and it is **proved
that the partner or partners acted in a fraudulent manner or abetted or colluded in any fraud** by or
in relation to the company or its directors or officers, the liability — **civil or criminal** — is
that of the **partner or partners concerned and of the firm, jointly and severally**. The **proviso**
narrows the criminal side: in respect of **criminal liability other than fine**, **only** the partners
who acted fraudulently or colluded are liable. So the firm shares the money; it does not share the
imprisonment.

<Callout kind="amendment" label="AMENDMENT-CHECK · rupee figures and the officer's imprisonment">
  <p>
    The <strong>rupee ranges in s. 147(1) and 147(2)</strong> and the <strong>penalty in
    s. 143(15)</strong> have been recast by the amendment Acts of 2017 and 2020, and
    <strong>imprisonment for officers in default under s. 147(1) was removed</strong>. The bank
    questions on s. 147 are written around the <em>structure</em> — which sections expose the auditor,
    the fine-versus-imprisonment split turning on intent, the multiples 4× and 8×, and the
    joint-and-several rule with its criminal-liability proviso — rather than around bare figures.
    Verify the amounts against the notified text before quoting them in an answer.
  </p>
</Callout>

  <p>
    M/s Iravati &amp; Co, a four-partner firm, audits Sthira Infra Limited for a remuneration of
    <strong>₹9,00,000</strong>. One partner, CA Bhavesh, signs a report he knows to be false, having

  </p>
  <p>

    liability under s. 447.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 12. Cost records and cost audit — section 148

**Section 148(1)** — the **Central Government** may, **by order**, in respect of a **prescribed class of
companies engaged in the production of prescribed goods or the provision of prescribed services**,
direct that **particulars relating to the utilisation of material or labour or other items of cost** be
included in the books of account kept by that class. Before making such an order for a class of
companies **regulated under a special Act**, the Central Government must **consult the regulator**
established under that Act.

**Section 148(2)** — where the Central Government is of the opinion that it is necessary, it may **by
order direct that an audit of the cost records** of such of those companies as have a **prescribed net
worth or a prescribed turnover** be conducted in the manner specified.

**Section 148(3) — who conducts it.** The cost audit is conducted by a **Cost Accountant**, appointed
by the **Board**, on **remuneration determined by the members** in the prescribed manner. Two provisos
matter: **no person appointed under s. 139 as the company's (statutory) auditor may be appointed to
conduct the cost audit**; and the **cost auditor must comply with the cost auditing standards**.

| Question | Cost audit under s. 148 | Statutory audit under s. 139 |
|---|---|---|
| Who is eligible | a **Cost Accountant** | a **Chartered Accountant** |
| Who appoints | the **Board** | the **members** (Board only for the first auditor) |
| Who fixes remuneration | **determined by the members**, in the prescribed manner | **fixed in general meeting** (Board for the first auditor) |
| Report goes to | the **Board** | the **members** |
| Can one person do both | **No** — the s. 139 auditor is expressly barred | — |

**The remuneration mechanics, in the order they happen.** Where the company is **required to have an
audit committee**, the Board appoints the cost auditor **on the audit committee's recommendation**, the
committee also **recommends the remuneration**, the **Board approves** it and the **shareholders ratify
it subsequently**. Where the company is **not required to have an audit committee**, the **Board
appoints** and **approves the remuneration**, and the **shareholders ratify it subsequently**. Either
way the shareholders have the last word — which is what s. 148(3) means by remuneration "determined by
the members".

**Sections 148(4) to (8) — the rest of the chain.**

- **148(4)** — the cost audit is **in addition to** the s. 143 audit, never in substitution for it.
- **148(5)** — the **qualifications, disqualifications, rights, duties and obligations** applicable to
  auditors under this Chapter apply, **so far as may be applicable**, to the **cost auditor**; the
  company must give him **all assistance and facilities**; and the **cost audit report is submitted to
  the Board**.
- **148(6)** — the company shall, **within 30 days from the date of receipt of a copy of the cost audit
  report**, **furnish it to the Central Government** together with **full information and explanation
  on every reservation or qualification** in it.
- **148(7)** — if, after considering the report, the Central Government wants **further information or
  explanation**, it may call for it and the company must furnish it within the time specified.
- **148(8)** — on default, the **company and every officer in default** are punishable as provided in
  **s. 147(1)**, and the **cost auditor in default** as provided in **ss. 147(2) to 147(4)**.

<div class="formula">
<strong>The s. 148 chain in one line.</strong> Cost auditor → <strong>report to the Board</strong> →
company files it with the <strong>Central Government within 30 days of receiving it</strong>, with
explanations on every qualification.<br />
Contrast s. 143: statutory auditor → <strong>report to the members</strong>. The destination of the
report is the cleanest discriminator between the two audits.
</div>

  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 7 — cost audit, appointment and filing, dated">
  <p>
    Prathama Cements Limited is covered by the cost-audit rules and has an audit committee. Its Board
    appoints CA Suhas — the company's statutory auditor under s. 139 — as cost auditor, at a
    remuneration fixed by the Board alone. The cost audit report is later received by the company on
    <strong>5 October 2026</strong>; the company files it with the Central Government on 20 November
    2026.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>1 · The appointee is ineligible twice over.</strong> The first proviso to s. 148(3) bars
    the person appointed under s. 139 as auditor from conducting the cost audit; and a cost audit must
    in any event be conducted by a <strong>Cost Accountant</strong>, not a chartered accountant acting
    as such. <strong>2 · The process is wrong.</strong> With an audit committee in place, the
    appointment and the remuneration had to come on the <strong>committee's recommendation</strong>,
    be approved by the Board, and be <strong>ratified by the shareholders</strong> — the Board could
    not fix it alone. <strong>3 · The filing is late.</strong> 30 days from 5 October 2026 expires on
    <strong>4 November 2026</strong>; filing on 20 November breaches s. 148(6).
    <strong>4 · Consequence.</strong> Section 148(8) applies s. 147(1) to the company and its officers
    in default, and ss. 147(2) to (4) to the cost auditor in default. <strong>5 · One more duty.</strong>
    Section 143(14) would have made the s. 143(12) fraud-reporting obligation the cost auditor's own,
    on the same 2 / 45 / 15-day clock.
  </p>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

1. **Saying the appointment is ratified at every AGM.** The ratification proviso to s. 139(1) was
   **omitted in 2017**. The term runs its full five AGMs.
2. **Mixing the first-auditor clocks.** Non-government: **Board 30 days from registration**, members
   **90 days** at an EGM. Government: **C&amp;AG 60 days**, Board **next 30**, members **60**. Government
   *subsequent* auditor: **180 days from the commencement of the financial year**.
3. **Making every casual vacancy go to the members.** Only a vacancy caused by **resignation** needs
   members' approval, in a meeting held **within three months of the Board's recommendation**.
4. **Applying the rotation cooling-off to all companies.** The s. 139(2) five-year bar is
   **company-specific**; the s. 140(5) five-year debarment applies to **every company**.
5. **Passing the special resolution before the Central Government's approval.** Under s. 140(1) the
   order is Board resolution → **ADT-2 within 30 days** → approval → **special resolution within 60
   days of the approval** → and the auditor must be **heard**.
6. **Treating ADT-3 as the company's filing.** Resignation is the **auditor's** duty, within **30 days
   of resignation**, to the company **and** the Registrar (and the C&amp;AG for a government company).
7. **Saying an LLP cannot be an auditor.** It can — it is the express exception to the body-corporate
   bar. But only its **chartered accountant partners** may act and sign, and a **majority of partners
   practising in India** must be chartered accountants.
8. **Giving the auditor himself the ₹1 lakh securities relief.** That relief is for a **relative**
   only, with a **60-day** corrective window. The auditor and his partner may hold **nothing**.
9. **Counting every audit towards the twenty.** Exclude **one-person, dormant and small companies**
   and **private companies with paid-up capital below ₹100 crore**; and remember the ceiling attaches
   to **each partner**.
10. **Reporting a ₹1 crore fraud straight to the Central Government.** Even then the **first** step is
    the audit committee or Board **within 2 days**; the Central Government comes after **45 + 15**.
11. **Forgetting that s. 143(12) covers the cost auditor and the secretarial auditor** through
    s. 143(14).
12. **Confusing the two audits' reports.** The **statutory** auditor reports to the **members**; the
    **cost** auditor reports to the **Board**, and the **company** then files with the Central
    Government within **30 days**.
13. **Saying the auditor may choose whether to attend a general meeting.** He **shall** attend unless
    the **company** exempts him, and his right to be heard is limited to business concerning him as
    auditor.
14. **Ignoring the intent limb of s. 147(2).** Ordinary contravention → fine, capped at the lower of
    ₹5,00,000 and **4×** remuneration. Knowing or wilful with intent to deceive → **imprisonment up to
    one year and** fine, capped at the lower of ₹25,00,000 and **8×** remuneration.

## One-page revision summary

**Who appoints whom.** **Board** — first auditor of a non-government company (30 days from
registration); casual vacancy (30 days); cost auditor under s. 148(3). **Members** — every subsequent
auditor at the AGM (s. 139(1)); the first auditor if the Board fails (EGM within 90 days); approval of
a resignation-caused casual vacancy (within 3 months of the Board's recommendation); remuneration
(s. 142). **C&amp;AG** — government-company auditors: **first within 60 days of registration**,
**subsequent within 180 days of the commencement of the financial year**, **casual vacancy within 30
days**. **Central Government** — previous approval for removal (s. 140(1)); appointment after a
Tribunal order on its application (s. 140(5)). **Tribunal** — direction to change auditors on fraud
(s. 140(5)).

**Every clock in the chapter.**

| Clock | Period | Runs from |
|---|---|---|
| First auditor, non-government (s. 139(6)) | **30 days** | date of **registration** |
| Members' EGM on the Board's failure (s. 139(6)) | **90 days** | the Board's information to members |
| First auditor, government company (s. 139(7)) | **60 days** → Board **30** → members **60** | date of **registration** |
| Subsequent auditor, government company (s. 139(5)) | **180 days** | **commencement of the financial year** |
| Casual vacancy (s. 139(8)) | **30 days** (Board or C&amp;AG; government company Board gets a further 30) | the vacancy |
| Members' approval of a resignation-caused vacancy (s. 139(8)(i)) | **3 months** | the **Board's recommendation** |
| Notice of appointment to the Registrar, ADT-1 (s. 139(1)) | **15 days** | the **meeting** |
| Rotation tenure / cooling-off (s. 139(2)) | **5 years** (individual), **5 + 5** (firm); **5 years** cooling | completion of the term |
| Application to the Central Government for removal, ADT-2 (s. 140(1)) | **30 days** | the **Board resolution** |
| Special resolution for removal (s. 140(1)) | **60 days** | **receipt of the approval** |
| Auditor's resignation statement, ADT-3 (s. 140(2)) | **30 days** | the **date of resignation** |
| Tribunal's interim order on the Central Government's application (s. 140(5)) | **15 days** | receipt of the application |
| Relative's corrective action on securities (s. 141(3)(d)(i)) | **60 days** | acquisition beyond the limit |
| C&amp;AG's supplementary audit or comments (s. 143(6)) | **60 days** | receipt of the **audit report** |
| Fraud: auditor to Board or audit committee (s. 143(12)) | **2 days** | **knowledge** of the fraud |
| Fraud: reply sought from Board or audit committee | **45 days** | the auditor's report to them |
| Fraud: auditor to the Central Government, ADT-4 | **15 days** | **receipt of the reply, or expiry of the 45 days** |
| Cost audit report filed with the Central Government (s. 148(6)) | **30 days** | **receipt of the report** by the company |

**Every money line.** Relative's securities — face value up to **₹1,00,000** (60-day cure).
Guarantee or security for a third person's debt — up to **₹1,00,000**. Indebtedness — up to
**₹5,00,000**. Fraud reportable to the Central Government — **₹1,00,00,000** and above. Penalty for
failing to report fraud — **₹5,00,000** listed / **₹1,00,000** unlisted. Auditor's fine under
s. 147(2) — **₹25,000 to ₹5,00,000 or 4×** remuneration, whichever is less; wilful — **₹50,000 to
₹25,00,000 or 8×**, plus imprisonment up to **1 year**. Rotation classes (rules-level) — unlisted
public **₹10 crore** paid-up, private **₹50 crore** paid-up, borrowings or deposits **₹50 crore**.
Ceiling — **20** audits per person, excluding one-person, dormant and small companies and private
companies below **₹100 crore** paid-up.

**Core positions.** **s. 139(1)** — appointment at the first AGM for five AGMs, written consent plus an
eligibility certificate before, ADT-1 within 15 days after; **no ratification**. **s. 139(2)–(4)** —
rotation for listed and prescribed classes; individual one term, firm two; five-year cooling; common
partner and network bars; joint auditors staggered; members may tighten. **s. 140** — removal needs
Central Government approval **then** a special resolution and a hearing; resignation needs ADT-3 in 30
days; special notice governs displacing a retiring auditor; the Tribunal may order a change on fraud,
with a five-year all-company debarment and s. 447 exposure. **s. 141** — chartered accountant only,
firm by firm name with a majority of practising partners, LLP allowed but only its CA partners sign;
disqualifications by status, holding, debt, guarantee, business relationship, relative's office,
employment elsewhere, the twenty-audit ceiling, fraud conviction within ten years and s. 144 conflicts;
a disqualification incurred later vacates the office as a **casual vacancy**. **s. 142** — remuneration
in general meeting; Board for the first auditor; fee plus audit expenses and facilities, excluding
other services. **s. 143** — access at all times including subsidiaries for consolidation; the six
inquiry matters; the ten report clauses with internal financial controls and Rule 11; reasons for every
qualification; the C&amp;AG's directions, supplementary audit within 60 days and test audit; branch
audit with the branch report sent to the company's auditor; compliance with auditing standards; fraud
reporting on the **2 / 45 / 15** clock above **₹1 crore**, audit committee or Board and Board's-report
disclosure below it, extended to cost and secretarial auditors, with s. 143(15) penalties. **s. 144** —
eight named prohibited services plus a residuary limb, directly or indirectly. **s. 145** — signing,
and adverse matter read at the general meeting. **s. 146** — notices to the auditor; duty to attend
unless exempted; right to be heard on business concerning him. **s. 147** — company and officers fined;
the auditor fined for ss. 139, 143, 144 and 145, with imprisonment on the wilful limb; refund of
remuneration and damages after conviction; the firm jointly and severally liable, but criminal
liability other than fine only for the colluding partners. **s. 148** — cost records and cost audit by
a **Cost Accountant** appointed by the **Board** on remuneration determined by the **members**; never
the s. 139 auditor; report to the **Board**; company files with the Central Government within **30
days**; defaults punished through s. 147.
