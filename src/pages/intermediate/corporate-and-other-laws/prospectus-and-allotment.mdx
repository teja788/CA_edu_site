A private company writes to eleven investors it already knows and raises ₹40 crore. A public company
takes out newspaper advertisements, files a document with the Registrar, and raises the same ₹40
crore from twenty thousand strangers. Both end with money in a bank account and shares in a register.
Chapter III of the Companies Act 2013 is the law of the difference between them: **who may be
approached, what must be told to them, in what document, when the money must be returned if the
issue fails, and who pays when the document lies**.

For the exam this chapter is **two clocks and one document**. The document is the prospectus — its
definition, its four kinds and the liability that attaches to a false statement in it. The clocks are
**section 39** (public allotment: minimum subscription, 30 days, refunds) and **section 42** (private
placement: 200 persons, 60 days, 15 days, 12% per annum). Examiners set the same question shapes year
after year: *give the date*, *give the amount*, *state whether the offer is a public offer or a
private placement*, and *who is liable and with what defence*. Nothing here rewards vague prose.

## 1. Two routes to raising securities — section 23

**Section 23** is the map of the whole chapter. It answers one question: *how may a company issue
securities at all?*

| Company | Permitted routes |
|---|---|
| **Public company** | **(a)** to the **public** through a **prospectus** (a "public offer"); **(b)** through a **private placement** under s. 42; **(c)** through a **rights issue** or a **bonus issue** under s. 62, with the Act's and, for a listed company, SEBI's requirements complied with |
| **Private company** | **(a)** through a **private placement** under s. 42; **(b)** through a **rights issue** or a **bonus issue** under s. 62 |

Two consequences follow immediately, and both are examined.

First, a **private company can never make a public offer**. Its articles must restrict the right to
transfer shares and **prohibit any invitation to the public** to subscribe for its securities
(s. 2(68)). If a private company advertises an issue, it has not merely broken s. 23 — it has
destroyed the very restriction that makes it private.

Second, **"public offer" is defined by the section itself** to include an **initial public offer**, a
**further public offer** of securities to the public by a company, **and an offer for sale** of
securities to the public by an **existing shareholder** through a company. That last limb is the door
to s. 28 (§6 below).

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>Section 23(2)–(4) — the "listing abroad" limbs.</strong> Section 23(3) permits a
    <strong>public company</strong> to issue such class of securities as may be prescribed for listing
    on <strong>permitted stock exchanges in permissible foreign jurisdictions</strong>, and s. 23(4)
    empowers the Central Government to exempt any class of public companies from provisions of
    Chapter III, Chapter IV, s. 89, s. 90 or s. 127 by notification, every such notification being
    laid before Parliament. These sub-sections are examinable as <em>existence</em> points — that the
    power exists and sits with the Central Government — not as detail.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. What counts as a prospectus — definition and kinds

**Section 2(70)** defines a prospectus as **any document described or issued as a prospectus**, and
includes a **red herring prospectus** under s. 32, a **shelf prospectus** under s. 31, or **any
notice, circular, advertisement or other document inviting offers from the public for the
subscription or purchase of any securities** of a body corporate.

Take the definition apart, because every trap in this area lives in one of its four elements:

1. **A document.** An oral invitation, however public, is not a prospectus. The document need not be
   called a prospectus — a circular, a newspaper advertisement or a notice qualifies.
2. **Inviting offers.** A prospectus is an **invitation to offer**, not an offer. The applicant makes
   the offer; the company accepts it by **allotment**. That single point decides a surprising number
   of questions about when a contract is formed.
3. **From the public.** An invitation to identified persons on a strictly private basis is not a
   public invitation — which is exactly the space s. 42 occupies.
4. **For subscription or purchase of securities.** "Purchase" widens it beyond fresh issues to an
   **offer for sale** of existing securities (s. 28).

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>A document is not saved by what it calls itself.</strong> Students often reason that a
    circular headed "Information Note — not a prospectus" escapes s. 2(70). It does not. The
    definition catches "any notice, circular, advertisement or <em>other document</em>" that
    <em>invites offers from the public</em>; the label is irrelevant and the test is the
    <strong>substance of the invitation</strong>. Conversely, a document that genuinely invites no one
    — an annual report, a research note — is not a prospectus merely because it describes securities.
  </p>
</Callout>

**The four kinds, at a glance.** The chapter's structure follows this table, and so does the exam.

| Kind | Section | One-line identity |
|---|---|---|
| **Abridged prospectus** | s. 33 (defined in s. 2(1)) | the memorandum containing the **salient features** of a prospectus, which **must accompany every application form** |
| **Shelf prospectus** | s. 31 | one prospectus filed by a **prescribed class** of company, valid for **one year**, with an **information memorandum** before each later offer |
| **Red herring prospectus** | s. 32 | a prospectus that **does not state the quantum or the price** of the securities, filed **at least three days** before the opening of the offer |
| **Deemed prospectus** | ss. 25, 28 | a document by which an **issuing house or an existing shareholder** offers securities to the public is **deemed to be a prospectus issued by the company** |

## 3. Abridged prospectus — section 33

**Section 33(1)** states the rule in a single breath: **no application form** for the purchase of any
securities of a company may be issued **unless it is accompanied by an abridged prospectus**. The
abridged prospectus is defined by **s. 2(1)** as a **memorandum containing such salient features of a
prospectus as may be specified by SEBI**.

The proviso lists the cases where the requirement does not bite:

- where the offer is made in connection with a **bona fide invitation to a person to enter into an
  underwriting agreement** with respect to the securities; or
- where the securities are **not offered to the public**.

**Section 33(2)** — a copy of the **full prospectus** must be furnished to **any person, before the
closing of the subscription list and the offer, on his request**. **Section 33(3)** — issuing an
application form without the abridged prospectus attracts a **penalty of ₹50,000 for each default**.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>Why the abridged prospectus exists.</strong> A full prospectus runs to hundreds of pages;
    an investor filling in a form will not read it. The abridged document puts the salient features in
    his hand at the moment of decision, while s. 33(2) preserves his right to demand the full text.
    The examiner's favourite pairing is <strong>s. 33(1) proviso (b)</strong> with <strong>s. 42</strong>:
    a private placement is not an offer to the public, so <em>no</em> abridged prospectus is needed —
    the PAS-4 offer letter does that job instead.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Shelf prospectus — section 31

A company that will tap the market repeatedly — typically a large lender — should not have to file a
fresh prospectus every quarter. **Section 31(1)** allows **any class or classes of companies as SEBI
may provide by regulations** to file a **shelf prospectus** with the Registrar at the stage of the
**first offer** of securities.

| Element | Position |
|---|---|
| Who may file | a class of companies **prescribed by SEBI regulations** — not every company |
| Filed with | the **Registrar**, at the stage of the **first offer** |
| Validity | must indicate a period **not exceeding one year** as the period of validity, **commencing from the date of opening of the first offer** of securities under it |
| Effect within validity | **no further prospectus is required** for any **second or subsequent offer** made within that period |
| Before each later offer | file an **information memorandum** with the Registrar, in the prescribed form and within the prescribed time |

**Section 31(2)** — the information memorandum states the **new charges created**, the **changes in
the financial position** of the company between the first offer and the succeeding offer, and such
other prescribed matters.

**The proviso to s. 31(2) is the deep point.** Where a company or any other person has **received
applications with advance payments** before a change is noted in the information memorandum, it must
**intimate the changes to the applicants**, and if any of them **wishes to withdraw** the application,
the company must **refund the whole of the money received** as advance payment — within the prescribed
time and manner. Investors who committed on stale information get an exit.

**Section 31(3)** — where an information memorandum is filed on every subsequent offer, the
**memorandum together with the shelf prospectus constitutes the prospectus** for that offer.

<div class="formula">Shelf prospectus validity = up to <strong>1 year</strong>, running from the <strong>date of opening of the first offer</strong> — not from the date of filing</div>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 1 — counting the shelf year, and the withdrawal right">
  <p>
    Sahyadri Infra Finance Limited, a company of a class SEBI has prescribed for this purpose, files a
    shelf prospectus with the Registrar on <strong>10 March 2026</strong>. Its first offer of
    debentures opens on <strong>2 April 2026</strong>. It plans a second tranche in November 2026 and a
    third in May 2027. In October 2026 it creates a fresh charge over its receivables. Applications
    with advance money for the second tranche have already been received when the charge is created.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>1 · The clock.</strong> Validity runs from the <strong>opening of the first offer</strong>,
    so the shelf prospectus is good to <strong>1 April 2027</strong> — not to 9 March 2027 (filing) and
    not to a rolling year from each tranche. The November tranche is inside; the
    <strong>May 2027 tranche is outside</strong>, and needs a fresh prospectus.
    <strong>2 · The information memorandum.</strong> Before the November tranche the company files an
    information memorandum disclosing the new charge and the change in its financial position; that
    memorandum <em>plus</em> the shelf prospectus is the prospectus for the tranche.
    <strong>3 · The applicants.</strong> Those who applied with advance payment before the charge was
    noted must be intimated; any of them who wishes to withdraw gets the <strong>whole</strong> of his
    advance money back. There is no "part refund" and no forfeiture.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Red herring prospectus — section 32

A **red herring prospectus** is defined in the Explanation to **s. 32** as a prospectus which **does
not include complete particulars of the quantum or price of the securities** included in it. It is
the book-building instrument: the issuer tests demand before fixing the price.

| Requirement | Position |
|---|---|
| Who may issue | a company **proposing to make an offer of securities**, prior to the issue of a prospectus |
| Filing | with the **Registrar**, **at least three days before the opening of the subscription list and the offer** |
| Obligations | a red herring prospectus **carries the same obligations as a prospectus**, and any **variation** between it and the prospectus is **highlighted as variations** in the prospectus |
| On closing | **upon the closing of the offer**, the **prospectus** stating the **total capital raised** (whether by way of debt or share capital), the **closing price** of the securities, and **any other details not included in the red herring prospectus**, is filed with the **Registrar and SEBI** |

<div class="formula">Red herring prospectus → file with the Registrar <strong>at least 3 days</strong> before the offer opens; after closing, file the final prospectus with <strong>the Registrar and SEBI</strong></div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Three days before the OPENING, not three days before printing; and the post-closing filing
    goes to two authorities.</strong> Two slips are common here. The first is treating the three-day
    period as running from the filing of the draft with SEBI — s. 32(2) fixes it to the
    <strong>opening of the subscription list and the offer</strong>. The second is answering that the
    final prospectus is filed with the Registrar alone: s. 32(4) says <strong>Registrar and
    SEBI</strong>. And note what a red herring prospectus is <em>not</em> — it is not a document with
    no price band and no liability. It <strong>carries the same obligations as a prospectus</strong>,
    so ss. 34 and 35 apply to it in full.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. Deemed prospectus and offer for sale — sections 25 and 28

Suppose a company allots its whole issue to a merchant bank, which the next week offers those shares
to the public. On a literal reading the company never invited the public, and the merchant bank is not
issuing "its" securities. **Section 25** closes that gap.

**Section 25(1)** — where a company **allots or agrees to allot** securities **with a view to all or
any of them being offered for sale to the public**, any document by which the **offer for sale to the
public is made** is **deemed to be a prospectus issued by the company**; all enactments and rules as
to the contents of a prospectus and as to **liability in respect of misstatements and otherwise**
apply as if the securities had been offered to the public directly by the company.

**Section 25(2)** supplies the evidential shortcut. It is **evidence that an allotment was made with a
view to the securities being offered for sale to the public** if it is shown either —

- **(a)** that an **offer of the securities for sale to the public was made within six months** after
  the allotment or agreement to allot; **or**
- **(b)** that at the date when the offer was made, the **whole consideration to be received** by the
  company in respect of the securities **had not been so received**.

**Section 25(3)** — in addition to the s. 26 contents, the deemed prospectus must state the **net
amount of the consideration received or to be received** by the company in respect of the securities
offered, and the **place and time** at which the contract under which the securities were allotted may
be inspected.

**Section 25(4)** — where the offeror is a **company or a firm**, it is enough that the document is
signed on behalf of the company by **two directors** or, in the case of a firm, by **not less than
one-half of the partners**.

**Section 28** is the shareholder's version. Where **certain members** of a company propose, **in
consultation with the Board**, to **offer the whole or part of their holding of shares to the
public**, they may do so **in accordance with the prescribed procedure**; any document by which the
offer is made is **deemed to be a prospectus issued by the company**, and all laws relating to a
prospectus apply. **Section 28(3)** requires the members to **reimburse the company** all expenses
incurred by it on the offer.

<div class="formula">s. 25(2) presumption: offer for sale within <strong>6 months</strong> of allotment, <strong>or</strong> full consideration not yet received at the date of the offer → deemed prospectus</div>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 2 — an offer for sale, and the six-month presumption">
  <p>
    Kalinga Ceramics Limited allots 80 lakh shares to Marut Capital Private Limited, an issuing house,
    on <strong>4 May 2026</strong>, for ₹96 crore payable in two instalments. Marut Capital pays ₹60
    crore and offers the shares to the public by a printed document dated <strong>18 September
    2026</strong>. The document contains an overstatement of Kalinga's order book.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>1 · Is it a prospectus?</strong> Yes, twice over. The offer for sale was made
    <strong>within six months</strong> of the allotment (4 May → 18 September is inside the window),
    and separately the <strong>whole consideration had not been received</strong> at the date of the
    offer. Either limb of s. 25(2) is enough; here both apply.
    <strong>2 · Whose prospectus?</strong> It is deemed to be a prospectus <strong>issued by Kalinga
    Ceramics</strong> — the company — so the s. 26 contents requirements and the ss. 34 and 35
    liabilities attach to it and to Kalinga's directors, not merely to Marut Capital.
    <strong>3 · Extra contents.</strong> Section 25(3) requires the document to state the
    <strong>net consideration</strong> received or receivable by Kalinga and the place and time for
    inspecting the allotment contract. <strong>4 · Signature.</strong> Marut Capital being a company,
    signature by <strong>two of its directors</strong> suffices under s. 25(4).
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. Matters to be stated, dating, signing and registration — section 26

**Section 26(1)** requires every prospectus issued by or on behalf of a public company, or by or on
behalf of any person engaged or interested in the formation of a public company, to be **dated and
signed** and to **state** the information and set out the reports specified — broadly, three families
of content:

1. **Identity and structure** — the name and registered-office address of the company; the names and
   addresses of the **company secretary, chief financial officer, auditors, bankers, trustees for
   debenture holders, underwriters** and such other persons as may be prescribed.
2. **The offer itself** — the **dates of opening and closing** of the issue; a declaration about the
   issue of an **allotment letter and refunds** within the prescribed time; a statement by the Board
   that **all monies received out of the issue** are to be **transferred to a separate bank account**
   as mentioned in **s. 40(3)**; details of **all monies utilised out of previous issues** and of
   monies **not so utilised**, with the form in which they are lying; the **capital structure**; the
   **main objects of the public offer** and terms of the present issue; the **minimum subscription**;
   the **premium payable on securities**; the **procedure and time schedule for allotment and issue**;
   and the **authority for the issue** with the details of the resolution passed for it.
3. **The reports** — reports for the purposes of the **financial information** specified in the
   section: the **auditor's report** on **profits and losses and assets and liabilities**, and where
   applicable on the **business of any subsidiary**, in the prescribed form.

Also required: **details of directors** including their appointments and remuneration; the
**particulars relating to management perception of risk factors** specific to the project; the
**gestation period** of the project; **deadlines for completion**; and any **litigation or legal
action** pending or taken by a Government Department or a statutory body during the last **five
years** immediately preceding the issue against the promoter of the company.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>Section 26(2) — what a prospectus need not repeat.</strong> Nothing in s. 26(1) requires
    the inclusion of matters or reports that would otherwise be required where the prospectus is
    issued <strong>more than two years after the company is entitled to commence business</strong>, in
    respect of an <strong>interval of more than two years</strong> between the issue and the last
    accounts. The point is that stale detail is not made compulsory; do not read s. 26(1) as an
    eternal checklist.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 26(3) — the expert's consent.** Where a prospectus includes a **statement made by an
expert**, the expert must have given his **written consent** to the issue and must **not have
withdrawn** it before delivery of a copy to the Registrar, and the prospectus must state that he has
given his consent and has not withdrawn it. The **Explanation** limits "expert" to a person who is
**not, and has not been, engaged or interested in the formation or promotion or management of the
company** — an independent expert only.

**Section 26(4)–(6) — dating, signing and registration.** No prospectus may be **issued** by or on
behalf of a company or in relation to an intended company unless, **on or before the date of its
publication**, a copy **signed by every person named in it as a director or proposed director**, or by
his duly authorised attorney, has been **delivered to the Registrar for registration**. The copy
delivered must have **endorsed on or attached to it** every consent to the issue given by an expert
and any other prescribed documents. Every prospectus must, **on its face**, state that a copy has been
delivered for registration and specify the documents delivered or attached.

**Section 26(7)–(9) — the outer limits.**

| Rule | Position |
|---|---|
| Registrar's duty | the Registrar **shall not register** a prospectus unless the s. 26(1)–(3) requirements are complied with and it is **accompanied by the consent in writing of every person named in it** as auditor, legal adviser, attorney, solicitor, banker or broker |
| **90-day validity** | a prospectus **shall not be valid if it is issued more than ninety days after the date on which a copy is delivered to the Registrar** |
| Contravention | **penalty on the company**, and on **every person knowingly a party to the issue** of a prospectus in contravention of s. 26 |

<div class="formula">A prospectus is <strong>not valid</strong> if issued more than <strong>90 days</strong> after the date a copy was delivered to the Registrar — the clock starts at <strong>delivery</strong>, not at Board approval or at registration</div>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 3 — the 90-day registration clock, dated">
  <p>
    Netravati Foods Limited's Board approves its prospectus on <strong>6 April 2026</strong>. A copy
    signed by every director is <strong>delivered to the Registrar on 21 April 2026</strong>. The
    Registrar registers it on 28 April 2026. The company, waiting for market conditions to improve,
    publishes the prospectus and opens the issue on <strong>24 July 2026</strong>.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — where the 90 days run from</div>
    Delivery to the Registrar = <strong>21 April 2026</strong><br />
    90 days from 21 April 2026 → 21 April + 9 days (April) = 30 April; + 31 (May) = 40 days to 31 May;
    + 30 (June) = 70 days to 30 June; + 20 = <strong>90 days on 20 July 2026</strong><br />
    Issue on <strong>24 July 2026</strong> is <strong>4 days late</strong> → the prospectus is
    <strong>not valid</strong>
  </div>
  <p>
    <strong>The trap.</strong> Counting from Board approval (6 April) gives 5 July; counting from
    registration (28 April) gives 27 July and makes the issue look safe. Both are wrong. Section 26(8)
    fixes the clock to the <strong>date on which a copy was delivered for registration</strong>. The
    company must deliver a fresh copy and start again.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. Variation of terms of contracts or objects — section 27

Money raised on a stated purpose may not be quietly redirected. **Section 27(1)** — a company **shall
not**, at any time, **vary the terms of a contract referred to in the prospectus** or **vary the
objects** for which the prospectus was issued, **except** subject to the **approval of, or except
subject to an authority given by, the company in general meeting by way of a special resolution**.

Three riders sit on that rule:

- The company must **publish the details of the notice** of the special resolution to shareholders,
  in the manner prescribed, in **two newspapers — one in English and one in the vernacular language**
  of the city where the registered office is situated — and place it on the company's **website**, if
  any, indicating the **justification** for the variation.
- The company **shall not use any amount raised by it through the prospectus** for **buying,
  trading or otherwise dealing in equity shares of any other listed company**.
- **Section 27(2)** — **dissenting shareholders**, being those who **have not agreed to the proposal
  to vary the terms of contracts or objects** referred to in the prospectus, must be given an **exit
  offer** by **promoters or controlling shareholders**, at such exit price and in such manner and
  conditions as may be **specified by SEBI** by regulations.

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Who approves a variation? | the **members**, by **special resolution** — not the Board, not the Registrar |
| What publicity? | **two newspapers** (English + vernacular of the registered-office city) **and** the website, with the **justification** |
| Who funds the exit? | **promoters or controlling shareholders**, at a price specified by **SEBI** |
| May the money be parked in listed equity? | **No** — an express prohibition in the proviso |

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Do not confuse s. 27 with s. 13(8).</strong> They look alike and are examined against each
    other. <strong>Section 13(8)</strong> is about <em>altering the objects clause of the
    memorandum</em> where a company that raised money from the public through a prospectus still has
    <strong>unutilised</strong> money — it needs a special resolution plus the two newspapers, the
    website and a dissenters' exit. <strong>Section 27</strong> is about <em>varying the terms of a
    contract referred to in the prospectus, or the objects for which the prospectus was issued</em>,
    and it applies <strong>"at any time"</strong> — it is not conditioned on money remaining unspent.
    Both end with a special resolution and a dissenters' exit; the <strong>trigger</strong> is what
    separates them.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Dematerialised issue and advertisement of prospectus — sections 29 and 30

**Section 29 — securities in dematerialised form.** Notwithstanding anything contained in any other
provisions of the Act, **every company making a public offer**, and **such other class or classes of
companies as may be prescribed**, must **issue securities only in dematerialised form** by complying
with the provisions of the **Depositories Act 1996** and the regulations made under it.
**Section 29(1A)** extends the discipline beyond issue: such class of **unlisted companies as may be
prescribed** must **hold and transfer** securities **only in dematerialised form**. **Section 29(2)**
leaves everyone else a choice — any other company **may convert** its securities into dematerialised
form **or issue** them in physical form under the Act, **or** in dematerialised form under the
Depositories Act.

<Callout kind="amendment" label="AMENDMENT-CHECK · which unlisted companies are caught by s. 29(1A)">
  <p>
    The <em>class</em> of unlisted companies required to hold and transfer only in dematerialised form
    is <strong>prescribed by rule</strong> and has been widened more than once — first for unlisted
    public companies, later extended to certain private companies with a transition window. The
    <strong>section</strong> is stable; the <strong>class</strong> is not. Answer from s. 29(1) and
    (1A) and say "such class as may be prescribed" unless the Study Material edition for your attempt
    names a class.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 30 — advertisement of prospectus.** Where an **advertisement of any prospectus** of a
company is published in any manner, it is **necessary to specify therein**:

- the **contents of its memorandum** as regards the **objects**, the **liability of members** and the
  **amount of share capital** of the company;
- the **names of the signatories to the memorandum** and the **number of shares subscribed for by
  them**; and
- its **capital structure**.

The point is that a short advertisement cannot smuggle a company past the disclosure the memorandum
would have forced. Note what s. 30 is not: it is not the s. 33 abridged prospectus (which accompanies
an **application form**), and it is not the s. 42(7) prohibition (which bans public advertising of a
**private placement** altogether).

## 10. Criminal liability for misstatements — section 34

**Section 34** — where a prospectus **issued, circulated or distributed** under Chapter III includes
any statement which is **untrue or misleading in form or context** in which it is included, **or where
any inclusion or omission of any matter is likely to mislead**, **every person who authorises the
issue** of such prospectus is **liable under section 447** (punishment for fraud).

**The proviso is the defence, and it has two independent limbs.** A person is not so liable if he
proves either:

- that the **statement or omission was immaterial**; **or**
- that he had **reasonable grounds to believe**, and **did up to the time of the issue of the
  prospectus believe**, that the statement was **true** or the inclusion or omission was
  **necessary**.

| Element | Section 34 |
|---|---|
| Who is liable | **every person who authorises the issue** of the prospectus |
| Nature | **criminal** — action under **s. 447** |
| Trigger | untrue or misleading statement, **or** a misleading inclusion or omission |
| Defence | **immateriality**, **or** reasonable and actual belief in truth or in the necessity of the inclusion/omission, held **up to the time of issue** |

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>The belief must survive to the moment of issue.</strong> A director who believed a forecast
    when the draft was settled, but learned it was false a week before the prospectus went out, has no
    defence — the proviso requires belief <em>up to the time of the issue</em>. The second common slip
    is treating s. 34 as requiring proof that someone <em>relied</em> on the statement. It does not:
    reliance and loss belong to <strong>s. 35</strong> (civil compensation). Section 34 punishes the
    <em>authorising</em> of a misleading prospectus whether or not a single investor was misled.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 11. Civil liability for misstatements — section 35

**Section 35(1)** — where a person has **subscribed for securities** of a company **acting on** any
statement included, or the inclusion or omission of any matter, in the prospectus **which is
misleading**, and has **sustained any loss or damage** as a consequence, the **company** and **every
person** who —

| Limb | Who |
|---|---|
| (a) | **is a director** at the time of the issue of the prospectus |
| (b) | has **authorised himself to be named and is named** in the prospectus as a director, or as having agreed to become a director either immediately or after an interval of time |
| (c) | is a **promoter** of the company |
| (d) | has **authorised the issue** of the prospectus |
| (e) | is an **expert** whose statement is included with his consent under s. 26 |

— is liable to **pay compensation to every person** who has sustained such loss or damage, **without
prejudice** to any punishment to which any person may be liable under **s. 36**.

**Section 35(2) — the two defences**, and they are narrow:

1. that, **having consented to become a director**, he **withdrew his consent before the issue** of
   the prospectus, and that it was **issued without his authority or consent**; or
2. that the prospectus was **issued without his knowledge or consent**, and that **on becoming aware
   of its issue he forthwith gave reasonable public notice** that it was issued without his knowledge
   or consent.

**Section 35(3) — fraud strips the ceiling.** Where it is proved that a prospectus has been issued
**with intent to defraud** the applicants for the securities, or any other person, **or for any
fraudulent purpose**, every person referred to in s. 35(1) is **personally responsible, without any
limitation of liability**, for all or any of the losses or damages incurred by any person who
subscribed to the securities on the basis of such prospectus.

<div class="formula">s. 34 = <strong>criminal</strong>, s. 447, "every person who authorises the issue", defence = immaterial <em>or</em> reasonable belief · s. 35 = <strong>civil</strong> compensation to a subscriber who acted on the statement and suffered loss, five categories, defence = withdrawal of consent <em>or</em> no knowledge + forthwith public notice</div>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 4 — the same prospectus, three different liabilities">
  <p>
    Girivan Renewables Limited's prospectus overstates a signed power-purchase agreement. Four people
    are before the court. <strong>P</strong> is a director who chaired the meeting approving the
    prospectus and knew the agreement had lapsed. <strong>Q</strong> agreed in March 2026 to become a
    director and is named in the prospectus as such; she wrote withdrawing her consent on 2 April 2026
    and the prospectus issued on 20 April 2026 without her authority. <strong>R</strong> is the
    valuer whose report, given with written consent, contains the overstated figure. <strong>S</strong>
    is an investor who read the prospectus, subscribed for 40,000 shares, and lost money when the
    truth emerged.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>P.</strong> He authorised the issue and had no belief in the truth of the statement:
    <strong>s. 34</strong> criminal liability under s. 447, and <strong>s. 35(1)(a)</strong> civil
    compensation to S. Because the prospectus was issued knowing the fact was false, s. 35(3) exposes
    him to <strong>unlimited personal liability</strong> if fraudulent intent is proved.
    <strong>Q.</strong> She falls squarely inside <strong>s. 35(2)(a)</strong> — consent withdrawn
    before issue, prospectus issued without her authority — so she is <strong>not liable</strong>. Had
    she merely disagreed but not withdrawn, she would be liable under limb (b).
    <strong>R.</strong> An expert under <strong>s. 35(1)(e)</strong>, liable for compensation; his
    escape route was to withdraw the s. 26(3) consent before delivery of the prospectus to the
    Registrar. <strong>S.</strong> He is the claimant — he must show he <strong>acted on</strong> the
    statement and <strong>sustained loss</strong>. An investor who never read the prospectus, or who
    lost money because the whole sector fell, recovers nothing under s. 35.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 12. Fraudulent inducement, impersonation and class action — sections 36, 38 and 37

**Section 36 — fraudulently inducing persons to invest money.** Any person who, **knowingly or
recklessly**, makes any **statement, promise or forecast which is false, deceptive or misleading**, or
**deliberately conceals any material facts**, to **induce another person** to enter into or offer to
enter into —

- **(a)** any agreement for, or with a view to, **acquiring, disposing of, subscribing for or
  underwriting securities**; or
- **(b)** any agreement the purpose or pretended purpose of which is to **secure a profit** to any of
  the parties from the **yield of securities** or by reference to **fluctuations in the value** of
  securities; or
- **(c)** any agreement for, or with a view to, **obtaining credit facilities from any bank or
  financial institution**,

is **liable for action under section 447**.

Section 36 is deliberately wider than s. 34. It needs **no prospectus at all** — a telephone call, a
messaging-app broadcast or a slide deck will do — and it reaches **bank credit**, which has nothing to
do with an offer of securities.

**Section 38 — punishment for personation for acquisition of securities.** Any person who —

- **(a)** makes or **abets making** of an application in a **fictitious name** to a company for
  acquiring or subscribing for its securities; or
- **(b)** makes or abets making of **multiple applications** to a company **in different names or in
  different combinations of his name or surname** for acquiring or subscribing for its securities; or
- **(c)** otherwise **induces a company, directly or indirectly, to allot or register any transfer**
  of securities to him or to any other person **in a fictitious name**,

is **liable for action under section 447**. Three consequences complete the section:

| Sub-section | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **38(2)** | s. 38(1) must be **prominently reproduced in every prospectus** issued by a company **and in every form of application** for securities |
| **38(3)** | on conviction, the **Court may also order disgorgement of the gain** made and the **seizure and disposal of the securities** in the person's possession |
| **38(4)** | the amount received through **disgorgement or disposal** is credited to the **Investor Education and Protection Fund** |

**Section 37 — class action.** A suit may be filed, or any other action taken, **under s. 34, s. 35 or
s. 36** by **any person, group of persons or association of persons** affected by any **misleading
statement** or by the **inclusion or omission of any matter** in the prospectus. The section does not
create a new wrong; it creates **standing** — investors need not sue one by one.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>Keep the four misstatement sections apart by asking two questions.</strong>
    <em>Is a prospectus required?</em> — ss. 34, 35 and 37 need one; <strong>s. 36 does not</strong>.
    <em>Who is the wrongdoer?</em> — s. 34 and s. 35 catch those on the <strong>issuer side</strong>
    (authorisers, directors, promoters, experts); <strong>s. 38 catches the applicant</strong>, the
    person on the <em>outside</em> trying to obtain securities by fictitious or multiple applications.
    Section 38 is the only one of the four in which the company is the victim.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 13. Allotment of securities — section 39

Section 39 protects an applicant in a **public** issue from two risks: that the company will take his
money on a half-subscribed issue that can never fund the project, and that it will keep the money if
the issue fails.

**Section 39(1) — minimum subscription.** **No allotment** of any securities of a company **offered to
the public for subscription** may be made unless (i) the amount stated in the prospectus as the
**minimum amount** has been **subscribed**, and (ii) the sums payable **on application** for the amount
so stated have been **paid to and received by the company** by cheque or other instrument.

**Section 39(2) — the application-money floor.** The amount payable on application on **every
security** must **not be less than five per cent of the nominal amount of the security**, or such
**other percentage or amount as may be specified by SEBI** by regulations.

<div class="formula">Minimum application money = <strong>5% of the NOMINAL (face) amount</strong> of the security — never 5% of the issue price — unless SEBI specifies a higher percentage or amount</div>

**Section 39(3) — the 30-day window and the refund.** If the stated minimum amount **has not been
subscribed** and the sum payable on application **is not received within thirty days from the date of
issue of the prospectus**, or such other period as may be specified by SEBI, the amount received under
s. 39(1) must be **returned within such time and manner as may be prescribed**.

**Section 39(4) — return of allotment.** Whenever a company **having a share capital** makes any
allotment of securities, it must **file with the Registrar a return of allotment** in the prescribed
manner.

**Section 39(5) — default.** On default under sub-section (3) or (4), the **company and its officer
who is in default** are liable to a penalty, **for each default, of ₹1,000 for each day during which
the default continues or ₹1,00,000, whichever is less**.

<Callout kind="amendment" label="AMENDMENT-CHECK · the refund mechanics and the return of allotment are rules-level">

    penalty in s. 39(5) are in the <strong>section</strong>. What is <em>prescribed</em> sits in the
    <strong>Companies (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) Rules 2014</strong>: the refund is to be
    made within <strong>fifteen days from the closure of the issue</strong>, failing which the
    directors who are officers in default become <strong>jointly and severally liable to repay with
    interest</strong> at a rate fixed by the rule; and the return of allotment under s. 39(4) is filed
    in <strong>Form PAS-3 within thirty days of the allotment</strong>. Both the form name and the
    rule-level interest rate have moved since 2014 — verify for your attempt. This chapter's bank
    examines the <strong>30-day</strong> and <strong>PAS-3 30-day</strong> periods and the
    <strong>5%</strong> floor, and deliberately does <em>not</em> examine the rule-level interest rate.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 14. Listing, application money and underwriting commission — section 40

**Section 40(1)** — **every company making a public offer** must, **before making such offer**, make
an **application to one or more recognised stock exchanges** and **obtain permission** for the
securities to be **dealt with** in such exchange or exchanges. **Section 40(2)** — where a prospectus
states that such an application has been made, the prospectus must **state the name or names of the
stock exchange** in which the securities are to be dealt with.

**Section 40(3) — the separate bank account.** All monies **received on application** from the public
for subscription must be **kept in a separate bank account in a scheduled bank**, and may **not be
utilised for any purpose other than**:

- **(a)** **adjustment against allotment** of securities, where the securities have been permitted to
  be dealt with in the stock exchange or exchanges; or
- **(b)** **repayment** of monies received from applicants, where the company is **for any other
  reason unable to allot** securities.

**Section 40(4)** — any **condition purporting to require or bind any applicant to waive compliance**
with any requirement of s. 40 is **void**. An investor cannot sign away this protection, and a clause
in the application form attempting it has no effect.

**Section 40(5)** — default in complying with s. 40 makes the **company punishable with a fine** and
**every officer in default punishable with imprisonment or fine or both** — the only place in this
chapter where imprisonment attaches to a purely procedural default.

**Section 40(6) — underwriting commission.** A company **may pay commission to any person in
connection with the subscription to its securities**, **subject to such conditions as may be
prescribed**. The conditions are in the **Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules 2014**:

| Condition | Position (rules-level) |
|---|---|
| Authority | payment must be **authorised by the articles** |
| Source | out of the **proceeds of the issue** or the **profits** of the company, or both |
| Cap — **shares** | **5% of the price at which the shares are issued**, or the rate authorised by the articles, **whichever is less** |
| Cap — **debentures** | **2.5% of the price at which the debentures are issued**, or the rate authorised by the articles, **whichever is less** |
| Disclosure | the **prospectus** discloses the **names of the underwriters**, the **rate of commission** and the **number of securities underwritten** |
| Bar | **no commission** on securities **not offered to the public** for subscription |
| Filing | a copy of the **contract for payment of commission** is delivered to the Registrar with the prospectus |

<div class="formula">Commission cap = <strong>lower of</strong> (rate in the articles) and (<strong>5%</strong> of the issue price for shares · <strong>2.5%</strong> of the issue price for debentures) — the base is the <strong>PRICE at which they are issued</strong>, not the face value</div>

<Callout kind="amendment" label="AMENDMENT-CHECK · the 5% / 2.5% caps live in the Rules, not in section 40">
  <p>
    Section 40(6) says only "subject to such conditions as may be prescribed". The
    <strong>5%</strong> and <strong>2.5%</strong> ceilings, the "whichever is less" formulation and the
    disclosure list are in <strong>rule 13 of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules
    2014</strong>. Under the 1956 Act the equivalent figures were 5% and 2.5% of the <em>price</em>
    too, but the surrounding conditions differed. Because these are rules-level, an examiner who wants
    a safe question asks for the <strong>section</strong> (40(6)) and the <strong>base</strong> (issue
    price); an examiner who wants a hard one asks for the arithmetic. This chapter's bank does both —
    re-verify the percentages against the edition notified for your attempt.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 15. Private placement — section 42

Private placement is the **quiet route**. Section 42 keeps it quiet by capping the audience, banning
advertising, forcing the money into a separate account, and putting a hard clock on allotment.

**Section 42(1)–(2) — who may be approached.** A company may make a private placement of securities to
a **select group of persons identified by the Board** ("identified persons"), whose number **shall not
exceed the prescribed maximum in a financial year**. The prescribed maximum is **200 persons in the
aggregate in a financial year**, **excluding qualified institutional buyers** and **employees offered
securities under a scheme of employees' stock option**.

<div class="formula"><strong>200 identified persons</strong> in the aggregate in a <strong>financial year</strong>, per kind of security — <strong>excluding</strong> qualified institutional buyers and <strong>excluding</strong> employees under an ESOP scheme</div>

**Explanation III to s. 42 — the deeming trap.** If a company, **listed or unlisted**, makes an offer
to allot, or invites subscription, or allots, or enters into an agreement to allot, securities to
**more than the prescribed number of persons** — **whether or not the payment has been received**, and
**whether or not the company intends to list** the securities anywhere in or outside India — the offer
is **deemed to be an offer to the public** and is governed by Part I of Chapter III. That is, the
company is retrospectively treated as having made a **public offer without a prospectus**, with every
consequence that follows.

**Section 42(3) — the offer letter.** The company issues a **private placement offer and application**
in the prescribed form to the identified persons, whose **names and addresses are recorded** by the
company in the prescribed manner. The **proviso** is short and heavily examined: the private placement
offer and application **shall not carry any right of renunciation**. Contrast a rights issue under
s. 62, where renunciation is the norm.

**Section 42(4) — the money.** Every identified person willing to subscribe applies in the offer
document **along with the subscription money paid by cheque, demand draft or other banking channel and
NOT by cash**. The proviso: a company **shall not utilise monies raised through private placement
unless allotment is made AND the return of allotment is filed with the Registrar** under s. 42(8).

**Section 42(5) — one offer at a time.** **No fresh offer or invitation** may be made unless the
**allotments with respect to any offer or invitation made earlier have been completed**, or that
earlier offer or invitation has been **withdrawn or abandoned** by the company.

**Section 42(6) — the 60/15/12 chain.** This is the single most examined provision in the chapter.

| Step | Period | Runs from |
|---|---|---|
| **Allot** the securities | **within 60 days** | the date of **receipt of the application money** |
| If not allotted — **repay** the application money | **within 15 days** | the **expiry of the 60 days** |
| If not repaid — **interest at 12% per annum** | until repayment | the **expiry of the sixtieth day** |

<div class="formula">Allot within <strong>60 days</strong> of receipt of application money → else repay within <strong>15 days</strong> of the expiry of the 60 days → else repay with interest at <strong>12% p.a. from the expiry of the SIXTIETH day</strong> (not from the end of the 15-day window)</div>

The **proviso to s. 42(6)** puts the money in quarantine: monies received on application must be kept
in a **separate bank account in a scheduled bank** and used only for **(a)** adjustment against
allotment, or **(b)** repayment where the company is unable to allot. The parallel with s. 40(3) is
exact.

**Section 42(7) — no publicity.** A company issuing securities under s. 42 **shall not release any
public advertisements** or utilise any **media, marketing or distribution channels or agents** to
inform the **public at large** about the issue. A private placement that is advertised is a
contradiction in terms.

**Section 42(8) — the return of allotment.** A company making any allotment under s. 42 must **file
with the Registrar a return of allotment within fifteen days from the date of the allotment**,
including a **complete list of all allottees** with full names, addresses, the number of securities
allotted and such other information as may be prescribed.

**Sections 42(9) and 42(10) — penalties.**

| Default | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **s. 42(9)** — failure to file the return of allotment in time | the **company, its promoters and directors** are liable to a penalty **for each default of ₹1,000 for each day** during which the default continues, **not exceeding ₹25,00,000** |
| **s. 42(10)** — making an offer or accepting monies **in contravention of the section** | the **company, its promoters and directors** are liable to a penalty which may extend to the **amount raised through the private placement or ₹2 crore, whichever is lower**, and the company must **refund all monies with interest as specified in s. 42(6) within 30 days of the order imposing the penalty** |

<Callout kind="amendment" label="AMENDMENT-CHECK · section 42 was substituted, and its ceilings have moved">
  <p>
    The whole of s. 42 was <strong>substituted by the Companies (Amendment) Act 2017</strong> with
    effect from 2018. Three things changed that older banks still get wrong: the return of allotment
    now goes in <strong>15 days</strong> (formerly 30); the company may <strong>not use the money until
    the return is filed</strong> (a new proviso to s. 42(4)); and the s. 42(10) penalty is now capped
    at the <strong>amount raised or ₹2 crore, whichever is lower</strong> (formerly the amount involved
    or ₹2 crore, whichever is <em>higher</em>). The <strong>200-person</strong> figure is
    <strong>rules-level</strong> — s. 42(2) says "fifty or such higher number as may be prescribed",
    and <strong>rule 14</strong> prescribes 200 per financial year per kind of security. The
    <strong>form names</strong> — PAS-4 for the offer-cum-application letter, PAS-5 for the record of
    offers, PAS-3 for the return of allotment — and the retention of PAS-5 itself are rules-level and
    have been amended. Verify all of these for your attempt.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 16. Global depository receipts — section 41

**Section 41** is one sentence and is worth exactly the marks it takes to state it: a company may,
**after passing a special resolution in its general meeting**, **issue depository receipts in any
foreign country** in such manner, and subject to such conditions, as may be **prescribed**.

Three points carry the marks:

1. **The approver is the members by special resolution** — not the Board, not the Central Government,
   not SEBI.
2. The instrument is a **depository receipt issued in a foreign country** against underlying shares
   deposited with a domestic custodian; the **overseas depository** issues the receipts.
3. Everything else — the eligible companies, the manner and the conditions — is **prescribed by
   rule**, and is therefore amendment-sensitive.

<Callout kind="amendment" label="AMENDMENT-CHECK · the s. 41 rules">
  <p>
    The <strong>Companies (Issue of Global Depository Receipts) Rules 2014</strong> supply the
    machinery, and the framework interacts with the Depository Receipts Scheme notified by the Central
    Government and with FEMA. The <strong>special resolution</strong> requirement is in the section and
    is safe to state; the machinery is not. Nothing in this chapter's question bank turns on the rules.
  </p>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

1. **Using the wrong base for a percentage.** **Section 39(2)** measures minimum application money
   against the **nominal (face) amount**; **rule 13** measures underwriting commission against the
   **price at which the securities are issued**. Swapping the bases is the single most costly slip in
   this chapter.
2. **Starting the section 42 interest clock at the wrong date.** Interest at 12% per annum runs from
   the **expiry of the sixtieth day**, not from the end of the further fifteen-day repayment window.
   The 15 days is a grace period for repayment, not a holiday from interest.
3. **Counting QIBs and ESOP employees towards the 200.** Both are **excluded**. So are they excluded
   from the private-placement machinery? No — the offer still has to comply; they simply do not
   consume the cap.
4. **Treating the 200-person cap as a per-offer cap.** It is **per financial year, in the aggregate**,
   and per kind of security. Four offers of 60 persons each in one year breach it.
5. **Confusing section 27 with section 13(8).** Section 27 varies the **terms of a contract referred
   to in the prospectus or the objects for which the prospectus was issued**, at any time; s. 13(8)
   alters the **objects clause of the memorandum** where prospectus money is still **unutilised**.
6. **Confusing section 34 with section 35.** Section 34 is **criminal** (s. 447) and needs no proof of
   reliance; s. 35 is **civil compensation** and requires that the subscriber **acted on** the
   statement and **sustained loss**.
7. **Forgetting that section 36 needs no prospectus.** It catches false statements, promises and
   forecasts made to induce investment **or to obtain bank credit**, in any form.
8. **Putting the section 38 liability on the company's officers.** Section 38 punishes the
   **applicant** who applies in a fictitious name or makes multiple applications; the company is the
   victim. Its distinctive remedies are **disgorgement**, **seizure and disposal**, and credit of the
   proceeds to the **Investor Education and Protection Fund**.
9. **Running the 90-day prospectus clock from the wrong event.** It runs from the date a copy was
   **delivered to the Registrar for registration** — not from Board approval, not from registration.
10. **Running the shelf-prospectus year from the date of filing.** It runs from the **date of opening
    of the first offer**.
11. **Saying the final prospectus after a red herring goes only to the Registrar.** It goes to the
    **Registrar and SEBI**.
12. **Allowing renunciation on a private placement.** Section 42(3) proviso: the offer and application
    **carries no right of renunciation**. Renunciation belongs to a **rights issue** under s. 62.
13. **Using private-placement money on allotment.** Not enough — the money may be used only once the
    allotment is made **and the return of allotment has been filed** (s. 42(4) proviso).
14. **Answering that a private company may make a public offer if it converts later.** Section 23
    gives a private company only **private placement** and **rights or bonus issues**.

## One-page revision summary

**Section 23 — the routes.** Public company: **public offer through a prospectus**, **private
placement (s. 42)**, or **rights/bonus (s. 62)**. Private company: **private placement** or
**rights/bonus** only. "Public offer" includes an **IPO, an FPO, and an offer for sale by an existing
shareholder** through the company.

**Section 2(70) — prospectus.** Any document **described or issued as a prospectus**, plus a **red
herring** (s. 32), a **shelf** (s. 31), and any **notice, circular, advertisement or other document
inviting offers from the public** for the subscription or purchase of securities. It is an
**invitation to offer**; the applicant offers, the company accepts by **allotment**.

**The four kinds.** **Abridged** (s. 33) — salient features specified by SEBI, **must accompany every
application form**; exceptions for an underwriting invitation and where securities are not offered to
the public; full prospectus on request before closing; **₹50,000 per default**. **Shelf** (s. 31) — a
**SEBI-prescribed class**, filed at the **first offer**, valid up to **one year from the opening of
the first offer**, with an **information memorandum** before each later offer disclosing new charges
and changes in financial position; applicants with advance money who wish to withdraw get the
**whole** of it back. **Red herring** (s. 32) — no quantum or price; filed with the Registrar **at
least 3 days** before the offer opens; **same obligations as a prospectus**; on closing, the final
prospectus with total capital raised and closing price goes to the **Registrar and SEBI**.
**Deemed** (ss. 25, 28) — an offer for sale by an **issuing house** or by **existing members in
consultation with the Board** is a prospectus **issued by the company**; presumption if the offer is
within **6 months** of the allotment **or** the whole consideration is not yet received; extra
contents = **net consideration** plus place and time for inspecting the contract; signed by **two
directors** (company) or **half the partners** (firm); under s. 28(3) the members **reimburse the
company's expenses**.

**Section 26.** Dated and signed; identity, offer details (opening/closing dates, separate bank
account under s. 40(3), use of earlier issue money, capital structure, main objects, minimum
subscription, premium, allotment schedule, authority for the issue), risk factors, gestation period,
and **litigation by a Government Department or statutory body in the last 5 years against the
promoter**; auditor's reports on profits and losses and assets and liabilities. **s. 26(3)** —
independent **expert's written consent**, not withdrawn before delivery. **s. 26(4)** — a copy signed
by **every director/proposed director** delivered to the Registrar **on or before publication**.
**s. 26(7)** — the Registrar registers only with the written consents of the named auditor, legal
adviser, attorney, solicitor, banker and broker. **s. 26(8)** — **not valid if issued more than 90
days after delivery**.

**Section 27.** Vary the terms of a prospectus contract or the objects only by **special resolution**;
notice details in **two newspapers** (English + vernacular of the registered-office city) **and the
website**, with **justification**; **no use of prospectus money to deal in equity shares of another
listed company**; **dissenting shareholders get an exit from promoters/controlling shareholders at a
SEBI-specified price**.

**Sections 29–30.** Every company making a **public offer** and prescribed classes issue **only in
demat**; prescribed **unlisted** classes also **hold and transfer** only in demat; others may choose.
An **advertisement of a prospectus** must specify the memorandum's **objects, members' liability and
share capital**, the **signatories and their shares**, and the **capital structure**.

**Misstatement map.**

| Section | Nature | Who | Key point |
|---|---|---|---|
| **34** | criminal, **s. 447** | every person who **authorises the issue** | defence: **immaterial** or **reasonable belief up to the time of issue** |
| **35** | civil **compensation** | company; **director at issue**; person **named** as director/agreed to be; **promoter**; **authoriser**; **expert** | claimant must have **acted on** the statement and **sustained loss**; defences: **consent withdrawn before issue**, or **no knowledge + forthwith public notice**; **s. 35(3)** fraud → **unlimited personal liability** |
| **36** | **s. 447** | any person | **no prospectus needed**; covers securities agreements, yield/fluctuation agreements and **bank credit facilities** |
| **37** | standing | **any person, group or association affected** | class action under **s. 34, 35 or 36** |
| **38** | **s. 447** | the **applicant** | fictitious name, **multiple applications**, inducing allotment/transfer in a fictitious name; **reproduce in every prospectus and application form**; **disgorgement**, **seizure and disposal**, proceeds to the **IEPF** |

**Section 39 — public allotment.** No allotment unless the **minimum amount** stated in the prospectus
is **subscribed** and the application sums **received**. Application money **≥ 5% of the NOMINAL
amount** (SEBI may specify higher). If not received within **30 days from the date of issue of the
prospectus**, the money is **returned as prescribed** (rules: within **15 days of the closure of the
issue**). Return of allotment filed with the Registrar (rules: **PAS-3 within 30 days** of allotment).
Default under (3) or (4): **₹1,000 per day or ₹1,00,000, whichever is less**, per default, on the
company and the officer in default.

**Section 40 — listing and money.** Apply to one or more **recognised stock exchanges** and obtain
permission **before** the public offer; **name the exchanges** in the prospectus; keep application
money in a **separate scheduled-bank account**, usable only for **adjustment against allotment** (once
permission is obtained) or **repayment**; any **waiver clause is void**; default carries a **fine on
the company and imprisonment or fine on officers**. **s. 40(6)** — commission as prescribed: articles
must authorise it; out of issue proceeds or profits; **shares ≤ 5%** and **debentures ≤ 2.5% of the
issue price**, or the articles' rate, **whichever is less**; disclosed in the prospectus; **none on
securities not offered to the public**.

**Section 42 — private placement.** **Identified persons** chosen by the **Board**; **≤ 200 in a
financial year**, aggregate, per kind of security, **excluding QIBs and ESOP employees**; exceed it
and **Explanation III deems it a public offer**. Offer in the prescribed **offer-cum-application**
(PAS-4), names and addresses recorded, **no right of renunciation**. Money by **banking channel, never
cash**, into a **separate scheduled-bank account**; **not usable until allotment is made AND the
return is filed**. **No fresh offer** while an earlier one is pending, unless withdrawn or abandoned.
**Allot within 60 days** of receipt of application money; else **repay within 15 days** of that
expiry; else **12% p.a. from the expiry of the sixtieth day**. **No public advertisement or media,
marketing or distribution channels or agents.** **Return of allotment within 15 days** of allotment
with the full list of allottees. **s. 42(9)** — ₹1,000 per day, **max ₹25 lakh**, on company,
promoters and directors. **s. 42(10)** — penalty up to the **amount raised or ₹2 crore, whichever is
lower**, plus refund with s. 42(6) interest **within 30 days of the order**.

**Section 41 — GDRs.** A company may issue **depository receipts in any foreign country** after a
**special resolution**, in the prescribed manner and on the prescribed conditions.
