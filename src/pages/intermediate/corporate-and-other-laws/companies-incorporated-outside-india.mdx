Helios Cloudworks Pte Ltd is incorporated in Singapore. It has no Indian subsidiary, no Indian
shareholders and no Indian directors. In March it signs a lease on two floors of an office block in
Bengaluru, moves in forty engineers, prints letterhead, and starts billing its Singapore parent for
the work done there. Its finance head is confident that nothing in the Companies Act 2013 touches
the company, because the company was never registered in India.

He is wrong, and the error costs a fine on the company plus a fine on every officer in default. From
the day those two floors opened, Helios Cloudworks became a **foreign company** under s. 2(42), and
Chapter XXII gave it **thirty days** to deliver its charter, its directors' particulars and the name
of a person resident in India who can accept service, to the Registrar.

That is the whole chapter in a sentence: **Indian company law does not care where you were born; it
cares where you set up shop.** For the exam this is a **definition-plus-clocks chapter**. The marks
sit in three places — whether the definition is satisfied at all (three limbs, all of which must
hold), which clock runs from which event (30 days · 30 days · 6 months · 60 days), and which
*other* provisions of the Act are dragged in by s. 384. Learn those and the rest is recall.

## 1. The definition of a foreign company — section 2(42)

**Section 2(42)** — a "foreign company" means **any company or body corporate incorporated outside
India** which **(a)** has a **place of business in India**, whether by itself or through an agent,
physically or through electronic mode; **and (b)** **conducts any business activity in India** in
any other manner.

Three limbs, and the conjunction between (a) and (b) is **"and"**, not "or":

| Limb | What it requires | Where students go wrong |
|---|---|---|
| **1 · Incorporated outside India** | a company **or body corporate** registered under the law of another country | the entity need not be a "company" in the Indian sense — any body corporate qualifies |
| **2 · Place of business in India** | **by itself or through an agent**, and **physically or through electronic mode** | an agent's office counts; a server-based presence can count; a one-off visit does not |
| **3 · Conducts business activity in India** | actual **business activity**, in any manner | a dormant address with no activity fails this limb |

<div class="formula">Foreign company = <strong>incorporated outside India</strong> + <strong>place of business in India</strong> (itself / agent · physically / electronically) + <strong>conducts business activity in India</strong>. Miss any one limb and Chapter XXII does not apply at all.</div>

**"Place of business."** The Act defines it only in **s. 386(c)**, and inclusively: place of business
**includes a share transfer or registration office**. So a foreign company that keeps nothing in
India but a registrar-and-transfer office for its own securities has a place of business here. The
Rules add the mirror image for the electronic route.

**"Electronic mode."** Rule 2(1)(c) of the Companies (Registration of Foreign Companies) Rules 2014
defines electronic mode very widely — carrying out electronically, **whether the main server is
installed in India or not**, business-to-business and business-to-consumer transactions, data
interchange, digital supply transactions, offering to accept deposits or subscriptions in India or
from citizens of India, financial settlements, web-based marketing, advisory and transactional
services, database services, supply-chain management, online services such as telemarketing,
telecommuting, telemedicine, education and information research, and all related data communication
services, whether by e-mail, mobile devices, social media, cloud computing, document management or
voice or data transmission.

<Callout kind="amendment" label="AMENDMENT-CHECK · the electronic-mode definition is rules-level and very broad">
  <p>
    Read literally, Rule 2(1)(c) would catch a great many overseas websites that transact with Indian
    customers, which is why the words <strong>"whether the main server is installed in India or
    not"</strong> matter so much: the <em>location of the server is not the test</em>. This definition
    sits in the <strong>Rules</strong>, not the Act, and has been amended. Answer the
    <em>section</em> first (s. 2(42) — place of business physically or through electronic mode), then
    cite the Rule for the detail, and re-check the Rule text against the edition notified for your
    attempt.
  </p>
</Callout>

**What is not a place of business.** An **isolated transaction** concluded in India — a single
export sale negotiated by post or e-mail, a director attending one meeting, a one-off consignment
sold through an independent buyer — does not by itself establish a place of business. The idea the
definition reaches is a **settled, continuing presence** from which business is done, coupled with
actual business activity. The distinction is the SM's, and it is a matter of degree: a leased
office, a branch, a liaison office, a project office or an agent's standing establishment are on the
wrong side of the line for the foreign company.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>All three limbs must hold — and the commonest exam trap drops the third.</strong> A
    company incorporated in Dubai that leases a flat in Mumbai as a guest house for visiting staff,
    and does no business from it, has a presence but <em>no business activity</em>: limb 3 fails and it
    is not a foreign company. Equally, a company incorporated in Germany that sells into India purely
    by shipping goods against orders placed from Germany, with no office, agent or electronic presence
    here, has business <em>with</em> India but <strong>no place of business in India</strong>: limb 2
    fails. Options that say "any company incorporated outside India that does business with India" are
    always wrong — they collapse three limbs into one.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 1 — three companies, one definition">
  <p>
    <strong>(i)</strong> Nordwind GmbH (Germany) appoints an independent Indian distributor who buys
    goods on his own account and resells them. <strong>(ii)</strong> Kestrel Analytics Inc (USA) keeps
    no premises in India but runs a subscription analytics platform sold to 4,000 Indian businesses,
    with its servers in Virginia and a support team reachable from India. <strong>(iii)</strong>
    Sabari Holdings Ltd (Mauritius) keeps a share transfer office in Chennai for its own listed
    securities and does nothing else here.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>(i) — not a foreign company.</strong> An independent distributor buying and reselling on
    his own account is not the company's <em>agent</em>, and the company has no place of business
    here. <strong>(ii) — foreign company.</strong> Limb 2 is satisfied through <strong>electronic
    mode</strong>; Rule 2(1)(c) expressly says the location of the main server is irrelevant, and
    business-to-business transactions and online services are named. Limb 3 is plainly satisfied.
    <strong>(iii) — foreign company.</strong> Section 386(c) makes a <strong>share transfer or
    registration office</strong> a place of business, and maintaining the register for its own
    securities is business activity conducted in India.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Which provisions apply, and to whom.** **Section 379(1)** — **ss. 380 to 386 and ss. 392 and 393
apply to all foreign companies**. Note what is *not* in that list: ss. 387 to 391 are not confined
to foreign companies at all. They bite on **any person** who issues a prospectus in India for a
company incorporated outside India, whether or not that company has a place of business here (see
§8).

## 2. Indian-held foreign companies — section 379

**Section 379(2)** raises the stakes for a foreign company that is substantially Indian-owned.

> Where **not less than fifty per cent of the paid-up share capital** — whether **equity or
> preference or partly equity and partly preference** — of a company incorporated outside India and
> having a place of business in India is held by **one or more citizens of India** or by **one or
> more companies or bodies corporate incorporated in India**, or by **any combination of them**,
> whether **singly or in the aggregate**, such company shall comply with **the provisions of this
> Chapter** and **such other provisions of this Act as may be prescribed**, with regard to the
> business carried on by it in India, **as if it were a company incorporated in India**.

Four things to fix in memory:

1. **The threshold is "not less than 50%"** — so **exactly 50% is enough**. "More than 50%" is a
   wrong reading, and it is the standard distractor.
2. **The base is paid-up share capital**, not voting power, not net worth, not turnover — and it is
   **equity or preference or partly both**, so preference capital held by an Indian body corporate
   counts towards the numerator and the denominator alike.
3. **Who counts:** **citizens of India**, and **companies or bodies corporate incorporated in
   India**. A person of Indian origin who is not a citizen does not count. A company incorporated
   abroad does not count, however Indian its ownership.
4. **Singly or in the aggregate** — you add every qualifying holder together. One Indian citizen
   holding 6%, another holding 9%, and an Indian company holding 35% together cross the line.

<div class="formula">s. 379(2) test = <strong>(Indian citizens' paid-up capital + Indian-incorporated bodies corporate's paid-up capital) ÷ total paid-up share capital ≥ 50%</strong> — equity and preference both counted, holders aggregated.</div>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 2 — running the 50% aggregation">
  <p>
    Aurelia Systems Inc, incorporated in Delaware, has a branch office in Hyderabad. Its paid-up
    capital is ₹40,00,000 of equity and ₹10,00,000 of preference. The equity is held as to
    ₹12,00,000 by three Indian citizens, ₹9,00,000 by Sundar Infotech Private Limited (incorporated
    in India) and ₹19,00,000 by its Delaware parent. The preference is held as to ₹4,00,000 by
    Sundar Infotech Private Limited and ₹6,00,000 by a Cayman fund.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — the s. 379(2) aggregation</div>
    Total paid-up share capital = ₹40,00,000 (equity) + ₹10,00,000 (preference) = <strong>₹50,00,000</strong><br />
    Indian citizens' holding = ₹12,00,000<br />
    Indian-incorporated body corporate = ₹9,00,000 (equity) + ₹4,00,000 (preference) = ₹13,00,000<br />
    Qualifying aggregate = ₹12,00,000 + ₹13,00,000 = <strong>₹25,00,000</strong><br />
    ₹25,00,000 ÷ ₹50,00,000 = <strong>50%</strong> → "not less than 50%" is <strong>satisfied</strong>
  </div>
  <p>
    So Aurelia Systems must comply with Chapter XXII <em>and</em> with such other provisions of the
    Act as are prescribed, <strong>as if it were a company incorporated in India</strong>, with regard
    to its Indian business. Two traps sit in this working. First, dropping the <strong>preference
    capital</strong> from either the numerator or the denominator: on equity alone the Indian share
    would be ₹21,00,000 of ₹40,00,000 = 52.5%, a different number reached by the wrong route.
    Second, reading the threshold as "more than 50%" and concluding that an exact half escapes — it
    does not.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Section 379(2) does not turn the foreign company into an Indian company.</strong> It is
    incorporated abroad, it stays incorporated abroad, and it is still a foreign company for s. 2(42).
    What changes is the <em>measure of compliance</em>: it must comply <strong>as if</strong> it were
    an Indian company, and only <strong>with regard to the business carried on by it in India</strong>.
    Options saying it "shall be deemed to be incorporated in India", or that it must register afresh
    under s. 7, or that it is required to convert into an Indian subsidiary, are all wrong. Also note
    the second half of the sub-section — "and such other provisions of this Act <em>as may be
    prescribed</em>" — which makes the reach of the extra compliance a <strong>rules-level</strong>
    question.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. Documents to be delivered on establishing a place of business — section 380

**Section 380(1)** — every foreign company which **establishes a place of business in India** must,
**within thirty days of the establishment**, deliver to the **Registrar** for registration:

1. a **certified copy of the charter, statutes or memorandum and articles** of the company, or other
   instrument constituting or defining its constitution — and, if the instrument is **not in
   English**, a **certified translation** in English;
2. the **full address of the registered or principal office** of the company;
3. a **list of the directors and secretary** of the company, with the prescribed particulars;
4. the **name and address of one or more persons resident in India** authorised to **accept on
   behalf of the company service of process** and any notices or other documents required to be
   served on the company;
5. the **full address of the office of the company in India** which is deemed to be its **principal
   place of business** in India;
6. **particulars of the opening and closing of a place of business in India on any earlier
   occasion or occasions**;
7. a **declaration** that **none of the directors** of the company **or the authorised
   representative in India** has ever been **convicted or debarred** from formation of companies and
   management in India or abroad; and
8. **any other prescribed information**.

**Section 380(3)** — where any **alteration** is made or occurs in the above particulars, the
company must deliver to the Registrar, **within thirty days of the alteration**, a return containing
the particulars of the alteration.

<div class="formula">s. 380(1): documents to the Registrar within <strong>30 days of establishing the place of business</strong> — <strong>Form FC-1</strong>. s. 380(3): any alteration in those particulars within <strong>30 days of the alteration</strong> — <strong>Form FC-2</strong>.</div>

<Callout kind="amendment" label="AMENDMENT-CHECK · FC-1 and FC-2 are rules-level">
  <p>
    The <strong>thirty-day periods and the list of documents are in the section</strong> and are what
    an examiner marks. The <strong>form numbers FC-1 and FC-2</strong>, the attachments, the fee, the
    manner of certification of copies and translations, and the requirement of prior regulatory
    approval where applicable, are all in the <strong>Companies (Registration of Foreign Companies)
    Rules 2014</strong> and change. Cite the section; mention the form only as the vehicle.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 3 — the FC-1 clock, dated">
  <p>
    Helios Cloudworks Pte Ltd (Singapore) takes possession of its Bengaluru premises and begins
    operations on <strong>9 March 2026</strong>. On <strong>6 November 2026</strong> it replaces the
    person resident in India authorised to accept service of process.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — count in days from the trigger, excluding the trigger day</div>
    s. 380(1) · establishment 9 Mar 2026 + 30 days = <strong>8 April 2026</strong> (FC-1)<br />
    s. 380(3) · alteration 6 Nov 2026 + 30 days = <strong>6 December 2026</strong> (FC-2)
  </div>
  <p>
    Note the two commonest wrong answers. <strong>9 April 2026</strong> comes from reading "thirty
    days" as "one calendar month" — the section says days. <strong>8 May 2026</strong> comes from
    borrowing the sixty-day figure that belongs to the <em>annual return</em> in Rule 7. And the
    clock starts from the <strong>establishment of the place of business</strong>, not from the date
    the lease was signed, not from the date the company was incorporated in Singapore, and not from
    the date the first invoice was raised.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>The authorised person is the hinge of the whole Chapter.</strong> Item 4 of the s. 380(1)
    list is not administrative housekeeping — it is what makes the foreign company reachable.
    <strong>Section 383</strong> then provides that service on that person, at the address delivered
    under s. 380, is <strong>sufficient service on the company</strong>. A foreign company that never
    files FC-1 has not thereby made itself unserveable; it has simply committed an offence under
    s. 392, and under <strong>s. 393</strong> it cannot bring a suit in India until it complies.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Accounts, audit and the annual return — section 381

**Section 381(1)** — every foreign company must, in **every calendar year**, **make out a balance
sheet and a profit and loss account** in the prescribed form, containing the prescribed particulars
and including or having annexed the prescribed documents, and **deliver a copy to the Registrar**.
**Section 381(2)** brings in, along with those documents, the prescribed documents relating to the
company's **latest consolidated financial statements** and other statements as prescribed.
**Section 381(3)** — if any of the documents is **not in English**, a **certified translation** must
accompany it. **Section 381(4)** — the company must send with those documents **a list, in the
prescribed form, of all the places of business established by it in India as at the date of the
balance sheet**.

The Rules supply the machinery, and this is where the two examinable practical points sit:

| Requirement | Position | Level |
|---|---|---|
| Form and content of the financial statements | as far as may be, in respect of its **Indian business operations**, in accordance with **Schedule III** as applicable | Act + Rules |
| **Audit** of the accounts of the Indian business operations | by a **practising chartered accountant in India**, or a **firm or LLP of practising chartered accountants** | **Rules-level** |
| Filing of the financial statements with the Registrar, with the list of places of business | **Form FC-3**, within **six months of the close of the financial year** (extendable by up to **three months** at the Registrar's discretion, for special reasons, on the company's application) | **Rules-level** |
| **Annual return** | **Form FC-4**, within **sixty days from the last day of its financial year** | **Rules-level (s. 384(2) is the enabling provision)** |

<div class="formula">Accounts clock: <strong>FC-3 within 6 months of the close of the financial year</strong> (+ up to 3 months on the Registrar's extension). Annual-return clock: <strong>FC-4 within 60 days from the last day of the financial year</strong>. Two different clocks off the <em>same</em> event.</div>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 4 — two clocks off one year-end">
  <p>
    Aurelia Systems Inc closes its financial year on <strong>31 March 2027</strong> in respect of its
    Indian business.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — both periods run from the last day of the financial year</div>
    Annual return · 31 Mar 2027 + 60 days = <strong>30 May 2027</strong> (FC-4)<br />
    Financial statements · 31 Mar 2027 + 6 months = <strong>30 September 2027</strong> (FC-3)<br />
    Extension, if the Registrar allows it on special grounds = up to <strong>31 December 2027</strong>
  </div>
  <p>
    Students routinely swap the two. The mnemonic that survives the exam hall: the
    <strong>return</strong> is the <strong>shorter</strong> clock (60 days), the
    <strong>accounts</strong> the longer (6 months), because the accounts have to be audited first.
    And 30 May is not "two months" — 31 March plus sixty days lands on 30 May 2027, not 31 May.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="amendment" label="AMENDMENT-CHECK · FC-3, FC-4, the audit and the six-month window are all rules-level">
  <p>
    <strong>Section 381 itself fixes no date.</strong> The six-month filing window, the three-month
    extension, the requirement that the Indian business accounts be audited by a
    <strong>practising chartered accountant in India</strong>, and the sixty-day annual-return window
    all come from the <strong>Companies (Registration of Foreign Companies) Rules 2014</strong>.
    Rules-level periods have been changed before. State the section for the obligation and the Rule
    for the period, and verify the Rule against the material notified for your attempt.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Display of name, service of documents and fees — sections 382, 383 and 385

**Section 382 — display of name.** Every foreign company must:

- **(a)** **conspicuously exhibit** on the **outside of every office or place** where it carries on
  business in India, the **name of the company** and the **country in which it is incorporated**, in
  letters easily legible in **English characters**, and **also in the characters of the language or
  one of the languages in general use in that locality**;
- **(b)** cause the **name of the company** and the **country of incorporation** to be stated in
  legible English characters in **all business letters, bill-heads and letter paper**, and in **all
  notices and other official publications** of the company; and
- **(c)** if the **liability of the members is limited**, cause **notice of that fact** to be stated
  in **every prospectus** issued and in **all business letters, bill-heads, letter paper, notices,
  advertisements and other official publications** in legible English characters, and to be
  **conspicuously exhibited on the outside of every office or place** where it carries on business
  in India, in legible English characters **and** in the characters of the local language.

<div class="formula">s. 382 display = <strong>name + country of incorporation</strong>, and where liability is limited, <strong>notice of limited liability</strong> as well — outside every place of business in <strong>English + local language</strong>, and on every business letter and official publication in <strong>English</strong>.</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>The country of incorporation is half the requirement — do not drop it.</strong> Compare
    s. 12(3), where an Indian company displays its <em>name and registered-office address</em>. A
    foreign company displays its <strong>name and the country in which it is incorporated</strong>.
    That is the whole point of the section: a person dealing with the Bengaluru office must be able to
    see, from the signboard, that he is dealing with a Singapore company. And note the language split
    — the <strong>local-language requirement attaches to the signboard outside the premises</strong>
    (and to the limited-liability notice), not to ordinary business letters, which need only English
    characters.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 383 — service on a foreign company.** Any process, notice or other document required to be
served on a foreign company is **deemed to be sufficiently served** if it is **addressed to any
person whose name and address have been delivered to the Registrar under s. 380** and **left at, or
sent by post to, that address**, or **sent by electronic mode**. The section works only because
s. 380(1) made the company file that name and address — the two provisions are a pair.

**Section 385 — fee for registration of documents.** There must be paid to the Registrar, for
registering any document required by the provisions of this Chapter to be registered by him, **such
fee as may be prescribed**. Nothing else is examinable in this section; the amount is rules-level.

## 6. Which other provisions of the Act apply — section 384

Section 384 is the bridge. It takes five blocks of the Act written for Indian companies and applies
them, with modifications, to foreign companies. Learn it as a **mapping table**, because that is how
it is examined.

| s. 384 | Provision pulled in | How it applies to a foreign company |
|---|---|---|
| **384(1)** | **s. 71 — debentures** | applies **mutatis mutandis** to a foreign company |
| **384(2)** | **s. 92 — annual return** | applies **subject to such exceptions, modifications and adaptations as may be prescribed** — this is the enabling power behind the **FC-4** return filed within **60 days** of the financial-year end |
| **384(3)** | **s. 128 — books of account** | applies **with respect to the business carried on by the foreign company in India**, as it applies to an Indian company |
| **384(4)** | **Chapter VI — ss. 77 to 87, registration of charges** | applies **mutatis mutandis** to **charges on properties which are created or acquired by any foreign company in India** |
| **384(5)** | **Chapter XIV — ss. 206 to 229, inspection, inquiry and investigation** | applies **mutatis mutandis** to the foreign company |

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>Two boundary lines run through this table, and both are examined.</strong> The first is
    <em>territorial</em>: ss. 128 and 384(4) reach only the <strong>Indian</strong> business and
    <strong>property in India</strong>. A charge created by a Singapore company over its Singapore
    factory is nothing to do with the Indian Registrar; a charge over its Bengaluru premises must be
    registered under Chapter VI within the ordinary <strong>thirty-day</strong> window, with the same
    60-day and 120-day extension windows and the same consequence of non-registration — the charge is
    void against the liquidator and other creditors. The second line is <em>what is left out</em>:
    s. 384 does <strong>not</strong> pull in the general-meeting provisions (s. 96 annual general
    meeting), the board-composition and directors provisions, the auditor-appointment machinery of
    s. 139, the dividend chapter, or the CSR provision. Only what s. 384 names is imported.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 5 — the s. 384 mapping applied">
  <p>
    Meridian Cloudworks Pte Ltd, a foreign company with a Pune office, does four things in one year:
    it issues debentures to Indian investors; it mortgages its Pune office building to a bank; it
    keeps the books of its Indian operations on a server in Singapore; and it holds no annual general
    meeting anywhere.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>Debentures</strong> — s. 71 applies mutatis mutandis (s. 384(1)). <strong>The
    mortgage</strong> — the property is <em>in India</em>, so Chapter VI applies mutatis mutandis
    (s. 384(4)) and the charge must be registered with the Registrar within thirty days of creation.
    <strong>The books</strong> — s. 128 applies to the business carried on <em>in India</em>
    (s. 384(3)), so the books of the Indian operations must be kept and maintained as s. 128 requires;
    the location and the conditions for keeping them electronically are governed by s. 128 and its
    rules. <strong>No AGM</strong> — <strong>no contravention under this Chapter</strong>: s. 96 is
    not among the provisions s. 384 imports. That last one is the mark students lose, because the
    instinct is to say every Indian-company obligation applies.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. Interpretation — section 386

Section 386 is three definitions, and each has been examined:

- **"certified"** means **certified in the prescribed manner** to be a **true copy** or a **correct
  translation** — so certification is a rules-level formality, not the company's own say-so;
- **"director"**, in relation to a foreign company, **includes any person in accordance with whose
  directions or instructions the Board of Directors of the company is accustomed to act** — the
  shadow-director extension, which matters because s. 380(1) requires a list of directors and
  s. 380(1)(g) a declaration about them;
- **"place of business"** **includes a share transfer or registration office**.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>The s. 386(b) definition is inclusive, and it is wider than s. 2(34).</strong> For an
    Indian company, "director" means a director appointed to the Board. For a
    <strong>foreign</strong> company, a person who never sits on the Board but whose instructions the
    Board is accustomed to follow <em>is</em> a director for Chapter XXII — so his particulars belong
    in the s. 380 list and he is caught by the s. 380(1)(g) declaration and by "officer in default"
    for s. 392. Note also the qualification the courts read into this kind of provision: a person
    giving advice merely in a professional capacity is not brought in by it.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. Prospectus and Indian Depository Receipts — sections 387 to 390

These four sections are the **capital-raising half** of the Chapter, and they have a different
target. They bite on **any person** who issues, circulates or distributes a prospectus in India for
a company incorporated outside India — **whether or not that company has established, or when
formed will establish, a place of business in India**. So a company that is not a "foreign company"
at all can be caught here.

**Section 387 — dating of the prospectus and particulars in it.** No person may issue, circulate or
distribute in India any **prospectus offering to subscribe for securities** of a company
incorporated outside India unless the prospectus is **dated** and **signed**, and contains
particulars of:

- **(a)** the **instrument constituting or defining the constitution** of the company;
- **(b)** the **enactments, or provisions having the force of an enactment**, under which the
  company was incorporated;
- **(c)** an **address in India** where the said instrument, enactments or provisions, or copies
  thereof, **can be inspected** (and, if not in English, a certified translation);
- **(d)** the **date on which and the country in which** the company was **incorporated**; and
- **(e)** whether the company has **established a place of business in India** and, if so, the
  **address of its principal office in India**;

and states the prescribed matters and sets out the prescribed reports. Two exceptions: the section
does **not** apply to a prospectus issued to **existing members or debenture-holders** of the
company, whether or not the applicant has a right to renounce; nor to a prospectus relating to
securities **which are or are to be in all respects uniform** with securities **previously issued
and for the time being dealt in or quoted on a recognised stock exchange**.

**Section 388 — expert's consent and allotment.** No person may issue in India a prospectus
containing a statement purporting to be made by an **expert** unless the expert has given, and has
**not withdrawn**, his **written consent** to the issue of the prospectus with the statement in the
form and context in which it appears, and a **statement to that effect appears in the prospectus**;
and unless the prospectus states on its face that a copy has been **delivered for registration** as
required by s. 389. The section also fixes the particulars a prospectus must contain as to
**application and allotment**.

**Section 389 — registration of the prospectus.** No person may issue, circulate or distribute in
India a prospectus offering for subscription securities of a company incorporated outside India
unless, **before the issue, circulation or distribution**, a copy of the prospectus **certified by
the chairperson of the company and two other directors** as having been approved by resolution of
the **managing body**, has been **delivered for registration to the Registrar**; and unless the
prospectus **states on its face** that a copy has been so delivered, with the prescribed documents
endorsed on or attached to it — the **consent of any expert** named in it, a **copy of every
material contract**, and the other prescribed particulars.

<div class="formula">Order of operations for an overseas issue in India: <strong>expert's written consent (s. 388) → certified copy delivered for registration to the Registrar (s. 389) → only then may the prospectus be issued, circulated or distributed</strong>. Registration is a <em>precondition</em>, never a follow-up filing.</div>

**Section 390 — offer of Indian Depository Receipts.** The **Central Government may make rules** as
to **(a)** the offer of **Indian Depository Receipts**; **(b)** the requirement of **disclosures** in
the prospectus or letter of offer issued in connection with them; **(c)** the manner in which IDRs
are to be **dealt with in a depository mode** and by **custodian and underwriters**; and **(d)** the
manner of **sale, transfer or transmission** of IDRs — **by a company incorporated or to be
incorporated outside India, whether or not it has established, or will establish, any place of
business in India**. An IDR is, in substance, an instrument issued in India by a domestic custodian
against the underlying equity of a company incorporated abroad, allowing Indian investors to take
economic exposure to a foreign issuer in rupees. The detail is rules-level.

<Callout kind="amendment" label="AMENDMENT-CHECK · s. 390 is an enabling section only">
  <p>
    Section 390 confers a <strong>rule-making power</strong>; it states no substantive condition of
    its own. Every operative requirement for IDRs — eligibility, minimum issue size, disclosure,
    depository and custodian mechanics, transfer and redemption — lives in the
    <strong>Rules</strong> and in the SEBI framework, and both move. Answer s. 390 as an
    <em>enabling</em> provision and cite the four heads (a) to (d); do not recite rules-level
    thresholds.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Misstatements, closure and punishment — sections 391 and 392

**Section 391(1)** — the provisions of **ss. 34 to 36** apply to **(i)** the issue of a prospectus by
a company incorporated outside India under **s. 389**, and **(ii)** the issue of **Indian Depository
Receipts** by a foreign company. So the whole misstatement code follows the prospectus into India:

- **s. 34 — criminal liability for misstatements in a prospectus**: where a prospectus includes any
  statement which is **untrue or misleading in form or context**, or where any **inclusion or
  omission** of any matter is likely to mislead, every person who **authorises the issue** is liable
  under **s. 447** (fraud) — unless he proves the statement or omission was **immaterial**, or that
  he had **reasonable grounds to believe, and did up to the time of issue believe**, that it was
  true or that the omission was necessary;
- **s. 35 — civil liability**: compensation to every person who **subscribes** for securities on the
  faith of the prospectus and **sustains loss or damage**, payable by the company and by its
  directors, promoters, persons named as directors, experts and persons who authorised the issue,
  with the statutory defences (withdrawal of consent before issue, issue without knowledge, honest
  belief on reasonable grounds); and
- **s. 36 — punishment for fraudulently inducing persons to invest money**, again attracting
  **s. 447**.

**Section 391(2)** — **notwithstanding anything in s. 376**, the provisions of **Chapter XX (winding
up)** apply **mutatis mutandis** for the **closure of the place of business of a foreign company in
India**, **as if it were a company incorporated in India**.

<Callout kind="amendment" label="AMENDMENT-CHECK · s. 391(2) was substituted">
  <p>
    As originally enacted, s. 391(2) applied Chapter XX only to a foreign company
    <strong>which had issued a prospectus or IDRs in India</strong>. The
    <strong>Companies (Amendment) Act 2017</strong> substituted the sub-section with the present text,
    which is not limited in that way and opens with a non obstante clause referring to
    <strong>s. 376</strong> (winding up of an unregistered company that has been carrying on business
    in India and has ceased to do so). Older banks and older editions still state the narrower
    position. Verify the current text before relying on either.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 392 — punishment for contravention.** Without prejudice to s. 391, if a **foreign company
contravenes any provision of this Chapter**:

| Who | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **The foreign company** | **fine not less than ₹1,00,000**, extendable to **₹3,00,000**, and for a **continuing offence** an **additional fine up to ₹50,000 for every day** after the first during which the contravention continues |
| **Every officer of the foreign company who is in default** | **fine not less than ₹25,000**, extendable to **₹5,00,000** |

<Callout kind="amendment" label="AMENDMENT-CHECK · s. 392 was decriminalised for officers">
  <p>
    As enacted, s. 392 punished an officer in default with <strong>imprisonment up to six months or a
    fine, or both</strong>. The <strong>Companies (Amendment) Act 2020</strong> removed the
    imprisonment limb, leaving a fine only. The rupee bands above are the amended position; note also
    that s. 392 imposes a <strong>fine</strong> (a court-imposed punishment), not a
    <strong>penalty</strong> (an adjudicating-officer levy), which is why the section speaks of an
    offence being "continuing". Both the bands and the classification have moved before —
    re-verify.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 6 — one default, three consequences">
  <p>
    Nordwind Marine GmbH (Hamburg) establishes a project office in Kochi on 2 February 2026 and files
    nothing at all with the Registrar. In September 2026 an Indian supplier refuses to pay for a
    consignment, and Nordwind Marine wants to sue in an Indian court.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>1 · The breach.</strong> FC-1 was due within thirty days of 2 February 2026; nothing was
    filed, so the company has contravened s. 380(1) — and, as the year runs on, s. 381 and the annual
    return as well. <strong>2 · The money.</strong> Section 392: a fine on the company of not less
    than ₹1,00,000 up to ₹3,00,000, plus up to ₹50,000 for every day the contravention continues, and
    a fine of not less than ₹25,000 up to ₹5,00,000 on every officer in default — remembering that
    "director" for this Chapter includes a person on whose instructions the Board is accustomed to act
    (s. 386(b)). <strong>3 · The suit.</strong> Section 393 is the sting: the supply contract
    <em>remains perfectly valid</em> and Nordwind Marine <em>can be sued</em> on it, but Nordwind
    Marine <strong>cannot bring its own suit, claim a set-off, make a counter-claim or institute any
    legal proceeding</strong> in respect of the contract <strong>until it has complied</strong> with
    the provisions of the Act applicable to it. The door is one-way until the filings are made.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. Validity of contracts and Central Government exemptions — sections 393 and 393A

**Section 393** — any **failure by a company to comply with the provisions of this Chapter** shall
**not affect the validity of any contract, dealing or transaction** entered into by the company, or
its **liability to be sued** in respect thereof; **but** the company **shall not be entitled to
bring any suit, claim any set-off, make any counter-claim or institute any legal proceeding** in
respect of any such contract, dealing or transaction **until it has complied** with the provisions
of this Act applicable to it.

<div class="formula">s. 393 = <strong>contracts stay valid</strong> · <strong>the company can still be sued</strong> · <strong>but the company cannot sue, set off or counter-claim until it complies</strong>. Non-compliance disables the sword, never the shield.</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Half of section 393 is the trap and half is the answer.</strong> The commonest wrong
    option says non-compliance makes the contracts <strong>void</strong> — it does not, expressly.
    The second-commonest says non-compliance has no consequence for litigation at all — it does, and
    the bar covers <em>four</em> things: suit, set-off, counter-claim and any legal proceeding. The
    third says the bar is permanent — it is not: it lifts <strong>the moment the company
    complies</strong>, so the practical advice to a defaulting foreign company is simply to file
    everything and then sue. Always state both halves.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 393A — exemptions.** The **Central Government may, by notification, exempt any class or
classes of foreign companies** from any of the provisions of **ss. 380 to 386** and **ss. 392 and
393**, and a copy of every such notification must, **as soon as may be after it is made, be laid
before both Houses of Parliament**.

<Callout kind="amendment" label="AMENDMENT-CHECK · s. 393A is a live notification power">
  <p>
    Section 393A was <strong>inserted by the Companies (Amendment) Act 2017</strong>. Two points to
    hold: the exemption power reaches only <strong>ss. 380 to 386 and ss. 392 and 393</strong> —
    <em>not</em> s. 379, and <em>not</em> the prospectus and IDR sections 387 to 391; and it operates
    by <strong>notification</strong>, so the classes exempted at any moment are a matter of the
    notifications in force, which must be laid before Parliament. Learn the <em>scope of the
    power</em>, not any particular notified class, and check what stands notified for your attempt.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 11. A compliance timeline for a foreign company entering India

Put together, the Chapter is a single sequence. This is the answer skeleton for the "advise a
foreign company setting up in India" question, which is the most likely six-mark descriptive in the
chapter.

| **0 · Test the definition** | confirm all three limbs of s. 2(42) — incorporated outside India, place of business in India (itself/agent, physical/electronic), business activity in India | — | s. 2(42), s. 386(c), Rule 2 |
| **0A · Test the ownership** | compute the Indian holding: if **≥ 50%** of paid-up capital (equity + preference) is held by Indian citizens and/or Indian-incorporated bodies corporate, singly or in aggregate, the company complies **as if it were an Indian company** for its Indian business | — | s. 379(2) |

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

1. **Treating any overseas company that trades with India as a foreign company.** All **three limbs**
   of s. 2(42) must hold. Exporting into India from abroad, with no office, agent or electronic
   presence here, is not enough.
2. **Reading s. 379(2) as "more than 50%".** It is **not less than 50%** — exactly half qualifies.
3. **Counting equity only in the s. 379(2) test.** The sub-section says **equity or preference or
   partly equity and partly preference**, and holders are aggregated **singly or in the aggregate**.
4. **Counting the wrong holders.** Only **citizens of India** and **companies or bodies corporate
   incorporated in India** count. A non-citizen of Indian origin does not; a foreign-incorporated
   company with Indian owners does not.
5. **Saying s. 379(2) makes the company an Indian company.** It makes it comply **as if** it were
   one, and only for the business carried on **in India**.
6. **Starting the FC-1 clock at the wrong event.** Thirty days run from the **establishment of the
   place of business** — not from incorporation abroad, not from signing the lease, not from the
   first invoice.
7. **Swapping the accounts clock and the return clock.** **FC-3 · six months** from the close of the
   financial year; **FC-4 · sixty days** from the last day of the financial year.
8. **Converting days into calendar months.** Thirty days from 9 March is **8 April**, not 9 April.
   Sixty days from 31 March is **30 May**, not 31 May.
9. **Dropping the country of incorporation from the s. 382 display.** Name **and** country, outside
   every place of business, in English **and** the local language.
10. **Importing all Indian-company obligations through s. 384.** Only **s. 71, s. 92, s. 128,
    Chapter VI and Chapter XIV** are imported. The AGM, board-composition, s. 139 auditor-appointment
    and dividend provisions are not.
11. **Limiting the charge provisions to Indian-registered property generally.** Section 384(4)
    reaches charges on **properties created or acquired by the foreign company in India** — the
    location of the property is the test.
12. **Saying non-compliance voids the contracts.** Section 393 expressly preserves their validity and
    the company's liability to be sued; what it takes away is the company's own **right to sue, set
    off or counter-claim, until it complies**.
13. **Confining ss. 387 to 390 to foreign companies.** They bite on **any person** issuing a
    prospectus in India for a company incorporated outside India, **whether or not** it has a place
    of business here.
14. **Treating registration of the prospectus as a post-issue filing.** Section 389 requires delivery
    for registration **before** issue, circulation or distribution.
15. **Forgetting that s. 393A cannot exempt anyone from s. 379 or ss. 387 to 391.** The notification
    power reaches only **ss. 380 to 386** and **ss. 392 and 393**.

## One-page revision summary

**The definition (s. 2(42)).** Company **or body corporate** incorporated outside India **+** a
**place of business in India** — by itself or through an **agent**, **physically or through
electronic mode** **+** it **conducts business activity in India**. All three. **s. 386(c)** — place
of business **includes a share transfer or registration office**. **Rule 2(1)(c)** — electronic mode
is defined very widely and expressly applies **whether or not the main server is in India**
(rules-level). An **isolated transaction** is not a place of business.

**Application (s. 379).** **379(1)** — **ss. 380 to 386 and ss. 392 and 393 apply to all foreign
companies**. **379(2)** — where **not less than 50%** of the **paid-up share capital** (**equity or
preference or partly both**) is held by **Indian citizens** and/or **bodies corporate incorporated
in India**, **singly or in the aggregate**, the company complies with this Chapter **and such other
provisions as may be prescribed**, for its **Indian business**, **as if it were incorporated in
India**.

**Every clock in the chapter.**

| Clock | Period | Runs from | Form |
|---|---|---|---|
| Documents on establishing a place of business (s. 380(1)–(2)) | **30 days** | **establishment of the place of business** | FC-1 |
| Return of alteration in those particulars (s. 380(3)) | **30 days** | **the alteration** | FC-2 |
| Financial statements + list of places of business (s. 381, Rules) | **6 months** (+ up to **3 months** on the Registrar's extension) | **close of the financial year** | FC-3 |
| Annual return (s. 384(2), Rules) | **60 days** | **last day of the financial year** | FC-4 |
| Registration of a charge on property in India (s. 384(4) → Chapter VI) | **30 days** (then 60 / 120 with fees) | **creation of the charge** | CHG-1 |
| Registration of a prospectus (s. 389) | **before issue** | — | — |

**s. 380(1) checklist.** Certified charter/constitution + certified English translation · registered
or principal office address · directors and secretary with particulars · **name and address of one
or more persons resident in India** authorised to accept **service of process** · full address of
the **principal place of business in India** · particulars of **earlier openings and closings** ·
**declaration of no conviction or debarment** for any director or the authorised representative ·
any other prescribed information.

**ss. 381–385.** **381** — balance sheet and P&amp;L **every calendar year** in the prescribed form,
with the consolidated statements, **certified translations**, and a **list of all places of business
in India as at the balance-sheet date**; Indian business accounts **audited by a practising CA in
India** (Rules); filed in FC-3. **382** — display **name + country of incorporation** outside every
place of business (**English + local language**) and on all **business letters, bill-heads, letter
paper, notices and official publications** (English); **notice of limited liability** where members'
liability is limited. **383** — service on the **person named under s. 380** at that address, by
post, delivery or **electronic mode**, is good service. **385** — prescribed **fee** for registering
documents.

**s. 384 mapping.** **384(1)** → **s. 71 debentures** (mutatis mutandis) · **384(2)** → **s. 92
annual return** (with prescribed exceptions, modifications and adaptations — FC-4) · **384(3)** →
**s. 128 books of account**, for the **Indian** business · **384(4)** → **Chapter VI charges**, on
property **created or acquired in India** · **384(5)** → **Chapter XIV inspection, inquiry and
investigation**. Nothing else is imported.

**s. 386 interpretation.** **"Certified"** = certified in the prescribed manner as a true copy or
correct translation. **"Director"** includes a person on whose **directions or instructions the
Board is accustomed to act**. **"Place of business"** includes a **share transfer or registration
office**.

**Prospectus and IDRs (ss. 387–390).** Apply to **any person** issuing in India for a company
incorporated outside India, **whether or not** it has a place of business here. **387** —
prospectus **dated and signed**, with the constitution, the incorporating enactments, an **Indian
address for inspection**, the **date and country of incorporation**, and whether a place of business
has been established with its principal Indian address; exceptions for issues to **existing members
or debenture-holders** and for securities **uniform with securities already quoted on a recognised
stock exchange**. **388** — **expert's written consent**, not withdrawn, and a statement to that
effect; application and allotment particulars. **389** — copy certified by the **chairperson and two
directors** as approved by the **managing body**, **delivered for registration before issue**, with
expert consents and material contracts. **390** — **enabling** rule-making power for **Indian
Depository Receipts** under four heads: offer · disclosures · depository/custodian/underwriter
mechanics · sale, transfer and transmission.

**Consequences (ss. 391–393A).** **391(1)** — **ss. 34 to 36** (criminal liability for
misstatements, civil liability for misstatements, fraudulently inducing investment) apply to a
s. 389 prospectus and to IDRs. **391(2)** — **Chapter XX (winding up)** applies **mutatis mutandis**
to the **closure of the Indian place of business**, notwithstanding s. 376. **392** — company
**₹1,00,000 to ₹3,00,000** plus **up to ₹50,000 per day** for a continuing offence; every **officer
in default ₹25,000 to ₹5,00,000** (imprisonment removed in 2020). **393** — contracts **remain
valid**, the company **can be sued**, but it **cannot sue, set off, counter-claim or institute any
proceeding until it complies**. **393A** — the Central Government may **by notification exempt
classes of foreign companies** from **ss. 380 to 386 and ss. 392 and 393**, the notification to be
**laid before both Houses of Parliament**.
