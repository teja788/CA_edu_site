On 12 August 2026 the members of Suryodaya Ceramics Limited sit down at the annual general meeting.
The Board has recommended a dividend of 14 per cent. The company earned very little this year, but it
has ₹5 crore of free reserves built up over a decade. A member stands up and proposes 18 per cent
instead — "the reserves are ours, after all." The chairman refuses. Six weeks later a shareholder who
never received his warrant writes in, and the company discovers that ₹9 lakh of the declared dividend
is still sitting in its current account.

Every mark in this chapter lives inside that story. **Where may the money come from?** **Who may fix
the rate?** **How many days until it must move, and to which account?** **What does it cost to be
late?** Sections 123 to 127 answer those four questions and almost nothing else, and they answer them
with numbers — 5 days, 30 days, 7 days, 7 years, 12 per cent, 18 per cent, one-tenth, fifteen per
cent. This is the most *computable* chapter in Paper 2, and the paper rewards students who can carry
a balance sheet through Rule 3 and a declaration date through a calendar.

## 1. Meaning of dividend — final and interim

**Section 2(35)** does not define dividend; it merely says that "dividend" **includes any interim
dividend**. That one word does the heavy lifting for the whole chapter: every obligation the Act
imposes on "dividend" — the five-day deposit, the thirty-day payment, the unpaid-dividend transfer,
the s. 127 punishment — applies to an **interim** dividend exactly as it applies to a final one.

Commercially, a dividend is that part of divisible profits which the company distributes among its
members in proportion to their shareholding. It is a **return on** capital, never a **return of**
capital: the entire architecture of s. 123 exists to keep dividend out of the capital fund which
creditors look to.

**Who declares what.** The two dividends have different masters, and the examiner tests the pair
constantly.

| Question | Final dividend | Interim dividend |
|---|---|---|
| Who declares it | the **members in general meeting** (the AGM) | the **Board of Directors** (s. 123(3)) |
| On whose initiative | on the **Board's recommendation** — members cannot declare without one | the Board's own motion |
| Can the rate be raised | **No.** Members may **reduce** the recommended rate, or refuse altogether; they may **never exceed** it | not applicable — the Board fixes it |
| When | at the AGM for the financial year | **during** the financial year, or **from the closure of the financial year till the AGM is held** |
| Source provision | s. 123(1) | s. 123(3) |
| Deposit within 5 days, payment within 30 days, s. 127 exposure | yes | **yes** — because of s. 2(35) |

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>The rate ceiling is one-directional, and it is a favourite one-mark trap.</strong> The
    Board <em>recommends</em>; the members <em>declare</em>. A general meeting that votes 18 per cent
    where the Board recommended 14 per cent has passed a resolution the company cannot act on. It may
    lawfully declare 14 per cent, 10 per cent, or nothing at all. The reverse relationship — members
    recommending and the Board declaring — exists nowhere in the Act for a final dividend.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. Sources of dividend — section 123

**Section 123(1)** is a prohibition, not a permission: **no dividend shall be declared or paid by a
company for any financial year except** out of one of the sources it names.

**Source (a) — profits.** Either (i) the **profits of the company for that financial year** arrived at
**after providing for depreciation in accordance with s. 123(2)**, or (ii) the **profits of any
previous financial year or years** arrived at after providing for depreciation **and remaining
undistributed**, or (iii) **out of both**. **Section 123(2)** fixes the depreciation standard: it must
be provided **in accordance with Schedule II**.

**Source (b) — government money.** Out of **money provided by the Central Government or a State
Government** for the payment of dividend by the company **in pursuance of a guarantee given by that
Government**. This is the only source that has nothing to do with profits at all, and it is examined
precisely because students forget it exists.

Four provisos then close the gaps:

1. **Transfer to reserves is voluntary.** Before declaring dividend, a company **may** transfer
   **such percentage of its profits for that financial year as it may consider appropriate** to its
   reserves. Under the 1956 Act a slab of compulsory transfer applied; the 2013 Act abolished it. An
   option saying "the company must transfer at least 10 per cent" is testing exactly this.
2. **Inadequate or absent profits.** Where, owing to **inadequacy or absence of profits** in any
   financial year, a company **proposes to declare dividend out of the accumulated profits earned in
   previous years and transferred to the free reserves**, the declaration must be made **only in
   accordance with the prescribed rules** — Rule 3, worked in §3.
3. **Set off past losses and unprovided depreciation first.** **No company shall declare dividend
   unless carried-over previous losses and depreciation not provided in previous year or years are set
   off against the profit of the company for the current year.**
4. **Free reserves only.** **No dividend shall be declared or paid by a company from its reserves
   other than free reserves.** A capital redemption reserve, a securities premium account or a
   revaluation reserve is therefore off limits, however large.

<div class="formula">Dividend may come from: current-year profits after Schedule II depreciation · undistributed profits of previous years after depreciation · both · or government money under a government guarantee. From nothing else.</div>

**What "free reserves" means — s. 2(43).** Free reserves are such reserves as, **per the latest
audited balance sheet**, are **available for distribution as dividend**, but **excluding**:

- any amount representing **unrealised gains, notional gains or revaluation of assets**, whether shown
  as a reserve or otherwise; and
- any **change in the carrying amount of an asset or a liability recognised in equity**, including the
  surplus in the profit and loss account on measurement of the asset or liability at **fair value**.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>A revaluation surplus is not a free reserve, and a large "reserves and surplus" figure on
    the face of the balance sheet is not the Rule 3 input.</strong> Students routinely take the whole
    reserves-and-surplus line into the one-tenth and fifteen-per-cent computations. Strip out
    revaluation reserve, capital redemption reserve, securities premium, debenture redemption reserve
    and any fair-value or unrealised-gain component first. What survives — and only what survives — is
    "free reserves" for s. 2(43), s. 123 and Rule 3.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 123(6) — the deposit-default bar.** A company which **fails to comply with ss. 73 and 74**
(acceptance and repayment of deposits) **shall not, so long as such failure continues, declare any
dividend on its equity shares.** Three features carry marks: the bar is **automatic**, needing no
order; it lasts **only while the failure continues** and revives the power the moment the default is
cured; and it is expressed to bite **equity shares**, so preference dividend is not caught by its
words.

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 1 — three source questions in one balance sheet">
  <p>
    Ambalika Alloys Limited closes the year to 31 March 2026 with a profit of ₹80,00,000 before
    depreciation. Schedule II depreciation for the year is ₹22,00,000. Brought forward from earlier
    years are an accumulated loss of ₹15,00,000 and depreciation of ₹6,00,000 that was never provided
    for. The balance sheet also shows a revaluation reserve of ₹2,40,00,000 and free reserves of
    ₹18,00,000. A ₹40,00,000 deposit repayment fell due in January 2026 and remains unpaid.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — what is actually distributable</div>
    <p>
      Profit for the year after Schedule II depreciation = ₹80,00,000 − ₹22,00,000 =
      <strong>₹58,00,000</strong>.<br />
      Less carried-over loss set off (third proviso) = ₹15,00,000.<br />
      Less depreciation not provided in previous years (third proviso) = ₹6,00,000.<br />
      Distributable current-year profit = 58,00,000 − 15,00,000 − 6,00,000 =
      <strong>₹37,00,000</strong>.<br />
      The revaluation reserve of ₹2,40,00,000 is <strong>not</strong> a free reserve (s. 2(43)) and the
      fourth proviso bars any dividend out of it.
    </p>
  </div>
  <p>
    <strong>And then the answer changes completely.</strong> Because the s. 73/74 deposit default
    subsists, <strong>s. 123(6) forbids any dividend on equity shares at all</strong> until the default
    is cured — the ₹37,00,000 computation is correct but academic. That two-step structure — compute,
    then apply the bar — is the standard six-mark question in this chapter.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. Dividend out of accumulated profits — Rule 3

When the current year's profits are **inadequate or absent** and the company still wants to pay, the
second proviso to s. 123(1) sends it to **Rule 3 of the Companies (Declaration and Payment of
Dividend) Rules 2014**. Rule 3 does not ask whether the reserves are large enough. It imposes
**three independent ceilings plus a set-off**, and the company must satisfy **every** one of them.

| Condition | The test | Measured against |
|---|---|---|
| **(a) Rate ceiling** | the **rate of dividend** declared shall **not exceed the average of the rates** at which dividend was declared by the company in the **three years immediately preceding** that year | the three preceding years' declared rates |
| **(b) Draw ceiling** | the **total amount drawn from the accumulated profits** shall **not exceed one-tenth of the sum of paid-up share capital and free reserves** | as appearing in the **latest audited financial statement** |
| **(c) Reserve floor** | the **balance of reserves after such withdrawal** shall **not fall below fifteen per cent of paid-up share capital** | as appearing in the **latest audited financial statement** |
| **Set-off** | the amount so drawn shall **first be used to set off the losses incurred in the financial year in which the dividend is declared**, before any dividend on equity shares is declared | the current year's loss |

**The exception to condition (a).** The rate ceiling **does not apply to a company which has not
declared any dividend in each of the three immediately preceding financial years.** Read the words
carefully: the exception needs **no dividend in each** of the three years. A company that skipped two
years but paid in the third is still inside condition (a) — and its "average" is then taken on the
Study Material's approach as the average of the rates for the three years, treating a nil year as nil.

<div class="formula">Rule 3 ceilings — rate ≤ average of the last 3 years' rates · draw ≤ 1/10 × (paid-up capital + free reserves) · reserves left ≥ 15% of paid-up capital · current-year loss set off first.</div>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 2 — Rule 3 worked, where the rate ceiling binds">
  <p>
    Vaidehi Chemicals Limited has a paid-up equity share capital of <strong>₹9,00,00,000</strong> and
    free reserves of <strong>₹5,00,00,000</strong> per its latest audited financial statement. For the
    year to 31 March 2026 it has a <strong>loss of ₹40,00,000</strong>. Dividend rates in the three
    immediately preceding years were <strong>14 per cent, 11 per cent and 8 per cent</strong>. The
    Board wants the largest dividend the law allows.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — the three ceilings, computed separately</div>
    <p>
      <strong>Condition (a) — rate.</strong> Average of 14, 11 and 8 = 33 ÷ 3 =
      <strong>11 per cent</strong>. Maximum dividend by rate = 11% × ₹9,00,00,000 =
      <strong>₹99,00,000</strong>.<br />
      <strong>Condition (b) — draw.</strong> One-tenth of (₹9,00,00,000 + ₹5,00,00,000) = 1/10 ×
      ₹14,00,00,000 = <strong>₹1,40,00,000</strong>.<br />
      <strong>Condition (c) — floor.</strong> 15% × ₹9,00,00,000 = ₹1,35,00,000. Reserves after the
      draw must be at least that, so the draw may not exceed ₹5,00,00,000 − ₹1,35,00,000 =
      <strong>₹3,65,00,000</strong>.
    </p>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — the set-off, and which ceiling actually bites</div>
    <p>
      The binding draw ceiling is the lower of ₹1,40,00,000 and ₹3,65,00,000 =
      <strong>₹1,40,00,000</strong>.<br />
      Out of the draw, the current year's loss of ₹40,00,000 must be set off <em>first</em>. Money left
      for dividend from the draw = ₹1,40,00,000 − ₹40,00,000 = <strong>₹1,00,00,000</strong>.<br />
      But condition (a) independently caps the dividend at ₹99,00,000. <strong>Maximum dividend =
      ₹99,00,000</strong>, i.e. 11 per cent.<br />
      <strong>Cross-check.</strong> Total draw = ₹99,00,000 + ₹40,00,000 = ₹1,39,00,000, which is
      within ₹1,40,00,000 ✓. Reserves left = ₹5,00,00,000 − ₹1,39,00,000 = ₹3,61,00,000, comfortably
      above the ₹1,35,00,000 floor ✓.
    </p>
  </div>
  <p>
    <strong>The examiner's point.</strong> All three ceilings must be computed; only one of them binds.
    Here it was the rate. Answer scripts that compute one-tenth, announce ₹1,40,00,000 and stop lose
    most of the marks.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 3 — the same rule, where the fifteen-per-cent floor binds">
  <p>
    Nandini Steels Limited has paid-up equity capital of <strong>₹20,00,00,000</strong> and free
    reserves of only <strong>₹4,00,00,000</strong>. It made no profit this year and no loss. Dividend
    rates in the three preceding years were <strong>10, 8 and 6 per cent</strong>.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — the floor overtakes the one-tenth cap</div>
    <p>
      <strong>Rate ceiling:</strong> average = (10 + 8 + 6) ÷ 3 = 8 per cent → 8% × ₹20,00,00,000 =
      ₹1,60,00,000.<br />
      <strong>Draw ceiling:</strong> 1/10 × (₹20,00,00,000 + ₹4,00,00,000) = ₹2,40,00,000.<br />
      <strong>Reserve floor:</strong> 15% × ₹20,00,00,000 = ₹3,00,00,000. Reserves are only
      ₹4,00,00,000, so the maximum draw is ₹4,00,00,000 − ₹3,00,00,000 =
      <strong>₹1,00,00,000</strong>.<br />
      <strong>Maximum dividend = ₹1,00,00,000</strong> — a rate of 5 per cent, well inside the 8 per
      cent ceiling. Here the <em>floor</em>, not the one-tenth cap and not the rate, is the binding
      constraint.
    </p>
  </div>
</Callout>

## 4. Interim dividend and its rate cap

**Section 123(3)** lets the **Board of Directors** declare an interim dividend, and it is drafted with
unusual precision on both **when** and **out of what**.

**When.** During **any financial year**, **or** at any time during the period **from the closure of
the financial year till the holding of the annual general meeting**.

**Out of what.** Any of three pools:

1. the **surplus in the profit and loss account**;
2. the **profits of the financial year for which the interim dividend is sought to be declared**; or
3. the **profits generated in the financial year till the quarter preceding the date of declaration**.

**The loss proviso — the examinable bit.** Where the company has **incurred a loss during the current
financial year up to the end of the quarter immediately preceding the date of declaration** of the
interim dividend, that interim dividend **shall not be declared at a rate higher than the average
dividends declared by the company during the immediately preceding three financial years**.

<div class="formula">Loss up to the end of the quarter immediately preceding declaration → interim rate ≤ average of the last 3 financial years' dividend rates. No loss → no rate cap in s. 123(3).</div>

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>Two different three-year averages, two different triggers — keep them apart.</strong> The
    <strong>Rule 3</strong> average applies to a <em>final</em> dividend declared out of
    <em>accumulated profits</em> when current profits are inadequate or absent, and it comes with the
    one-tenth and fifteen-per-cent conditions attached. The <strong>s. 123(3) proviso</strong> average
    applies to an <em>interim</em> dividend where there is a <em>loss up to the preceding quarter</em>,
    and it comes with <strong>no</strong> draw cap and <strong>no</strong> reserve floor. Answers that
    import one-tenth into an interim-dividend problem are answering a different question.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 4 — an interim dividend after a loss-making quarter">
  <p>
    Girija Textiles Limited has a paid-up equity capital of <strong>₹3,00,00,000</strong>. For the year
    2026-27 it has an accumulated <strong>loss of ₹12,00,000</strong> up to 31 December 2026, though
    the surplus in its profit and loss account stands at ₹1,10,00,000. On <strong>20 January
    2027</strong> the Board proposes an interim dividend of 16 per cent. Dividend rates for 2023-24,
    2024-25 and 2025-26 were <strong>18, 15 and 9 per cent</strong>.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — identifying the quarter, then the ceiling</div>
    <p>
      <strong>1 · The quarter.</strong> The quarter immediately preceding 20 January 2027 is the
      quarter <strong>ended 31 December 2026</strong>. There is a loss up to that date, so the proviso
      to s. 123(3) engages.<br />
      <strong>2 · The average.</strong> (18 + 15 + 9) ÷ 3 = <strong>14 per cent</strong>.<br />
      <strong>3 · The ceiling in money.</strong> 14% × ₹3,00,00,000 = <strong>₹42,00,000</strong>. The
      proposed 16 per cent (₹48,00,000) is <strong>not permissible</strong>; the Board may declare up
      to 14 per cent.<br />
      <strong>4 · The source is still fine.</strong> The dividend comes out of the surplus in the
      profit and loss account, which s. 123(3) expressly allows. The loss restricts the
      <em>rate</em>, not the <em>source</em>.
    </p>
  </div>
</Callout>

## 5. Depositing and paying the dividend — the 5-day and 30-day clocks

**Section 123(4) — the five-day deposit.** The **amount of the dividend, including interim dividend**,
shall be **deposited in a scheduled bank in a separate account within five days from the date of
declaration**. Three examinable features: the account must be **separate** (not the company's ordinary
current account), the bank must be **scheduled**, and the obligation attaches to **interim dividend
too** — that is why the sub-section says so expressly.

**Section 123(5) — to whom, and in what form.** No dividend shall be paid in respect of any share
**except to the registered shareholder of that share, or to his order, or to his banker**, and it
**shall not be payable except in cash**. Two provisos:

- nothing in the sub-section prohibits the **capitalisation of profits or reserves** for the purpose of
  **issuing fully paid-up bonus shares**, or **paying up any amount for the time being unpaid** on
  shares held by the members; and
- any dividend payable in cash **may be paid by cheque or warrant or in any electronic mode** to the
  shareholder entitled to payment.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>"Payable in cash" does not mean currency notes, and bonus shares are not a dividend in
    kind.</strong> The bar in s. 123(5) is against dividend <strong>in kind</strong> — distributing the
    company's goods, its investments, or shares of a subsidiary as dividend. Cheque, warrant and
    electronic transfer are all "in cash" for this purpose. Bonus shares survive the bar not because
    they are a permitted dividend but because they are a <strong>capitalisation of profits or
    reserves</strong>, expressly saved by the first proviso — no cash leaves the company at all.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 127 — the thirty-day payment clock.** Where a dividend has been declared but **has not been
paid, or the warrant in respect of it has not been posted, within thirty days from the date of
declaration**, the consequences in §9 follow. So the two clocks run **in parallel from the same
event**, the date of declaration: **5 days to deposit**, **30 days to pay**.

<div class="formula">Date of declaration → deposit in a separate scheduled-bank account within 5 days (s. 123(4)) → pay or post the warrant within 30 days (s. 127). Both clocks start on the declaration date, not on the deposit date.</div>

## 6. Unpaid dividend and the Unpaid Dividend Account — section 124

Some shareholders never claim. **Section 124** is the machinery that keeps that money identifiable
instead of letting it drift back into the company's working capital.

**124(1) — the seven-day transfer.** Where a dividend has been declared but **has not been paid or
claimed within thirty days** from the date of declaration, the company shall, **within seven days from
the date of expiry of that period of thirty days**, transfer the **total amount** unpaid or unclaimed
to a **special account opened in any scheduled bank** called the **Unpaid Dividend Account**.

**124(2) — the ninety-day statement.** Within **ninety days of making any transfer** to the Unpaid
Dividend Account, the company shall prepare a statement containing the **names, last known addresses
and the unpaid dividend to be paid to each person**, and place it on **its own website, if any**, and
**on any other website approved by the Central Government** for the purpose.

**124(3) — interest at twelve per cent.** If default is made in transferring the whole or any part of
that amount to the Unpaid Dividend Account, the company **shall pay, from the date of such default,
interest at twelve per cent per annum** on so much as has not been transferred; and that **interest
enures to the benefit of the members in proportion to the amount remaining unpaid to them**.

**124(4) — claiming from the company.** Any person claiming to be entitled to money transferred to the
Unpaid Dividend Account may **apply to the company** for payment.

**124(5) — seven years, then the money moves.** Money in the Unpaid Dividend Account which remains
unpaid or unclaimed for **seven years from the date of that transfer** shall be transferred by the
company, **together with interest accrued**, to the **Fund established under s. 125(1)**. The company
sends a statement of the details to the **authority administering the Fund**, which issues a
**receipt** as evidence of the transfer.

**124(6) — and the shares follow the money.** **All shares in respect of which dividend has not been
paid or claimed for seven consecutive years or more shall be transferred by the company in the name of
the Investor Education and Protection Fund**, along with a statement in the prescribed form. The
proviso preserves the owner: **any claimant of shares so transferred is entitled to claim the transfer
of the shares back from the IEPF** on the prescribed procedure and documents.

<div class="formula">30 days to pay → 7 days after that to move the unpaid balance into the Unpaid Dividend Account → 90 days from that transfer to publish the statement → 7 years from that transfer and the money AND the shares go to the IEPF.</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Twelve per cent and eighteen per cent are different penalties for different
    defaults.</strong> <strong>Twelve per cent</strong> under s. 124(3) is charged for failing to
    <em>transfer</em> unpaid dividend into the Unpaid Dividend Account, and it belongs to the
    <em>members</em>. <strong>Eighteen per cent</strong> under s. 127 is charged for failing to
    <em>pay</em> the dividend within thirty days, and it is payable to the shareholders kept waiting.
    A company that is late on both pays both, on different amounts, for different periods. Mixing the
    rates is the single most common error in this chapter.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 5 — a section 124(3) interest computation">
  <p>
    Kalindi Papers Limited was required to transfer <strong>₹15,00,000</strong> of unclaimed dividend
    to its Unpaid Dividend Account by <strong>20 September 2026</strong>. It actually made the transfer
    on <strong>13 February 2027</strong>.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — days of default, then the interest</div>
    <p>
      Days from 20 September 2026 to 13 February 2027: September 10 + October 31 + November 30 +
      December 31 + January 31 + February 13 = <strong>146 days</strong>.<br />
      Interest at 12 per cent per annum = ₹15,00,000 × 12/100 × 146/365 =
      <strong>₹72,000</strong>.<br />
      That ₹72,000 does <strong>not</strong> stay with the company: s. 124(3) directs it to the benefit
      of the members in proportion to the amounts remaining unpaid to them.
    </p>
  </div>
</Callout>

## 7. The Investor Education and Protection Fund — section 125

**Section 125(1)** establishes the **Investor Education and Protection Fund**. For the exam you need
its principal **credits** and its permitted **uses**, not an exhaustive list.

**What is credited to the Fund (s. 125(2)), in outline:** amounts given by the Central Government by
way of **grants**, and **donations** by governments, companies or other institutions; the amount in the
**Unpaid Dividend Account** transferred under s. 124(5); **application money received for allotment of
securities and due for refund**; **matured deposits** with companies other than banking companies;
**matured debentures** with companies; the **interest accrued** on all of those; **sale proceeds of
fractional shares** arising on bonus issues, mergers and amalgamations; the **redemption amount of
preference shares** remaining unclaimed; amounts **disgorged** under s. 38(4); income from investments
of the Fund; and such other amounts as may be prescribed.

The controlling proviso: the amounts in the middle group **do not form part of the Fund unless they
have remained unclaimed and unpaid for a period of seven years** from the date they became due for
payment. Seven years is the pulse of the whole chapter.

**What the Fund may be used for (s. 125(3)):**

- **refund** of unclaimed dividends, matured deposits, matured debentures, application money due for
  refund, and the interest on them;
- **promotion of investors' education, awareness and protection**;
- **distribution of any disgorged amount** among eligible and identifiable applicants for shares or
  debentures who suffered losses due to wrongful actions of any person, in accordance with orders made
  by the **Court**;
- **reimbursement of legal expenses** incurred in pursuing **class action suits under ss. 37 and 245**
  by members, debenture-holders or depositors, as **sanctioned by the Tribunal**; and
- any **other purpose incidental** to the above, as may be prescribed.

**Section 125(4)** preserves the individual claim: a person whose **amount** was transferred to the
Fund under s. 124, or whose **shares** were transferred under s. 124(6), may **apply to the Authority
for a refund**, and the Authority orders refund after verification.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>Transfer to the IEPF is not confiscation.</strong> Both limbs of s. 124 have a claw-back —
    s. 124(6)'s proviso for the shares and s. 125(4) for the money. Any option that says the
    shareholder "permanently forfeits" his dividend or his shares after seven years is wrong, and
    knowing <em>who</em> he applies to is the second half of the mark: to the <strong>company</strong>
    while the money is still in the Unpaid Dividend Account (s. 124(4)), and to the
    <strong>IEPF Authority</strong> once it has gone to the Fund (s. 125(4)).
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. Dividend in abeyance — section 126

**Section 126** deals with the shares in transit. Where an **instrument of transfer of shares has been
delivered to the company for registration** and the transfer **has not yet been registered**, the
company shall, notwithstanding anything else in the Act:

- **(a)** **transfer the dividend** in relation to those shares **to the Unpaid Dividend Account**
  referred to in s. 124 — **unless the company is authorised by the registered holder in writing to
  pay that dividend to the transferee** named in the instrument; and
- **(b)** **keep in abeyance**, in relation to those shares, any **offer of rights shares under
  s. 62(1)(a)** and any **issue of fully paid-up bonus shares** under the first proviso to s. 123(5).

Notice the asymmetry that the section is built on: the **dividend is parked**, not withheld
permanently — it goes into the Unpaid Dividend Account and is paid to whoever is finally held
entitled; whereas the **rights and bonus entitlements are frozen** until the register is settled. And
notice who can shortcut it: the **registered holder**, in **writing**. The transferee cannot authorise
his own payment.

## 9. Failure to pay — section 127 and its five defences

**Section 127** engages where a dividend **has been declared** but has **not been paid, or the warrant
in respect of it has not been posted, within thirty days from the date of declaration**, to any
shareholder entitled to payment. Two limbs of consequence:

| Who | Consequence |
|---|---|
| **Every director** who is **knowingly a party to the default** | **imprisonment which may extend to two years** **and** **fine of not less than ₹1,000 for every day** during which the default continues |
| **The company** | liable to pay **simple interest at eighteen per cent per annum** during the period for which the default continues |

<div class="formula">s. 127 — director knowingly a party: imprisonment up to 2 years AND fine of at least ₹1,000 per day of default. Company: simple interest at 18% p.a. for the period of default.</div>

**The five statutory defences.** The proviso says **no offence under this section shall be deemed to
have been committed**:

1. where the dividend **could not be paid by reason of the operation of any law**;
2. where a **shareholder has given directions** to the company regarding the payment of the dividend
   and **those directions cannot be complied with**, **and the same has been communicated to him**;
3. where there is a **dispute regarding the right to receive the dividend**;
4. where the dividend has been **lawfully adjusted by the company against any sum due to it from the
   shareholder**; or
5. where, **for any other reason, the failure to pay or to post the warrant was not due to any default
   on the part of the company**.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Defence 2 has two halves and defence 4 has the word "lawfully".</strong> A shareholder's
    impossible payment instruction protects the company <em>only if</em> the company
    <strong>communicated the difficulty back to him</strong>; silence forfeits the defence. And a
    set-off protects the company only if the adjustment was <strong>lawful</strong> — a company cannot
    invent a claim against a member and call it an adjustment. Note too that the defences bar the
    <em>offence</em>; where a defence applies, neither the director's punishment nor the company's
    18 per cent interest follows.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 6 — the eighteen-per-cent computation, and a defence that works">
  <p>
    Rukmini Papers Limited declares a dividend on <strong>10 July 2026</strong>. A block of
    <strong>₹50,00,000</strong> payable to one institutional shareholder is neither paid nor posted;
    the company finally pays on <strong>21 October 2026</strong>. Director K knew of and was party to
    the delay. Separately, ₹4,00,000 payable to another member could not be paid because a civil court
    was seized of a dispute between two claimants over the right to receive it.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — the default period and the interest</div>
    <p>
      <strong>1 · When the default begins.</strong> Thirty days from 10 July 2026 expire on
      <strong>9 August 2026</strong>. The default runs from then.<br />
      <strong>2 · Days of default.</strong> 9 August 2026 to 21 October 2026 = August 22 + September 30
      + October 21 = <strong>73 days</strong>.<br />
      <strong>3 · Interest.</strong> ₹50,00,000 × 18/100 × 73/365 = <strong>₹1,80,000</strong>, as
      simple interest.<br />
      <strong>4 · Director K.</strong> Being knowingly a party, he faces imprisonment up to two years
      and a fine of at least ₹1,000 for each day of the default.<br />
      <strong>5 · The ₹4,00,000.</strong> Defence (c) applies — there is a genuine
      <strong>dispute regarding the right to receive the dividend</strong>. No offence is deemed
      committed, and no 18 per cent interest runs on that amount. Under s. 126 the money is parked in
      the Unpaid Dividend Account until entitlement is settled.
    </p>
  </div>
</Callout>

## 10. Dividend in proportion to paid-up capital — section 51

**Section 51** is one sentence: a company **may, if so authorised by its articles, pay dividends in
proportion to the amount paid-up on each share**.

Everything examinable flows from the two conditions inside it. **First**, the power is
**permissive** — the company "may". **Second**, it is **conditional on the articles**; without an
enabling article the company pays dividend on the **nominal amount** of the shares, so a partly paid
share carries the same dividend as a fully paid one of the same denomination. Regulation 88 of
**Table F** contains the enabling provision, so a company that has adopted Table F has the power
already.

**Calls in advance carry no dividend.** Money paid to the company **in advance of calls** is a debt
owed by the company to the member, not paid-up capital. Under s. 50(2) it carries **no voting rights**
until it becomes presently payable, and it is not counted for dividend either; the articles typically
allow **interest** on it instead. So the base for a s. 51 dividend is the amount **called up and paid**
— never the amount actually received where that includes an advance.

<div class="formula">Dividend base under s. 51 = amount paid-up on the share, if the articles so authorise. Calls in advance are excluded. Without an enabling article, dividend follows the nominal amount of the share.</div>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 7 — three shareholders, one rate, three answers">
  <p>
    Prakriti Foods Limited, whose articles adopt Regulation 88 of Table F, declares a dividend of
    <strong>12 per cent</strong> for the year. Its equity shares have a face value of ₹10 each.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note — apply the rate to the paid-up amount, share by share</div>
    <p>
      <strong>Aarav</strong> — 20,000 shares, fully paid. Paid-up = ₹2,00,000 → dividend
      <strong>₹24,000</strong>.<br />
      <strong>Bhavna</strong> — 20,000 shares, ₹7 per share paid up. Paid-up = ₹1,40,000 → dividend
      <strong>₹16,800</strong>.<br />
      <strong>Charu</strong> — 10,000 shares on which ₹6 has been called and paid, plus ₹4 per share
      paid <em>in advance of calls</em>. Paid-up for dividend = ₹60,000 → dividend
      <strong>₹7,200</strong>. The ₹40,000 advance is ignored, though the articles may allow interest
      on it.<br />
      <strong>The variation.</strong> Had the articles contained no enabling provision, s. 51 would not
      apply and dividend would follow the <em>nominal</em> value — Aarav and Bhavna would each receive

## 11. Revocation, and the master timeline

**A declared dividend is a debt.** Once the members declare a final dividend at the general meeting,
the amount becomes a **debt due from the company to each shareholder**, enforceable as such. It
follows that the declaration **cannot be revoked** by a later resolution of the members or of the
Board. The narrow exceptions are where the declaration was **itself unlawful** — for example made out
of capital or in breach of s. 123 — or where **all the shareholders consent** to the revocation.

**Interim dividend, once declared, stands on the same footing.** Under the pre-2013 position an
interim dividend was often treated as revocable because it was a mere Board resolution creating no
debt. The 2013 Act closed that gap: s. 2(35) brings interim dividend inside "dividend", s. 123(4)
requires the **same five-day deposit**, and s. 127 applies the **same thirty-day clock and eighteen per
cent**. A Board that declares an interim dividend and then changes its mind is in default.

**The master timeline.** Take Suryodaya Ceramics Limited from the opening: the members declare the
final dividend at the AGM on **12 August 2026**, and ₹9,00,000 of it is neither paid nor claimed.

| Step | Provision | Period | Runs from | Last date |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deposit the whole dividend in a separate scheduled-bank account | s. 123(4) | **5 days** | date of declaration, 12 Aug 2026 | **17 August 2026** |
| Pay the dividend or post the warrant | s. 127 | **30 days** | date of declaration, 12 Aug 2026 | **11 September 2026** |
| Transfer the unpaid ₹9,00,000 to the Unpaid Dividend Account | s. 124(1) | **7 days** | **expiry of the 30 days**, 11 Sep 2026 | **18 September 2026** |
| Place the statement of unpaid amounts on the websites | s. 124(2) | **90 days** | date of that transfer, 18 Sep 2026 | **17 December 2026** |
| Transfer the still-unclaimed money, with interest, to the IEPF, and transfer the underlying shares | ss. 124(5) and 124(6) | **7 years** | date of that transfer, 18 Sep 2026 | **18 September 2033** |

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Working note — the date arithmetic, spelled out</div>
  <p>
    <strong>12 Aug + 5 days</strong> = 17 August 2026.<br />
    <strong>12 Aug + 30 days</strong> — 19 days remain in August after the 12th, so 19 + 11 = 30 lands
    on <strong>11 September 2026</strong>.<br />
    <strong>7 days from the expiry of the 30-day period</strong> — the clock restarts on
    <strong>11 September</strong>, not on 12 August, giving <strong>18 September 2026</strong>. This is
    the single most common date error in the chapter: adding 37 days to the declaration date would give
    18 September only by coincidence of month lengths, and adding 7 days to the declaration date gives
    19 August, which is wrong by a month.<br />
    <strong>90 days from 18 September 2026</strong> — 12 remaining in September + 31 in October + 30 in
    November = 73; 17 more days gives <strong>17 December 2026</strong>.<br />
    <strong>7 years from 18 September 2026</strong> = <strong>18 September 2033</strong>, counted from
    the date of transfer into the Unpaid Dividend Account, not from the date of declaration.
  </p>
</div>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

1. **Letting members raise the rate.** Members may reduce or refuse the Board's recommended final
   dividend; they may **never exceed** it. The Board alone fixes an interim dividend.
2. **Confusing 12 per cent with 18 per cent.** **12 per cent (s. 124(3))** — failure to **transfer** to
   the Unpaid Dividend Account, and the interest belongs to the **members**. **18 per cent (s. 127)** —
   failure to **pay within thirty days**.
3. **Starting the seven-day clock at the declaration date.** It runs from the **expiry of the thirty
   days**, not from the declaration.
4. **Starting the seven-year clock at the declaration date.** It runs from the **date of transfer into
   the Unpaid Dividend Account**.
5. **Treating "reserves and surplus" as "free reserves".** Revaluation reserve, securities premium,
   capital redemption reserve and any unrealised or fair-value gain are excluded by s. 2(43), and the
   fourth proviso to s. 123(1) bars dividend from reserves other than free reserves.
6. **Computing only one Rule 3 ceiling.** All three must be computed — rate average, one-tenth of
   (capital + free reserves), and the fifteen per cent of **paid-up capital** floor — and the
   **lowest** governs. The current-year loss is set off out of the draw **first**.
7. **Using the wrong base in Rule 3.** The one-tenth cap is on **capital plus free reserves**; the
   fifteen per cent floor is on **paid-up capital alone**.
8. **Importing Rule 3 into an interim dividend.** The s. 123(3) proviso caps only the **rate**, and
   only where there is a **loss up to the quarter immediately preceding declaration**. No one-tenth
   cap, no reserve floor.
9. **Forgetting the s. 123(6) bar.** While an s. 73/74 deposit default subsists, **no dividend on
   equity shares** may be declared, however healthy the profits.
10. **Saying dividend in kind is allowed because bonus shares are.** Bonus shares are a
    **capitalisation** saved by the first proviso to s. 123(5), not a dividend in kind.
11. **Treating the IEPF transfer as forfeiture.** Both the money (s. 125(4)) and the shares (proviso to
    s. 124(6)) can be claimed back from the **IEPF Authority**.
12. **Paying dividend on calls in advance.** Calls in advance are a debt, not paid-up capital: no
    dividend, and no voting rights until presently payable (s. 50(2)).
13. **Revoking a declared dividend.** It is a debt from the moment of declaration — and under the 2013
    Act that is true of an **interim** dividend as well.
14. **Missing the second half of s. 127 defence (b).** The company must have **communicated** to the
    shareholder that his directions cannot be complied with.

## One-page revision summary

**Who does what.** **Members at the AGM** declare the final dividend, on the **Board's recommendation**
and never above the recommended rate. The **Board** declares an interim dividend, during the year or
between the year-end and the AGM. **s. 2(35)** — "dividend" **includes interim dividend**, so every
obligation applies to both.

**Sources — s. 123(1).** Current-year profits **after Schedule II depreciation**; or undistributed
profits of previous years after depreciation; or **both**; or **money provided by the Central or a
State Government under a government guarantee**. Provisos: transfer to reserves is **voluntary**;
inadequate profits send you to **Rule 3**; **carried-over losses and unprovided depreciation must be
set off first**; **only free reserves**, as defined in **s. 2(43)** (no revaluation, no unrealised or
fair-value gains). **s. 123(6)** — no dividend on **equity shares** while an **ss. 73/74** deposit
default continues.

**Rule 3 — dividend out of accumulated profits.**

| Ceiling | Test | Base |
|---|---|---|
| Rate | ≤ **average of the last three years' rates** (not applicable if **no dividend in each** of those three years) | declared rates |
| Draw | ≤ **one-tenth** of the sum | **paid-up capital + free reserves**, latest audited financial statement |
| Floor | reserves left ≥ **fifteen per cent** | **paid-up capital**, latest audited financial statement |
| Set-off | the draw sets off the **current year's loss first** | current year |

**Interim — s. 123(3).** Sources: **surplus in the P&amp;L account**, profits of the year, or profits
till the **quarter preceding** declaration. If there is a **loss up to the end of that quarter**, the
rate may not exceed the **average of the three preceding years' rates**.

**Form and mode — s. 123(5).** Only to the **registered holder, his order or his banker**; **in cash**,
payable by **cheque, warrant or electronic mode**; **no dividend in kind**; **bonus shares** are a
saved capitalisation.

**Every clock in the chapter.**

| Clock | Period | Runs from |
|---|---|---|
| Deposit in a separate scheduled-bank account (s. 123(4)) | **5 days** | date of **declaration** |
| Pay the dividend or post the warrant (s. 127) | **30 days** | date of **declaration** |
| Transfer unpaid or unclaimed to the Unpaid Dividend Account (s. 124(1)) | **7 days** | **expiry of the 30 days** |
| Statement of unpaid amounts on the websites (s. 124(2)) | **90 days** | **date of that transfer** |
| Money, with interest, to the IEPF (s. 124(5)); shares to the IEPF (s. 124(6)) | **7 years** | **date of that transfer** / seven consecutive years unclaimed |

**Money consequences.** **s. 124(3)** — **12 per cent per annum** for failing to transfer to the
Unpaid Dividend Account, enuring to the **members** in proportion to the amounts unpaid to them.
**s. 127** — **18 per cent per annum simple interest** on the company, and for a director **knowingly a
party**, **imprisonment up to two years and a fine of at least ₹1,000 per day**.

**The five s. 127 defences.** Operation of law · shareholder's directions that cannot be complied with
**and were communicated back** · a **dispute** over the right to receive · a **lawful adjustment**
against a sum due to the company · **any other reason not due to the company's default**.

**IEPF — s. 125.** Credits include the s. 124(5) transfers, unclaimed application money, matured
deposits and debentures with companies, unclaimed preference-share redemption amounts, fractional-share
sale proceeds, disgorged amounts under s. 38(4), grants and donations — the middle group only after
**seven years** unclaimed. Uses: **refunds**, **investor education and awareness**, distribution of
**disgorged amounts** under Court orders, **reimbursement of class-action legal expenses** sanctioned
by the Tribunal. **s. 125(4)** — claim a refund from the **Authority**.

**s. 126 — abeyance.** Transfer instrument delivered but not registered: the **dividend goes to the
Unpaid Dividend Account** unless the **registered holder authorises payment to the transferee in
writing**; **rights and bonus entitlements are held in abeyance**.

**s. 51 — proportional dividend.** Payable on the **amount paid-up**, **if the articles so authorise**
(Table F, Regulation 88). **Calls in advance are excluded**. Without an enabling article, dividend
follows the **nominal amount**.

**Revocation.** A declared dividend is a **debt** and is **irrevocable**, save where the declaration
was unlawful or all shareholders consent — and under the 2013 Act this is true of an **interim**
dividend too.
