A Central Act says an appeal **shall be filed within thirty days from the date of the order**. The
order is dated **31 January 2027**. Two clerks in the same office produce two answers. The first
counts 31 January as day one and reports that the appeal must be filed by **1 March 2027**. The
second excludes 31 January, starts the count on 1 February, and reports **2 March 2027**. The office
is then closed on 2 March for a declared holiday, and the appeal is actually lodged on **3 March**.

Three different provisions decide who is right, and none of them is in the Act that creates the
appeal. **Section 9** of the General Clauses Act says the word "from" excludes the first day, so the
second clerk is right and the deadline is 2 March. **Section 10** says that where the office is
closed on the last day, doing the act on the next open day is in time — so the appeal lodged on
3 March is good. And **section 3(35)** would have decided the answer differently again had the Act
said "one month" instead of "thirty days".

That is the whole point of this chapter. The General Clauses Act 1897 contains almost no substantive
law. It is a **statute about statutes** — a dictionary and a set of default rules that Parliament
writes once so that it need not write them into every Act it passes. For the exam it is a
**definition-and-default paper**: high-scoring, memorisable, and lost only by candidates who guess
where a rule is a default and where it is absolute.

## 1. Purpose and scheme of the General Clauses Act

The Act does four jobs, and an examiner who asks "why do we need a General Clauses Act" wants all
four:

1. **It shortens drafting.** Rather than defining "person", "month", "immovable property" and
   "offence" in every Act, Parliament defines them once. Section 3 is a dictionary that every later
   Central Act borrows.
2. **It secures uniformity.** The same expression carries the same meaning across the statute book,
   so a court is not free to give "good faith" one sense in one Act and another sense in a
   neighbouring Act without the legislature saying so.
3. **It supplies defaults where the drafter is silent.** When does an Act come into force if it says
   nothing? Does "from" include the first day? What happens to a prosecution when the offence-creating
   Act is repealed? Sections 5, 9 and 6 answer without the later Act saying a word.
4. **It guards against slips.** Section 6 saves accrued rights and pending proceedings that a
   careless repeal would otherwise destroy; section 24 keeps subordinate legislation alive across a
   re-enactment.

**Where it applies.** The Act applies to **Central Acts and Regulations** — and, through s. 4 and
s. 4A, the principal s. 3 definitions are carried back to Central Acts made after 1868 and forward to
**rules, regulations and bye-laws made under Central Acts**. It is not a code of general
interpretation for everything; it is a code for **Central** legislation and what hangs off it.

**Where it does not apply.** Four situations, and each is a favourite distractor:

| The Act steps aside where … | Because |
|---|---|
| the enactment being read **defines the expression itself** | the special definition governs; s. 3 opens with "unless there is anything repugnant in the subject or context" |
| there is something **repugnant in the subject or context** | the default is displaced by the sense the provision plainly requires |
| a **different intention appears** from the later Act | ss. 6, 8, 11, 14, 16 and 27 each carry those words expressly |
| the legislation is a **State Act** | each State has its own General Clauses Act; the Central Act does not read a State statute |

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>"Default", not "command".</strong> Nearly every operative section of this Act is
    <em>subject to a contrary indication</em>. Section 6 saves accrued rights "unless a different
    intention appears"; s. 14 makes a power exercisable from time to time "unless a different
    intention appears"; s. 27 deems service "unless a different intention appears" and deems the
    time of service "unless the contrary is proved". The exceptions are <strong>s. 9</strong> (a
    rule of drafting shorthand, not of substance) and <strong>s. 26</strong> (a protection against
    double punishment that a later Act cannot casually displace). Any option that states one of
    these rules as absolute where the section says "unless a different intention appears" is
    testing exactly this.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. Section 3 — the definitions that matter

Section 3 defines more than sixty expressions. The Study Material examines a shortlist. State each
one in the section's own structure — the operative verb matters, because **"shall include"** extends
an ordinary meaning while **"shall mean"** replaces it.

| Expression | The definition | The trap |
|---|---|---|
| **affidavit** | **includes** affirmation and declaration in the case of persons **by law allowed to affirm or declare instead of swearing** | it does not cover an unsworn statement by a person who *was* required to swear |
| **act** | used with reference to **an offence or a civil wrong**, **includes a series of acts**; and words referring to acts done **extend also to illegal omissions** | the extension is not at large — it operates in the offence / civil-wrong context, and the omission must be **illegal** |
| **document** | **includes** any matter written, expressed or described upon any substance by letters, figures or marks (or more than one of those means), **intended to be used or which may be used for recording that matter** | the medium is irrelevant; the *recording purpose* is the test |
| **good faith** | a thing is deemed done in good faith where it is **in fact done honestly**, **whether it is done negligently or not** | honesty alone — contrast the IPC, §3 below |
| **Government** | **includes both** the Central Government **and** any State Government | not "the Union alone" |
| **Central Government** | the **President**, for anything done after the Constitution commenced; **includes** a State Government for functions entrusted to it under Art. 258(1), and the **administrator of a Union territory** acting under Art. 239 | the Union-territory and entrusted-functions limbs are the examinable part |
| **State Government** | in a State, the **Governor**; in a Union territory, the **Central Government** | a Union territory has no "State Government" of its own for this purpose |
| **immovable property** | **includes land, benefits to arise out of land, and things attached to the earth or permanently fastened to anything attached to the earth** | standing timber, growing crops and grass are excluded by the Transfer of Property Act, not by this Act |
| **movable property** | property of **every description except immovable property** | a residual definition — decide immovability first |
| **imprisonment** | imprisonment of **either description** as defined in the Indian Penal Code — **rigorous or simple** | a bare "imprisonment" in a penal provision is not confined to simple imprisonment |
| **month** | a month reckoned according to the **British (Gregorian) calendar** | *not* thirty days |
| **year** | a year reckoned according to the **British (Gregorian) calendar** | *not* 365 days, and not the financial year |
| **oath** | **includes** affirmation and declaration in the case of persons by law allowed to affirm or declare instead of swearing | parallel to "affidavit" and "swear" |
| **swear** | **includes** affirming and declaring in the same class of case | |
| **offence** | any **act or omission made punishable by any law for the time being in force** | punishability, not morality, is the test |
| **person** | **includes any company or association or body of individuals, whether incorporated or not** | an unregistered firm, a club and a trade union are all "persons" |
| **registered** | with reference to a document, **registered in India** under the law for the time being in force for the registration of documents | registration abroad does not satisfy it |
| **rule** | a rule made **in exercise of a power conferred by any enactment**, and **includes a Regulation made as a rule** under any enactment | an internal office circular is not a "rule" |
| **schedule** | a schedule **to the Act or Regulation in which the word occurs** | not a schedule to some other Act |
| **section** | a section **of the Act or Regulation in which the word occurs**; a sub-section is a sub-section of the section in which it occurs | the same self-referential logic |
| **sign** | with reference to a person **unable to write his name**, **includes "mark"** | it does not authorise a mark by a literate signatory |
| **will** | **includes a codicil and every writing making a voluntary posthumous disposition of property** | the label on the document is irrelevant |

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>"Month" is a calendar month, and that changes the answer.</strong> A period of
    <strong>one month from 31 January 2027</strong> ends on <strong>28 February 2027</strong> — the
    corresponding day of the following month, and where there is none, the last day of that month.
    A period of <strong>thirty days from 31 January 2027</strong> ends on <strong>2 March
    2027</strong>. Same-looking words, five days apart. Whenever a stem says "month" or "year", stop
    and use the calendar; whenever it says "days", count days.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 1 — is it immovable property?">
  <p>
    Deviprasad grants Konark Mills the right to <strong>draw water from a well on his land for
    twenty years</strong>, and separately sells it <strong>an overhead water tank bolted to a
    concrete plinth cast into the ground</strong>, a <strong>diesel generator standing loose on the
    factory floor</strong>, and the <strong>right to collect tolls at a private bridge</strong>.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The right to draw water</strong> is a benefit to arise out of land — <strong>immovable
    property</strong>. <strong>The tank</strong> is permanently fastened to something attached to
    the earth — <strong>immovable property</strong>. <strong>The generator</strong> rests on the
    floor by its own weight, is not attached to the earth, and is therefore
    <strong>movable property</strong> under the residual limb of s. 3(36). <strong>The toll
    right</strong> is a benefit arising out of land — <strong>immovable property</strong>. The
    method never changes: test immovability first; whatever fails that test is movable by
    definition.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. Good faith, person and property — three definitions that decide cases

**Good faith.** Under this Act a thing is done in good faith where it is **in fact done honestly,
whether it is done negligently or not**. The test is **subjective honesty**. A clerk who despatches
a notice to a stale address because he never checked the register acts negligently, but if he
believed the address to be current he acts in good faith for the purposes of a Central Act that uses
the expression without defining it.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>"Good faith" here is not "good faith" in the Penal Code.</strong> Section 52 of the
    Indian Penal Code reverses the rule: <em>nothing</em> is said to be done in good faith which is
    done <strong>without due care and attention</strong>. So the same conduct can be in good faith
    under the General Clauses Act and outside good faith under the IPC — honesty is enough for one,
    diligence is demanded by the other. Where an enactment supplies its own definition (as the IPC
    does, and as the Limitation Act 1963 does), that definition governs and s. 3(22) steps aside;
    where an enactment such as the <strong>Negotiable Instruments Act 1881</strong> uses the
    expression without defining it, the General Clauses Act's honesty test carries over and
    negligence alone does not destroy good faith. Options that import "due care and attention" into
    a General Clauses Act question are always wrong.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Person.** The definition **includes any company or association or body of individuals, whether
incorporated or not**. Three consequences worth stating in an answer: a **company** is a person
without needing any further words in the later Act; an **unincorporated** body — a partnership firm,
a club, an unregistered association — is equally a person; and the word "includes" means the
ordinary sense (a **natural person**) survives alongside the extension. A penal provision that
punishes "any person" who fails to file a return therefore reaches a firm as well as its individual
partners, unless the Act says otherwise.

**Immovable and movable property.** The pair is designed to be exhaustive. Section 3(26) **includes**
three categories in immovable property — **land**, **benefits to arise out of land**, and **things
attached to the earth or permanently fastened to anything attached to the earth**. Section 3(36) then
sweeps up everything else: **property of every description except immovable property**. Because the
second definition is residual, a candidate never has to argue that something is movable; he argues
that it is **not immovable**, and movability follows.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>Beware of the Transfer of Property Act overlay.</strong> The General Clauses Act
    definition does <em>not</em> carve out standing timber, growing crops or grass — that exclusion
    lives in <strong>s. 3 of the Transfer of Property Act 1882</strong>. In a question set on the
    General Clauses Act, the correct answer applies the three limbs of s. 3(26) and nothing else. If
    a stem expressly invokes the Transfer of Property Act, say so and apply that carve-out
    instead.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Section 5 — commencement of enactments

Two rules, and students routinely merge them.

**Rule one — which day.** Where a Central Act **is not expressed to come into operation on a
particular day**, it comes into operation on the day it **receives the assent** — of the **President**
for an Act of Parliament (and of the Governor General for a Central Act made before the Constitution
commenced). Where the Act *does* fix a commencement, or leaves it to be appointed by notification,
that provision governs and s. 5 never engages.

**Rule two — which moment.** Unless the contrary is expressed, a Central Act or Regulation is
construed as **coming into operation immediately on the expiration of the day preceding its
commencement** — that is, from the **first moment of the commencement day**. There is no
part-day operation and no waiting until an office opens.

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 2 — assent, notification and the first moment">
  <p>
    The (fictitious) Central Warehousing Reform Act 2027 is passed by both Houses on 9 March 2027 and
    receives the President's assent on <strong>16 March 2027</strong>. Version A of the Bill contained
    no commencement clause. Version B, as enacted, says the Act "shall come into force on such date as
    the Central Government may by notification appoint", and the notification appoints
    <strong>1 April 2027</strong>. A warehousing licence is surrendered at 6.30 a.m. on 1 April 2027.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>Under version A</strong> the Act would have commenced on <strong>16 March 2027</strong>,
    the date of assent — not the date of passing, and not the date of publication in the Gazette.
    <strong>Under version B</strong> s. 5 is displaced: the appointed date governs and the Act
    commences on <strong>1 April 2027</strong>. <strong>The 6.30 a.m. surrender is caught</strong>,
    because the Act operates from the first moment of 1 April, immediately on the expiration of
    31 March. An answer that says the Act "applies only from the time the notification was published"
    or "from office hours on 1 April" loses the mark.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Sections 6 to 8 — the effect of repeal

**Section 6** is the most heavily examined provision in the chapter. Where a Central Act or Regulation
repeals an enactment, then — **unless a different intention appears** — the repeal shall **not** do
five things:

| The repeal does NOT … | What that saves | Clause |
|---|---|---|
| **revive** anything not in force or existing at the time the repeal takes effect | a provision the repealed Act had itself killed stays dead | 6(a) |
| affect the **previous operation** of the repealed enactment or **anything duly done or suffered** thereunder | licences granted, assessments made, acts already done | 6(b) |
| affect any **right, privilege, obligation or liability** acquired, accrued or incurred under it | a vested right of appeal, an accrued tax liability | 6(c) |
| affect any **penalty, forfeiture or punishment** incurred for an offence committed against it | the offender cannot escape by repeal | 6(d) |
| affect any **investigation, legal proceeding or remedy** in respect of any such right, liability, penalty or punishment | a pending inquiry or prosecution | 6(e) |

and — the operative tail that candidates forget to quote — **any such investigation, legal proceeding
or remedy may be instituted, continued or enforced, and any such penalty, forfeiture or punishment
may be imposed, as if the repealing Act had not been passed**. Note the word **instituted**: a
prosecution may be *begun* after the repeal for an offence committed before it.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Section 6 is a default, not a guarantee.</strong> Every one of the five savings is
    subject to the opening words <strong>"unless a different intention appears"</strong>. A repealing
    Act that expressly wipes out pending proceedings does exactly that, and s. 6 yields. The examiner's
    trap runs the other way too: candidates say the repeal "automatically revives" the law that the
    repealed Act had itself replaced. It does not — s. 6(a) says so, and <strong>s. 7</strong> makes
    the point unmistakable.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 6A — repeal of an amending Act.** Where a later Central Act repeals an enactment **by which
the text of a Central Act or Regulation had been amended** by express omission, insertion or
substitution, then, unless a different intention appears, the repeal **does not affect the
continuance of that amendment**. The reason is practical: an amending Act is spent once its text is
absorbed into the principal Act, and repealing the spent shell must not unpick the principal Act. So
repealing an amendment Act of 2019 does **not** restore the pre-2019 text.

**Section 7 — revival requires express words.** To revive, wholly or partially, an enactment wholly or
partially repealed, a later Central Act must **expressly state that purpose**. Implied revival does not
exist. Section 7 and s. 6(a) are two sides of one coin: nothing comes back to life by accident.

**Section 8 — references to repealed enactments.** Where a Central Act **repeals and re-enacts, with
or without modification**, a provision of a former enactment, then references to the repealed
provision **in any other enactment or in any instrument** are, unless a different intention appears,
**construed as references to the re-enacted provision**. Three things are examinable here: the
requirement is **repeal *and* re-enactment** (a bare repeal leaves the reference dangling); the
re-enactment may be **with modification**; and the rule reaches **instruments** — contracts, deeds,
notifications — not only other statutes.

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 3 — a repeal, a prosecution and a dangling reference">
  <p>
    The (fictitious) Central Grain Levy Act 1994 made short-levy an offence and empowered the
    Government to notify levy rates. On <strong>1 July 2026</strong> the Central Grain Levy Act 2026
    repeals it and re-enacts the offence provision in substantially the same terms as its s. 19. On
    <strong>4 March 2026</strong> Bharti Agro was found to have short-levied; no prosecution had begun
    by 1 July. A 2011 supply contract between Bharti Agro and a State corporation refers to
    "s. 14 of the Central Grain Levy Act 1994". A rate notification issued under the 1994 Act is still
    in force and is not inconsistent with the 2026 Act.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>1 · The offence survives.</strong> Section 6(d) saves the penalty incurred for an offence
    committed against the repealed Act, and s. 6(e) with its tail allows the prosecution to be
    <em>instituted</em> after the repeal, as if the 2026 Act had not been passed.
    <strong>2 · The contract reference.</strong> Because the 1994 provision was repealed
    <em>and re-enacted</em>, s. 8 makes the contract's reference read as a reference to the
    corresponding provision of the 2026 Act; the clause does not become meaningless.
    <strong>3 · The notification.</strong> Section 24 keeps it alive — it continues in force so far
    as it is not inconsistent with the re-enacted provisions, and is deemed to have been issued under
    them, until superseded. <strong>4 · What does not happen.</strong> The pre-1994 law is not
    revived (s. 6(a) and s. 7), and nothing is void merely because the enabling Act has gone.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. Sections 9 and 10 — computation of time

**Section 9** is a rule of drafting shorthand with enormous practical bite. In a Central Act, it is
**sufficient**, for the purpose of **excluding the first** in a series of days or any other period of
time, to use the word **"from"**; and, for the purpose of **including the last**, to use the word
**"to"**.

<div class="formula">"FROM" a date → the first day is EXCLUDED, counting starts the next day · "TO" a date → the last day is INCLUDED</div>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 4 — the same period, counted correctly">
  <p>
    A Central Act allows an objection to be filed <strong>within 21 days from 5 March 2027</strong>,
    and a licence issued under the same Act is expressed to run <strong>from 1 April 2026 to
    31 March 2027</strong>.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — 21 days from 5 March 2027</div>
    5 March is <strong>excluded</strong> by s. 9 → day 1 = 6 March 2027<br />
    Days remaining in March after the 5th = 26 → day 21 falls inside March<br />
    Day 21 = 5 March + 21 days = <strong>26 March 2027</strong><br />
    Counting 5 March as day 1 would give 25 March — the classic one-day error
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — the licence period</div>
    "from 1 April 2026" → 1 April is <strong>excluded</strong> as the starting point of the count<br />
    "to 31 March 2027" → 31 March is <strong>included</strong>, so the licence is live all day on
    31 March<br />
    Days in the period = 2 April 2026 to 31 March 2027 inclusive = <strong>364</strong>
  </div>
  <p>
    The examinable idea is not the arithmetic; it is that the <strong>two words do different work</strong>.
    "From" pushes the start forward by a day. "To" holds the end open for the whole of the last day. A
    stem that says "between 1 April and 31 March" uses neither word and is deliberately ambiguous —
    say so and apply the section's rule only to the word actually used.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 10 — the closed-day rule.** Where a Central Act directs or allows any **act or proceeding**
to be done or taken **in any court or office** on a certain day or **within a prescribed period**, and
the court or office is **closed on that day or on the last day of the period**, the act or proceeding
is considered done **in due time** if it is done on the **next day afterwards on which the court or
office is open**.

Four conditions, all of which must be satisfied:

1. the act or proceeding is one to be done **in a court or office** (not, for example, a payment to be
   made to a private party);
2. it is directed or allowed by a **Central Act or Regulation** (or subordinate legislation under one);
3. the **last day** of the period — not some intermediate day — is a day on which that court or office
   is **closed**; and
4. the act is then done on the **next open day**, without further delay.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Section 10 does not apply where the Limitation Act 1963 applies.</strong> The section
    carries its own carve-out: it does not extend to any act or proceeding to which the Limitation Act
    applies. That is not a gap in the protection — the Limitation Act has its own closed-day
    provision — but it decides <em>which statute you cite</em>. A suit or an appeal governed by the
    Limitation Act is saved by that Act, not by s. 10. An answer that cites s. 10 for a time-barred
    suit is citing the wrong provision even when it reaches the right result. The second trap is
    subtler: a holiday <strong>in the middle</strong> of the period does nothing at all. Section 10
    looks only at the <strong>last</strong> day.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. Section 27 — service by post

Where a Central Act **authorises or requires a document to be served by post** — whether the Act uses
the word "serve", "give", "send" or any other expression — then, unless a different intention appears,
service is **deemed to be effected** by doing three things, and is deemed to have been effected at a
particular **time**.

<div class="formula">DEEMED SERVICE (s. 27) = properly ADDRESSING + PRE-PAYING + POSTING BY REGISTERED POST a letter containing the document · deemed effected at the time the letter would be delivered in the ORDINARY COURSE OF POST · "unless the contrary is proved"</div>

Take the two halves separately, because they are rebuttable to different degrees:

- **The fact of service** follows from the three acts — proper address, pre-payment and posting **by
  registered post**. Drop any one of the three and the deeming provision never engages: an ordinary
  unregistered letter, or a registered letter bearing a wrong address, is outside s. 27 and the sender
  is thrown back on proving actual receipt.
- **The time of service** is when the letter **would be delivered in the ordinary course of post** —
  and this limb is expressly displaced where **the contrary is proved**. The addressee may show that
  the letter never arrived, or arrived later; the sender may show earlier delivery. Refusal to accept
  a properly tendered registered letter does not help the addressee.

**Section 9, section 10 and section 27 answer three different questions**, and mixing them is the
commonest way to lose a mark in this chapter:

| Provision | Question it answers | Rule | Displaced by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **s. 9** | *When does the period start and end?* | "from" excludes the first day; "to" includes the last day | the Act using different language |
| **s. 10** | *What if the last day is a closed day?* | the act may be done on the **next open day** the court or office is open | the **Limitation Act 1963** applying to the act or proceeding |
| **s. 27** | *When is a posted document served?* | deemed served when it would arrive in the **ordinary course of post**, on proper address + pre-payment + **registered** post | a **different intention** in the Act; and the time limb, by **proof to the contrary** |

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 5 — a deadline, a holiday and a posted notice">
  <p>
    A Central Act requires an objection to be lodged in the office of a Controller
    <strong>within 30 days from 2 February 2027</strong>. The Controller's office is closed on
    <strong>4 and 5 March 2027</strong> and reopens on <strong>6 March 2027</strong>. Separately, the
    Controller posts his order to Meghdoot Textiles by properly addressed, pre-paid
    <strong>registered</strong> post on <strong>27 February 2027</strong>; evidence shows that a
    letter so posted on that route is delivered in the ordinary course of post on the
    <strong>fourth day</strong> after posting.
  </p>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 1 — the last day of the 30-day period (s. 9)</div>
    2 February 2027 is <strong>excluded</strong>; 2027 is not a leap year, so February has 28 days<br />
    Days available in February after the 2nd = 26 → 30 − 26 = 4 days into March<br />
    Last day = <strong>4 March 2027</strong>
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 2 — the closed last day (s. 10)</div>
    4 March 2027 (the last day) — office <strong>closed</strong><br />
    5 March 2027 — office <strong>closed</strong><br />
    Next day on which the office is open = <strong>6 March 2027</strong> → lodging on 6 March is in
    due time
  </div>
  <div class="working-note">
    <div class="working-note-label">Working note 3 — deemed service of the order (s. 27)</div>
    Posted 27 February 2027; ordinary course of post = fourth day after posting<br />
    27 Feb + 4 days = <strong>3 March 2027</strong> (February 2027 has 28 days)<br />
    Deemed served 3 March 2027 — <strong>unless the contrary is proved</strong>
  </div>
  <p>
    Three provisions, three answers, and none of them borrowed from the others. Note also what would
    have changed nothing: had the office been closed on <strong>20 February</strong>, an intermediate
    day, s. 10 would not have moved the deadline by an hour.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. Sections 11, 13 and 26 — distance, gender and double punishment

**Section 11 — measurement of distance.** For the purposes of a Central Act, and unless a different
intention appears, a distance is measured **in a straight line on a horizontal plane**. Two
consequences follow directly and are both examined: the **route actually travelled is irrelevant** —
a road that winds for nine kilometres between two points five kilometres apart does not make the
distance nine; and **vertical elevation is ignored** — a hill between the two points does not lengthen
the distance. The rule is often described as the "crow-flight" or "as the crow flies" measure.

**Section 13 — gender and number.** In all Central Acts and Regulations, unless there is anything
repugnant in the subject or context, **words importing the masculine gender are taken to include
females**, and **words in the singular include the plural, and words in the plural include the
singular**. So a provision that speaks of "he" or "the director" reaches a woman and reaches several
directors. The safety valve is the repugnancy clause: a provision about maternity benefit or about a
sole surviving member cannot be read through s. 13 to defeat its own subject.

**Section 26 — an act punishable under two enactments.** Where an act or omission constitutes an
offence under **two or more enactments**, the offender is **liable to be prosecuted and punished under
either or any of those enactments**, **but shall not be liable to be punished twice for the same
offence**.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Section 26 bars double PUNISHMENT, not double prosecution.</strong> The prosecuting agency
    is free to choose its enactment, and nothing in the section prevents proceedings being launched
    under one enactment after an acquittal under another. What the section forbids is
    <strong>punishing the offender twice for the same offence</strong>. Options that say the offender
    "may be prosecuted only once", "must be prosecuted under the special Act", or "must be prosecuted
    under the enactment carrying the higher penalty" are all wrong. The section also does not let the
    offender choose which enactment he is tried under.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Sections 14 to 24 — powers, functionaries and subordinate legislation

The last block of the chapter is a set of short, high-yield defaults about **statutory powers** and the
**people who exercise them**. Learn them as one list.

| Section | The default |
|---|---|
| **14** | Where a Central Act confers a **power**, then, unless a different intention appears, the power may be exercised **from time to time as occasion requires**. A power is not spent on first use. |
| **15** | Where a power to **appoint** a person to an office or to execute a function is conferred, the appointment may, unless otherwise expressly provided, be made **either by name or by virtue of office** (*ex officio*). |
| **16** | Where a power to make an **appointment** is conferred, the authority having for the time being the power to appoint also has the power to **suspend or dismiss** any person appointed — whether appointed by itself or by another authority in exercise of that power. |
| **17** | To apply a law to **every person for the time being executing the functions of an office**, it is sufficient to mention the **official title** of the officer presently executing them, or of the officer by whom they are commonly executed. |
| **18** | To indicate the relation of a law to the **successors** of functionaries, or of bodies politic or corporate, it is sufficient to express its relation to **the functionaries or bodies** themselves. |
| **19** | To express that a law relating to the **chief or superior** of an office applies to **deputies or subordinates lawfully performing his duties in his place**, it is sufficient to **prescribe the duty of the superior**. |
| **20** | Expressions used in a **notification, order, scheme, rule, form or bye-law** made under a Central Act carry, unless repugnant to the subject or context, **the same meanings as in the Act conferring the power**. |
| **21** | A power to issue **notifications, orders, rules or bye-laws** includes a power — exercisable **in the like manner and subject to the like sanction and conditions** — to **add to, amend, vary or rescind** them. |
| **22** | Where a Central Act is **not to come into force immediately on passing**, a power under it to make rules or bye-laws or to issue certain orders may be exercised **at any time after the passing** of the Act; but such rules, bye-laws or orders **do not take effect until the Act commences**. |
| **23** | Where a rule-making power is given **subject to previous publication**: publish a **draft** for the information of persons likely to be affected, in such manner as the authority deems sufficient (or as prescribed); publish with it a **notice specifying a date on or after which the draft will be taken into consideration**; **consider objections and suggestions** received before that date; and publication of the rule in the **Official Gazette** as having been made after previous publication is **conclusive proof** that it was duly made. |
| **24** | Where a Central Act is **repealed and re-enacted**, any **appointment, notification, order, scheme, rule, form or bye-law** made under the repealed Act **continues in force** so far as it is **not inconsistent** with the re-enacted provisions, and is **deemed made under them**, **until superseded**. |

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>Section 21 is about symmetry, not about freedom.</strong> The power to amend or rescind is
    exercisable <em>in the like manner and subject to the like sanction and conditions</em>. So if the
    parent Act required consultation, or previous publication, or the approval of another authority
    before the order was issued, the same steps are needed to vary or rescind it. Options that let an
    authority rescind its own notification "at any time and in any manner" miss the qualifying words.
    Note also the list: <strong>notifications, orders, rules and bye-laws</strong>. Section 20, by
    contrast, has a longer list that adds <strong>schemes and forms</strong>, and does a different job
    — it fixes the <em>meaning</em> of expressions, not the power to change the instrument.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 6 — a power exercised, varied and inherited">
  <p>
    A Central Act empowers "the Director of Standards" to notify testing fees, and requires such
    notifications to be issued only <strong>after consultation with an advisory committee</strong>.
    The Act was passed on 12 January 2027 but is to come into force on 1 May 2027. In March 2027 the
    Director makes the fee rules. In June 2027 he wants to raise the fees without going back to the
    committee. The Director is then transferred and a Deputy Director lawfully performs his duties;
    the Deputy also wishes to <strong>dismiss</strong> an inspector whom the previous Director had
    appointed by name.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>1 · The March rules.</strong> Section 22 lets the power be exercised at any time after the
    Act was <em>passed</em>, so the rules are validly made — but they <strong>take effect only from
    1 May 2027</strong>, when the Act commences. <strong>2 · The June increase.</strong> Section 21
    permits amendment or variation, but only <em>in the like manner and subject to the like sanction
    and conditions</em> — the advisory committee must be consulted again. <strong>3 · The
    Deputy.</strong> Section 19 makes the law relating to the superior apply to a subordinate lawfully
    performing his duties in his place, and s. 17 attaches the law to whoever for the time being
    executes the functions of the office. <strong>4 · The dismissal.</strong> Section 16 gives the
    authority having <em>for the time being</em> the power to appoint the power to suspend or dismiss,
    <strong>even though the appointment was made by another authority</strong> in exercise of that
    power. <strong>5 · The power itself.</strong> Section 14 means the fee-notifying power was not
    exhausted when it was first used in March.
  </p>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

1. **Reading "month" as thirty days.** "Month" and "year" are reckoned by the **British (Gregorian)
   calendar**. One month from 31 January 2027 ends on 28 February 2027; thirty days from the same date
   ends on 2 March 2027.
2. **Importing "due care and attention" into good faith.** Under the General Clauses Act good faith is
   **honesty, negligent or not**. The due-care standard is **IPC s. 52**, and it governs only where the
   enactment supplies it.
3. **Counting the first day.** "From" **excludes** it. "To" **includes** the last day. The two words do
   opposite work and both are examined.
4. **Applying s. 10 to a mid-period holiday.** Section 10 looks only at the **last** day, and only at
   the closure of the **court or office** where the act is to be done.
5. **Citing s. 10 where the Limitation Act 1963 applies.** The section expressly carves those acts and
   proceedings out. Right result, wrong provision, no mark.
6. **Turning s. 26 into a bar on double prosecution.** It bars **double punishment** for the same
   offence; the choice of enactment for prosecution remains with the prosecutor.
7. **Forgetting that s. 27 needs registered post.** Proper address **plus** pre-payment **plus**
   **registered** post. Ordinary post does not attract the deeming provision at all, and the time limb
   is rebuttable by proof to the contrary.
8. **Saying a repeal revives the earlier law.** It does not — **s. 6(a)** — and revival needs
   **express** words under **s. 7**. Equally, repealing an **amending** Act does not undo the amendment
   (**s. 6A**).
9. **Treating the s. 6 savings as absolute.** All five operate **"unless a different intention
   appears"**.
10. **Missing "in the like manner and subject to the like sanction and conditions" in s. 21.** The
    power to vary or rescind carries the parent conditions with it.
11. **Saying rules made under s. 22 are void.** They are validly **made** before commencement; they
    simply do not **take effect** until the Act commences.
12. **Applying the Act to a State Act.** Each State has its own General Clauses Act. The 1897 Act reads
    **Central** Acts and Regulations, and the instruments made under them.

## One-page revision summary

**What the Act is.** A statute about statutes: a **dictionary** (s. 3), a set of **defaults** for
commencement, repeal, time and powers, and a **guard** against drafting slips. It applies to **Central
Acts and Regulations**, and to rules, regulations and bye-laws made under them. It steps aside where
the enactment defines the term, where the subject or context is repugnant, where a different intention
appears, and for **State** legislation.

**Definitions to be able to state cold.** **affidavit** — includes affirmation and declaration for
those allowed to affirm. **act** — with reference to an offence or civil wrong, includes a **series of
acts**; words referring to acts done extend to **illegal omissions**. **document** — any matter
recorded on any substance by letters, figures or marks, intended or usable for recording it. **good
faith** — done **honestly, whether negligently or not**. **Government** — includes **both** the Central
and any State Government. **Central Government** — the President; includes a State Government for
Art. 258(1) entrusted functions and a Union territory administrator under Art. 239. **State
Government** — the Governor in a State; the **Central Government** in a Union territory. **immovable
property** — land, **benefits to arise out of land**, things attached to the earth or **permanently
fastened** to anything so attached. **movable property** — every other kind. **imprisonment** — of
**either description**, rigorous or simple. **month** / **year** — the **British (Gregorian)
calendar**. **oath** and **swear** — include affirmation and declaration. **offence** — any act or
omission **made punishable** by law in force. **person** — includes any **company, association or body
of individuals, incorporated or not**. **registered** — registered **in India**. **rule** — made under
a power conferred by an enactment; includes a Regulation made as a rule. **schedule** / **section** —
of the Act in which the word occurs. **sign** — includes **mark** for one unable to write. **will** —
includes a **codicil** and every writing making a voluntary posthumous disposition.

**Commencement (s. 5).** No commencement provision → the Act commences on the day it receives the
**President's assent**. Commencement on a day → **from the first moment** of that day, immediately on
the expiration of the preceding day.

**Repeal (ss. 6, 6A, 7, 8, 24).** Unless a different intention appears, a repeal does not **revive**
(6(a)), does not affect **previous operation or anything duly done** (6(b)), **accrued rights and
liabilities** (6(c)), **penalties incurred** (6(d)), or **investigations, proceedings and remedies**
(6(e)) — which may be **instituted, continued or enforced** as if the repealing Act had not been
passed. **s. 6A** — repealing an amending Act does not undo the amendment. **s. 7** — revival needs
**express** words. **s. 8** — on repeal **and re-enactment**, references in other enactments **and in
instruments** read as references to the re-enacted provision. **s. 24** — appointments, notifications,
orders, schemes, rules, forms and bye-laws **continue** so far as not inconsistent, **until
superseded**.

**Time (ss. 9, 10) and service (s. 27).** **"From" excludes the first day; "to" includes the last.**
Act to be done in a **court or office** and the **last day** is a closed day → the **next open day**
suffices — **except** where the **Limitation Act 1963** applies. Service by post is **deemed effected**
by **properly addressing + pre-paying + posting by registered post**, at the time the letter would
arrive **in the ordinary course of post**, **unless the contrary is proved**.

**Miscellany.** **s. 11** — distance in a **straight line on a horizontal plane**. **s. 13** —
masculine includes feminine; singular includes plural and vice versa, unless repugnant. **s. 26** —
prosecution under **either or any** enactment, but **no punishment twice** for the same offence.

**Powers and functionaries.** **s. 14** — exercisable **from time to time**. **s. 15** — appointment
**by name or by office**. **s. 16** — power to appoint includes power to **suspend or dismiss**, even
one appointed by another authority. **ss. 17–19** — official titles, **successors**, and **deputies
lawfully performing** the superior's duties. **s. 20** — expressions in subordinate instruments carry
the **parent Act's meanings**. **s. 21** — power to issue includes power to **add to, amend, vary or
rescind**, **in the like manner and subject to the like sanction and conditions**. **s. 22** — rules
may be **made** between passing and commencement but **take effect only on commencement**. **s. 23** —
previous publication: **draft**, **notice of the date of consideration**, **consideration of
objections**, and Gazette publication as **conclusive proof** of due making.
