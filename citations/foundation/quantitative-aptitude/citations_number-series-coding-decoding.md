## Reading a number series by its differences

- **Convention relied on:** the reliable first step on any number or letter
  series is to write the row of differences between consecutive terms; a constant
  difference is an arithmetic series, a difference row that is itself a simple
  series is a difference series, and a difference row that swings up and down
  signals an alternating (two-thread) series. When the differences do not settle,
  the ratios of consecutive terms are tested next.
- **Source:** standard convention of aptitude and reasoning tests; SM Ch 9 —
  reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f3c9-001 to q-f3c9-004, q-f3c9-046; notes §1 and §2.
- **Spot-checked by:** _(blank until a human checks)_

## Arithmetic, geometric and two-step series

- **Conventions relied on:** an arithmetic series adds a constant difference; a
  geometric series multiplies by a constant ratio; a series may instead use a
  growing multiplier (as in 1, 2, 6, 24, 120 with multipliers 2, 3, 4, 5) or a
  two-step rule that multiplies by a fixed number and then adds or subtracts a
  fixed number (as in "double and add one"). A term is confirmed to follow a rule
  once the rule fits at least two consecutive steps.
- **Source:** standard; SM Ch 9 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f3c9-005 to q-f3c9-009, q-f3c9-013; notes §2 and §3, and worked
  example 2.

## Square, cube and shifted-power series

- **Convention relied on:** many series are the squares (1, 4, 9, 16, 25, ...) or
  the cubes (1, 8, 27, 64, ...) of the counting numbers, sometimes shifted by a
  fixed amount (n² + 1, n² − 1, n³ + 1). Recognising the terms as powers is
  faster and less error-prone than chasing the difference row, though the
  difference row (the odd numbers, for a square series) confirms the same answer.
- **Source:** standard; SM Ch 9 — reference_only.

## Alternating, mixed and Fibonacci-type series

- **Conventions relied on:** an alternating series interleaves two simple series,
  the odd-position terms following one rule and the even-position terms another,
  and is solved by reading the two threads apart; a mixed series applies a
  repeating cycle of operations (such as +1, +4, +1, +4); a sum (Fibonacci-type)
  series builds each term by adding the two before it.
- **Source:** standard; SM Ch 9 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f3c9-015 to q-f3c9-018; notes §5, and worked example 3.

## Missing term and wrong term

- **Conventions relied on:** in a missing-term question the rule is recovered from
  the terms on both sides of the gap and the answer is checked against both
  sides; in a wrong-term question the rule is built from a stretch of terms that
  clearly agree, and the single term that breaks that rule is named (with the
  value it should have held).
- **Source:** standard; SM Ch 9 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f3c9-019 to q-f3c9-022; notes §6, and worked examples 4 and 9.

## Letter series and the place value of letters

- **Convention relied on:** each letter carries a place value counting from the
  front, A = 1 to Z = 26; a letter series is solved by converting to place
  values, reasoning in numbers, and converting back, with a step past Z wrapping
  to A and a step past A wrapping to Z. An alternating letter series interleaves a
  forward thread and a backward thread.
- **Source:** standard reasoning-test convention; SM Ch 9 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f3c9-047, q-f3c9-048, q-f3c9-049; notes §7, and worked example 9.

## Coding and decoding by letter shifts, reversal and substitution

- **Conventions relied on:** a letter-shift code moves every letter the same
  number of places, forward or backward, with wrap-around at the ends of the
  alphabet; a reversal code writes the word backward; a substitution language
  gives coded phrases and is decoded by matching a shared code word to the shared
  meaning of the phrases that contain it; a partial rule may change only the
  vowels (or only the consonants) and leave the rest unchanged.
- **Source:** standard; SM Ch 9 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f3c9-023 to q-f3c9-029, q-f3c9-034, q-f3c9-035, q-f3c9-037;
  notes §8, and worked examples 5 and 7.

## Coding and decoding with numbers and symbols

- **Conventions relied on:** a position code writes each letter as its place value
  (A = 1 to Z = 26); a reverse position code writes each letter as 27 − place
  value (A = 26 to Z = 1); a sum code adds the place values; a code may apply an
  operation to the place value (doubling it, or a rule that depends on whether it
  is odd or even); a symbol code is evaluated by swapping every symbol for its
  meaning and then doing the arithmetic in the ordinary order.
- **Source:** standard; SM Ch 9 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f3c9-030 to q-f3c9-033, q-f3c9-036; notes §9, and worked
  example 6.

## Odd man out

- **Convention relied on:** an odd-man-out group shares a single property — a
  number property (perfect square, perfect cube, prime, multiple of some number),
  a fixed relation inside each pair, or an internal letter-gap pattern — and the
  answer is the one member that lacks it. The item that merely looks different
  (the largest, the only even one) is a decoy.
- **Source:** standard; SM Ch 9 — reference_only.

# Reviewer's checklist — conventions chosen where practice varies

These are editorial calls, not sourced positions. Each one changes what a student
is taught, and each needs a human to confirm it against the SM.

1. **Wrap-around direction in a letter shift.** The chapter treats a forward shift
   past Z as returning to A, and a backward shift past A as returning to Z
   (q-f3c9-025 sends A back to Z). If the SM's coding examples never cross an
   alphabet boundary, the wrap rule is still standard, but confirm the SM does not
   instead teach a 26-letter *non-wrapping* convention for any question type.
   Only q-f3c9-025 depends on the wrap; every other shift question is built to
   stay inside A–Z.

2. **Reverse alphabet numbering.** §9 introduces A = 26 ... Z = 1 as one coding
   rule via 27 − place value (q-f3c9-032). Confirm the SM presents the reverse
   numbering the same way (as a rule stated per question) rather than as a
   standing table students are expected to have memorised.

3. **How a wrong term is reported.** In q-f3c9-020 and q-f3c9-022 the chapter
   names the offending term *and* the value it should have held. If the SM's
   convention is to name only the term (or only its position), the option wording
   would change but not the key. Confirm which form the SM uses, since students
   match option wording literally.

4. **Odd-man-out with a genuinely ambiguous group.** Every odd-man-out item here
   is built so exactly one member fails a single clear rule (q-f3c9-038 to
   q-f3c9-045). Confirm the SM does not set groups where two different rules each
   single out a different member; the chapter deliberately avoids that style.

5. **Letter series worked on place values.** §7 teaches converting letters to
   numbers, solving, and converting back, rather than reciting the alphabet.
   q-f3c9-047, q-f3c9-048 and q-f3c9-049 all follow that method. If the SM
   presents letter series purely as alphabet-position tables, the method differs
   in presentation only; the keys are unaffected.

6. **Section count and length.** The notes run to 12 numbered sections and about
   540 lines, with 9 worked examples. The verifier
   (`scripts/verify_numerical/verify_number-series-coding-decoding.py`) recomputes
   every `numerical: true` answer — the series next-terms from the given terms and
   the coded words from the stated shift/reversal/position rule — so no key here
   rests on the prose alone.
