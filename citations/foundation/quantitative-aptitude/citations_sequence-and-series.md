## Sequence, series and progression

- **Result relied on:** a sequence is a set of numbers written in a definite
  order, each number being a term; a series is the sum of the terms of a
  sequence; a progression is a sequence formed by a stated rule linking each
  term to the one before it.

## Arithmetic progression: the nth term and the sum

- **Result relied on, in the conventional statement:** for an AP with first term
  a and common difference d,

  > Tₙ = a + (n − 1)d, and Sₙ = (n ÷ 2)[2a + (n − 1)d] = (n ÷ 2)(a + l),

  where l is the last of the n terms summed. The two sum forms are equivalent by
  the substitution l = a + (n − 1)d. Also Tₙ = Sₙ − Sₙ₋₁.
- **Source:** standard result of elementary algebra; SM Ch 6 — reference_only.

## Counting terms and counting from the end (AP)

## Geometric progression: the nth term and the sum

- **Result relied on, in the conventional statement:** for a GP with first term a
  and common ratio r ≠ 0,

  > Tₙ = a·rⁿ⁻¹; Sₙ = a(1 − rⁿ) ÷ (1 − r) when r < 1 and Sₙ = a(rⁿ − 1) ÷ (r − 1)
  > when r > 1; Sₙ = na when r = 1; and Sₙ = (l·r − a) ÷ (r − 1) where l is the
  > last term.

  The nth term from the end is l ÷ rⁿ⁻¹.
- **Source:** standard result of elementary algebra; SM Ch 6 — reference_only.

## The sum of an infinite GP

- **Result relied on:** an infinite GP has a sum only when |r| < 1, and that sum
  is S∞ = a ÷ (1 − r). The justification given is that rⁿ tends to 0 as n grows

## Arithmetic mean, geometric mean, and the AM–GM relation

- **Result relied on:** for two numbers a and b the arithmetic mean is
  A = (a + b) ÷ 2, and three numbers are in AP exactly when the middle is the
  arithmetic mean of the other two. For two positive numbers the geometric mean
  is G = √(ab), and three numbers are in GP exactly when the square of the middle
  equals the product of the other two. For positive a and b, A ≥ G, with equality
  only when a = b, since A − G = (√a − √b)² ÷ 2. Further, a + b = 2A, ab = G²,
  and a and b are the roots of x² − 2Ax + G² = 0.
- **Source:** standard results; SM Ch 6 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f3c6-025, q-f3c6-044, q-f3c6-046, q-f3c6-047, cs-f3c6-03-c;
  notes §8, §13, §14 and worked examples 8(b), 9.
- **Spot-checked by:** _(blank until a human checks)_

## Inserting n means between two numbers

- **Result relied on:** inserting n arithmetic means between a and b produces a
  single AP of n + 2 terms with d = (b − a) ÷ (n + 1), and the n means sum to
  n(a + b) ÷ 2. Inserting n geometric means between a and b produces a single GP
  of n + 2 terms with r = (b ÷ a)^[1 ÷ (n + 1)], and the n means multiply to
  (ab)^(n/2).
- **Source:** standard; SM Ch 6 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f3c6-026, q-f3c6-027, q-f3c6-045; notes §8, §13 and worked
  examples 5, 8.

# Reviewer's checklist — conventions where practice varies

Each item below is an editorial call. The mathematics is not in doubt; the
wording or the counting convention is, and a different SM presentation would
change how a student matches an option to a remembered form. **Confirm each
against the May 2026 SM before removing the draft badge.**

1. **Does a single number, or a two-term list, count as a progression?**
   The chapter treats a progression as needing enough terms for a common
   difference or ratio to be observed, and never asks a student to classify a
   one-term or two-term list. Some texts say every two-term sequence is
   trivially both an AP and a GP. No question in this bank turns on the answer,
   but if the SM states a position, §1 should be aligned with it.

2. **Is the geometric mean of two positive numbers the positive root only?**
   The chapter takes G = √(ab) as the **positive** root throughout, and
   q-f3c6-044 and cs-f3c6-03-c both rely on it (16 and 30, not ±16 and ±30).
   Strictly, G² = ab has two roots, and a GP with a negative ratio would use the
   negative one. The convention here is the usual Foundation one — positive
   numbers, positive mean — but a reviewer should confirm the SM says so
   explicitly rather than leaving it implied. The same call is made when
   inserting geometric means (q-f3c6-045 takes r = 5, not −5).

3. **Is an infinite GP with r = 1 "divergent" or does it simply "have no sum"?**
   The chapter says the sum to infinity **does not exist** and gives the reason
   (the partial sums are na and grow without limit). It deliberately avoids the
   words "divergent" and "convergent", which belong to a later syllabus.
   q-f3c6-039 option D and q-f3c6-042 both use the "no sum exists" phrasing. If
   the SM uses "divergent", the option text in q-f3c6-042 should adopt that word
   so students recognise it.

4. **The r = −1 case.** The chapter lists it separately (partial sums oscillate
   between a and 0, so no sum exists). Some presentations fold r = −1 into
   "|r| ≥ 1" without comment. No key depends on this, but §11's table would need
   trimming if the SM does not treat the case.

5. **Does "insert n means" include the two endpoints?**
   The chapter says it does **not**: inserting n means between a and b gives a
   progression of n + 2 terms, of which the n means are the inner terms, and the
   number of gaps is n + 1. q-f3c6-026, q-f3c6-027 and q-f3c6-045 all depend on
   this, and so do their distractors (the n-versus-n+1 divisor error). This is
   the standard convention, but it is also the single most common source of a
   one-place shift in a student's answer, so a reviewer should confirm the SM
   phrases it the same way.

6. **Endpoints of a stated range.** §6 reads "between 100 and 500" as excluding
   both endpoints and "from 100 to 500" as including them. In the bank the
   ambiguity is designed out rather than relied on: q-f3c6-013 says "greater
   than 50 and less than 300" in words, because 300 is itself a multiple of 6
   and an inclusive reading would change the total; and in q-f3c6-020 neither
   100 nor 500 is a term of the progression, so both readings agree. If the SM
   states a different default for the word "between", only §6's paragraph needs
   rewording — no key moves.

7. **Rounding when a target is first crossed.** q-f3c6-022 and cs-f3c6-01-c both
   round the period count **up**, on the ground that the target must actually be
   reached. Confirm that the SM's worked examples of this pattern do the same
   rather than reporting the last period below the target.

8. **Which arrangement of the finite GP sum is taught first.** The chapter
   presents a(1 − rⁿ) ÷ (1 − r) for r < 1 and a(rⁿ − 1) ÷ (r − 1) for r > 1, and
   says explicitly that they are the same formula. Students match printed forms
   literally, so if the SM leads with only one arrangement, §10 should follow its
   order.

9. **Notation.** The chapter writes Tₙ for the nth term and Sₙ for the sum of n
   terms, and uses l for the last term. Some texts use aₙ or tₙ. §1 and §2 use
   aₙ for the general term of a plain sequence and then switch to Tₙ inside the
   progressions, which is deliberate but should match the SM's usage.

10. **Length of the notes.** The chapter runs to about 1,245 lines with 18
    numbered sections, 12 fully worked examples and a one-page summary. If a
    shorter page is wanted, §7 (equidistant terms) and §15 (symmetrical forms)
    compress most easily without dropping any examinable result.
