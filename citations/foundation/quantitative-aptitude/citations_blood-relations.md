## The vocabulary of relationships

- **Result relied on:** paternal relatives are reached through the father,
  maternal relatives through the mother. An uncle is a parent's brother or the
  husband of a parent's sister; an aunt is a parent's sister or the wife of a
  parent's brother. A nephew/niece is a sibling's son/daughter. A cousin is the
  child of an uncle or aunt and is gender-free. In-laws (father-, mother-, son-,
  daughter-, brother- and sister-in-law) are joined by marriage; brother-in-law
  and sister-in-law each name two distinct people.
- **Source:** standard kinship conventions used across Indian competitive
  aptitude papers; SM Blood Relations — reference_only.

## The family-tree symbols

- **Convention relied on:** □ marks a male, ○ a female, = a marriage drawn
  horizontally between two people of the same generation, and | a
  parent-to-child descent link drawn vertically. People of the same generation
  are placed on the same horizontal level.
- **Source:** the standard family-tree notation used across aptitude texts; SM

## Reading pointing and photograph puzzles

- **Method relied on:** a "pointing to a photograph / person, X said …" puzzle
  states a chain of relatives ending, usually, at the speaker; the chain is read
  from the innermost relative outwards, and the relationship asked for is that of
  the FIRST noun of the sentence to the speaker. The speaker is placed on the
  tree like any other relative.
- **Source:** standard aptitude-reasoning technique; SM Blood Relations —
  reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f3c12-015 to q-f3c12-024; notes §3 and worked examples 1 and 6.

## Coded relations shown by symbols

- **Convention relied on:** in a coded-relation question the symbols +, −, × and
  ÷ carry NO arithmetic meaning and NO order of operations; each is a shorthand
  for a relationship word fixed by that question's own key, read strictly left to
  right with the left letter first. A symbol meaning "husband/wife of" or
  "son/daughter of" or "father/mother of" also fixes a gender.
- **Source:** standard coded-relationship format used across Indian competitive
  papers; SM Blood Relations — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f3c12-025 to q-f3c12-034; notes §4 and worked examples 3 and 9.
- **Spot-checked by:** _(blank until a human checks)_ — each of these questions
  states its OWN key, so the symbols mean different relationships in different
  questions by design; confirm the SM presents coded relations in the same
  per-question-key form rather than a single fixed convention.

## Counting generations, members and sub-groups

- **Result relied on:** a generation is one level of the family tree, so the
  generation count is the number of levels, not the number of people; the number
  of members is the number of distinct nodes; female/male counts are the ○/□
  nodes; married couples are the "=" links; grandchildren are the lowest level.
  Siblings are SHARED: "each of the n children has one sister/brother" describes
  one common sibling, not one per child.
- **Source:** standard counting conventions for family trees; SM Blood Relations
  — reference_only.

## The gender-assumption trap and "cannot be determined"

- **Result relied on:** letters (A, B, C) carry no gender; gender-free
  relationship words (cousin, child, spouse, sibling, parent, in-law) do not fix
  a gender either. When the exact single-word answer would change with a gender
  the puzzle never states, the correct response is "cannot be determined", which
  is offered as a genuine option.
- **Source:** standard aptitude-reasoning convention; SM Blood Relations —

# Reviewer's checklist — conventions chosen where practice varies

These are editorial calls, not sourced positions. Each one changes what a student
is taught, and each needs a human to confirm it against the SM.

1. **The shared-sibling reading.** The chapter reads "each of the four sons has
   one sister" as ONE sister shared by the four brothers (four sons + one
   daughter = five children), and likewise "each of the five daughters has one
   brother" as ONE shared brother. This is the standard reading, and the keys of
   q-f3c12-038 and q-f3c12-041 rest on it. If the SM ever intends "one sister
   each", both keys and the §5 "mistake" callout would change; confirm the
   standard reading is expected.

2. **Family-tree symbol set.** The notes use □ male, ○ female, = marriage, |
   descent. Some texts use △/▽, ♂/♀, or capital/lower-case letters for gender.
   Any set works, but the notes and bank must use one consistently, which they
   do. Confirm the SM's own symbols if it prints a fixed set, so students match
   forms literally.

3. **"Cannot be determined" as an answer.** q-f3c12-043, q-f3c12-046,
   q-f3c12-047 and q-f3c12-050 make "cannot be determined" (or "either of these")
   the correct option, on the ground that an unstated gender or a two-sided
   in-law word leaves the exact single word open. If the SM instead teaches a
   default (e.g. always assume male, or always give both readings as the answer),
   the option wording — though not the underlying reasoning — would change.

4. **Reading direction of a chain.** §3 reads pointing puzzles from the innermost
   relative outwards and answers for the first noun of the sentence. This is the
   standard technique; confirm the SM does not present a different house method
   that would reword the explanations (the keys are unaffected).

5. **Coded relations use a per-question key.** Every coded question states its
   own meaning for +, −, × and ÷, so the same symbol means different things in
   different questions by design (see q-f3c12-025 through q-f3c12-034). Confirm
   the SM presents coding this way rather than fixing one global key; if it fixes
   one key, the questions still stand but the framing note in §4 should say so.

6. **Section count and length.** The notes run to 7 numbered sections and about
   440 lines, with 9 worked examples. §4's coded-relation treatment and §6's
   gender-trap treatment are the two places most likely to exceed or fall short
   of the SM's depth; both can be trimmed or extended without touching a bank
   key, except that §5's shared-sibling rule underpins q-f3c12-038 and
   q-f3c12-041.
