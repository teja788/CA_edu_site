## Ratio — definition and the rules for handling it

- **Result relied on (conventional statement):** a ratio a : b compares two
  quantities of the same kind, expressed in the same unit, by division; it is
  the quotient a/b, is a pure number with no unit, and requires b ≠ 0.
  Multiplying or dividing both terms by the same non-zero number leaves a ratio
  unchanged; adding or subtracting a constant does not. Two ratios a : b and
  c : d are equal when ad = bc. A ratio is in lowest terms when the HCF of its
  terms is 1. Adding the same positive number to both terms moves a ratio
  towards 1 : 1.
- **Used in:** q-f3c1-001 to q-f3c1-004, q-f3c1-014, cs-f3c1-01-d; notes §§1–2
  and §5 (the "ratio itself changes" set-up).

## Derived ratios — inverse, compound, duplicate, triplicate and the sub- forms

- **Result relied on (conventional statement):** from a : b, the inverse
  (reciprocal) ratio is b : a; the compound of a : b and c : d is ac : bd; the
  duplicate is a² : b²; the sub-duplicate is √a : √b; the triplicate is
  a³ : b³; the sub-triplicate is ∛a : ∛b. The duplicate ratio is the ratio
  compounded of a ratio with itself.
- **Used in:** q-f3c1-005 to q-f3c1-008; notes §3 (the table and the
  "duplicate is not double" callout) and worked example 3.

## Continued ratio and division of a quantity

- **Result relied on (conventional statement):** where A : B = a : b and
  B : C = c : d, the continued ratio is A : B : C = ac : bc : bd, obtained by
  scaling each ratio so that the shared term agrees. A quantity T divided in the
  ratio a : b : c gives shares T × a/(a+b+c), and so on for each term.
- **Used in:** q-f3c1-009 to q-f3c1-013, cs-f3c1-01-a to cs-f3c1-01-c; notes §4
  and §5, worked examples 1, 2 and 10.

## Proportion — fourth, third and mean proportional; continued proportion

- **Result relied on (conventional statement):** a : b :: c : d means a/b = c/d
  and therefore ad = bc, the product of the extremes equalling the product of
  the means. The fourth proportional to a, b, c is d = bc/a; the third
  proportional to a, b is c = b²/a; the mean proportional between a and c is
  b = √(ac), taken as the positive root. Three quantities are in continued
  proportion when b² = ac, and quantities in continued proportion form a
  geometric progression, so three of them may be written a/r, a, ar.
- **Used in:** q-f3c1-015 to q-f3c1-018; notes §6 (the table of the three
  proportionals and the k-method working note).
- **Spot-checked by:** _(blank until a human checks)_

## The operations on a proportion

- **Result relied on (conventional statement):** from a/b = c/d — invertendo
  gives b/a = d/c; alternendo gives a/c = b/d; componendo gives
  (a + b)/b = (c + d)/d; dividendo gives (a − b)/b = (c − d)/d; componendo et
  dividendo gives (a + b)/(a − b) = (c + d)/(c − d). Addendo: if
  a/b = c/d = e/f = k then (a + c + e)/(b + d + f) = k; subtrahendo is the same
  with differences.
- **Used in:** q-f3c1-019, q-f3c1-020; notes §7 and worked example 4.
- **Spot-checked by:** _(blank until a human checks)_

## Variation

- **Result relied on (conventional statement):** y ∝ x means y = kx, so
  y₁/x₁ = y₂/x₂; y ∝ 1/x means xy = k, so x₁y₁ = x₂y₂; joint variation means
  y = kxz, and mixed statements combine the two. A quantity that is partly
  constant and partly varying is written y = a + bx, with a and b found from two
  observations.
- **Used in:** q-f3c1-021 to q-f3c1-023; notes §8 and worked example 5. The
  partly-constant form is included because cost questions of that shape are
  routinely set alongside variation; see the convention note below if the SM
  places it elsewhere.

## Indices and surds

- **Result relied on (conventional statement):** for a positive real base,
  aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ; aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ; (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ; (ab)ⁿ = aⁿbⁿ; (a/b)ⁿ = aⁿ/bⁿ.
  a⁰ = 1 for a ≠ 0, with 0⁰ undefined; a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ; a^(m/n) = ⁿ√(aᵐ). An index
  does not distribute over a sum. A surd ⁿ√a is a^(1/n); surds of different
  orders are compared by raising both to the LCM of the orders; a denominator is
  rationalised using (x + y)(x − y) = x² − y². If aˣ = aʸ with a positive and
  a ≠ 1 then x = y; aˣ = 1 forces x = 0.
- **Used in:** q-f3c1-024 to q-f3c1-037, cs-f3c1-02-a to cs-f3c1-02-c; notes
  §§9–12 and worked examples 6 and 7.

## Logarithms — definition and laws

- **Result relied on (conventional statement):** logₐ N = x means aˣ = N, with
  a positive, a ≠ 1 and N positive; the logarithm of zero or of a negative
  number does not exist in the real system. logₐ 1 = 0, logₐ a = 1 and
  a^(logₐ N) = N. The laws are log (mn) = log m + log n,
  log (m/n) = log m − log n and log (mᵖ) = p log m, all terms being to the same
  base; log (m + n) has no expansion. Change of base:
  logₐ N = (logₚ N) ÷ (logₚ a), from which logₐ p × logₚ a = 1.
- **Used in:** q-f3c1-038 to q-f3c1-046, q-f3c1-050, cs-f3c1-03-a and
  cs-f3c1-03-b; notes §§13–15, §17 and worked examples 8, 9 and 11.
- **Spot-checked by:** _(blank until a human checks)_

## Characteristic, mantissa and antilogarithms

- **Result relied on (conventional statement):** for a common logarithm, the
  characteristic is the integral part and the mantissa the decimal part, the
  mantissa being kept **positive**. For a number of n digits before the decimal
  point the characteristic is n − 1; for a number below 1 with m zeros between
  the decimal point and the first significant digit it is −(m + 1), written with
  a bar over the digit. The mantissa depends only on the sequence of significant
  digits. An antilogarithm reverses the process: the mantissa gives the digits
  and the characteristic places the decimal point.
- **Used in:** q-f3c1-047 to q-f3c1-049, cs-f3c1-03-c; notes §16 including the
  four-figure-table walk-throughs.

## Conventions chosen where practice varies — the reviewer's checklist

These are the six places where more than one defensible presentation exists.
Each is a deliberate choice, and each would move content or keys if the SM
does it differently. **Confirm each one before the draft badge comes off.**

1. **`log` with no base means base 10.** The chapter states this in §15 and
   applies it throughout: `log` is the common logarithm, `ln` (or logₑ) is the
   natural logarithm, and the two are related by ln N = 2.3026 log N. Some
   texts write `log` for the natural logarithm. If the SM writes `log` for base
   e anywhere in Ch 1, §15 and q-f3c1-046 need re-reading, though no answer key
   in this bank changes because every stem that matters names its base.
2. **Sign convention on the characteristic of a number below 1.** The chapter
   uses the bar notation, 3̄.6304, and insists that it means −3 + 0.6304 =
   −0.3696 with the **mantissa kept positive**. q-f3c1-047 and q-f3c1-048 both
   rest on this. If the SM instead teaches the single-negative-decimal form
   throughout, the notes' bar-notation table and the §16 mistake callout should
   be re-worded — the keys stand either way, because the characteristic asked
   for is −3 under both conventions.
3. **How mantissa tables are treated.** §16 describes a **four-figure** table
   read with a mean-difference column, and says plainly that the antilog of
   0.8572 comes out as 719.7 against a true value of 720 — the gap being
   rounding, not error. The bank never requires a table lookup: every logarithm
   question supplies the values it needs in the stem. Confirm that the SM
   examines at four-figure precision and not five, and that supplying the values
   in the stem matches the way the paper is actually set.
4. **The mean proportional is taken as the positive root.** q-f3c1-016 and
   q-f3c1-018 both report only the positive value, and q-f3c1-018 says so in the
   stem ("x is positive"). If the SM admits the negative root as a second
   answer, q-f3c1-016 would need the same qualifier added to its stem.
5. **Extraneous roots are rejected, not listed.** q-f3c1-050 and worked example
   11 hold that a root making any logarithm's argument zero or negative is no
   solution at all, so log₂ x + log₂ (x − 2) = 3 has the single answer x = 4.
   This is the standard position and the option "x = 4 or x = −2" is the trap.
   Confirm the SM states the domain check explicitly; if it does not, the
   question is still correct but the notes carry more weight than the SM does.
6. **The partly-constant, partly-varying cost form (y = a + bx) is taught in
   §8**, alongside variation, and q-f3c1-023 tests it. Some presentations place
   it under simultaneous equations (Ch 2) instead. Nothing in the key depends on
   where it sits, but if the SM keeps it out of Ch 1 entirely, §8's last block
   and q-f3c1-023 can be moved without disturbing anything else.

## Note on the questions themselves

Every distractor in the bank is the value that a **named** error produces —
the inverted ratio, the unreconciled continued ratio, a difference read as a
share, "duplicate" read as "double", a negative index read as a negative
number, a fractional index read upside down, the log of a sum expanded as a sum
of logs, a quotient of logs written for the log of a quotient, the
characteristic counted one out, and an extraneous root retained. Each option's
explanation names that error. This is deliberate: Paper 3 deducts 0.25 for a
wrong answer, and the practical value of the chapter is that a student learns to
recognise the wrong answer they are about to reach.

All 53 numerical questions are recomputed from their stems by
`scripts/verify_numerical/verify_ratio-proportion-indices-logarithms.py`, which
uses `fractions.Fraction` for exact ratio and proportion arithmetic and
`math.log`/`math.log10` for logarithms. Where a question is set with four-figure
values (log 2 = 0.3010, log 3 = 0.4771, ln 10 = 2.3026), the verifier computes
from those same stated values and then rounds explicitly to the precision the
options are quoted to; the comment on each such function says so.
