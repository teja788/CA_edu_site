## Article 19(1)(g) with Article 19(6) — freedom of trade and reasonable restrictions

- **Fragment quoted in the notes:** "reasonable restrictions … in the interests
  of the general public".
- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** all citizens have the right to practise
  any profession or to carry on any occupation, trade or business; the State may
  impose reasonable restrictions on that right in the interests of the general
  public, which is why licensing and regulatory regimes are constitutionally
  permissible.

## Article 111 — assent to Bills; and the assent/commencement distinction

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** when a Bill has been passed by both
  Houses it is presented to the President, who declares that he assents,
  withholds assent, or (in the case of a Bill other than a Money Bill) returns
  it for reconsideration; if the Houses pass it again, with or without
  amendment, the President shall not withhold assent.
- **Separate position (not from Art. 111):** an Act comes into force on the date
  it states or on a date the Government notifies, and different sections may be
  brought into force on different dates. This is a feature of the commencement
  clause of individual Acts, read with the General Clauses Act 1897 — **not** a
  constitutional provision. The Companies Act 2013 is cited as the example.
- **Used in:** q-f2c1-010 (all four options), q-f2c1-012, d-f2c1-02 (points 3–4),
  notes §4, one-page revision summary.

## Article 123 — Ordinances

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** where both Houses are not in session and
  the President is satisfied that circumstances exist rendering it necessary to
  take immediate action, he may promulgate an Ordinance, which has the same
  force and effect as an Act; it must be laid before both Houses and ceases to
  operate at the expiration of six weeks from the reassembly of Parliament,
  unless disapproved earlier.
- **Source:** Constitution of India, Art. 123 (Art. 213 for a Governor) —
  transcribed 7 Aug 2026, not re-fetched.
- **Used in:** q-f2c1-011 (all four options), d-f2c1-02 (point 5), notes §4,

## Article 141 — law declared by the Supreme Court

- **Bare text relied on:** "The law declared by the Supreme Court shall be
  binding on all courts within the territory of India."
- **Positions built on it (paraphrase):** what binds is the **ratio decidendi**,
  not obiter dicta; the Supreme Court is not bound by its own earlier decisions
  and may reconsider them, ordinarily through a larger Bench; Art. 141 binds

## Articles 246, 248 and 254 with the Seventh Schedule — legislative competence

- **Positions relied on (paraphrase):** Art. 246 with the Seventh Schedule
  divides subjects into List I (Union), List II (State) and List III
  (Concurrent); Art. 248 vests residuary legislative power, including the power
  to make a law imposing a tax not mentioned in either the State or the
  Concurrent List, in **Parliament**; Art. 254(1) provides that where a State
  law on a Concurrent subject is repugnant to a Union law, the Union law

  the exception in Art. 254(2) for a State law reserved for and receiving the

## Articles 32, 124, 131–136, 143 — the Supreme Court

- **Positions relied on (paraphrase):** Art. 124 establishes the Supreme Court,
  consisting of the Chief Justice of India and such number of other judges as
  Parliament may prescribe; Art. 131 gives original jurisdiction over disputes
  between the Government of India and States, and between States; Art. 32
  provides the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental
  Rights; Arts. 132–134 provide appeals from High Courts in constitutional,
  civil and criminal matters; Art. 136 permits the Court, in its discretion, to
  grant special leave to appeal from any judgment, decree, determination,
  sentence or order of any court or tribunal in the territory of India, save
  those of a court or tribunal constituted under a law relating to the Armed
  Forces; Art. 143 provides the advisory jurisdiction on a reference by the
  President.
- **Institutional fact stated in the notes:** the **sanctioned strength of the
  Supreme Court is currently 34** judges. **CHECK CLOSELY** — this figure is
  fixed by the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act and has been raised more
  than once; it is stated with the word "currently" and **no question turns on
  it**, deliberately.
- **Simplification flagged:** the notes render the Art. 136(2) exclusion as

## Articles 214, 226 and 227 — the High Courts

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** there shall be a High Court for each

  confined; and it has superintendence over all courts and tribunals throughout

## Doctrine of precedent — binding force below the Supreme Court

- **Positions relied on (paraphrase of settled doctrine, no single statutory
  source):** a High Court's decision binds all courts and tribunals within its
  own territorial jurisdiction and is persuasive elsewhere; a single judge is
  bound by a Division Bench or Full Bench of the same High Court; decisions of
  foreign courts are persuasive only; a precedent may be **distinguished**
  (material facts differ), **overruled** (a higher court or a larger Bench holds
  it wrong in law) or **reversed** (the same case decided the other way on
  appeal).
- **Used in:** q-f2c1-020, q-f2c1-021, q-f2c1-022 (overruling vs reversal —
  all four options), d-f2c1-04 (points 1, 2, 4, 5), notes §7,

## Delegated legislation — ultra vires, and the status of a circular

- **Positions relied on (paraphrase of settled administrative-law doctrine, no
  single statutory source):** subordinate legislation derives its authority from
  the parent Act and is void so far as it exceeds the power conferred; where a
  rule conflicts with the section under which it was made, the section prevails;
  an administrative circular or clarification binds departmental officers and
  guides the public but cannot create a liability, take away a right conferred
  by the statute, or contradict it; and parent Acts commonly require rules to be
  laid before Parliament, which may modify or annul them.
- **Statutory example named (not quoted):** the Companies (Incorporation) Rules
  2014, made under the rule-making power in s. 469 of the Companies Act 2013.

## Jurisdiction — subject-matter, territorial and pecuniary

- **Positions relied on (paraphrase of the Code of Civil Procedure 1908 and
  settled doctrine):** a civil suit is ordinarily instituted where the defendant
  resides or carries on business, or where the cause of action wholly or in part
  arose; civil courts are tiered by the value of the suit; a decree passed
  without subject-matter jurisdiction is a nullity that may be questioned even
  in execution, and consent of the parties cannot confer a jurisdiction the
  court does not have.
- **CHECK CLOSELY — the pecuniary figures are hypothetical.** Pecuniary limits
  of the district judiciary are fixed by the **State** concerned and differ from
  State to State. Every stem that routes a claim by value (q-f2c1-030 and
  cs-f2c1-01) therefore **states its own limits inside the stem**, and the
  verifier module takes those limits as parameters rather than hard-coding any
  State's figures. No real State's limits are asserted anywhere in this chapter.
- **Also relied on (paraphrase):** where a statute confers exclusive
  jurisdiction on a tribunal, the jurisdiction of the civil court over that
  subject-matter is barred.
- **Used in:** q-f2c1-029, q-f2c1-030, q-f2c1-031, cs-f2c1-01-a, cs-f2c1-01-b,

## Civil and criminal proceedings — the new criminal codes

- **Positions relied on (paraphrase):** civil proceedings are governed
  procedurally by the Code of Civil Procedure 1908 and are begun by a plaint;
  the plaintiff bears the burden and the standard is the preponderance of
  probabilities; the outcome is a decree. Criminal proceedings are governed by
  the **Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita 2023** and the offences by the
  **Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023** (with the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam 2023
  replacing the Indian Evidence Act 1872); the prosecution bears the burden, the
  standard is proof beyond reasonable doubt, the accused is presumed innocent,
  and the outcome is conviction or acquittal. Only compoundable offences may be
  compromised.
- **Amendment fact stated in the notes and in the amendment callout:** the three
  new codes came into force on **1 July 2024**, replacing the IPC 1860, the CrPC
  1973 and the Evidence Act 1872. **CHECK CLOSELY** — the commencement date was
  transcribed from memory, and the reviewer should also confirm whether the ICAI
  SM for the May 2026 edition has switched to the new nomenclature; if it has
  not, notes §11 and d-f2c1-05 will read differently from the SM.

## Alternative dispute resolution — the four methods and Lok Adalat

- **Positions relied on (paraphrase, not quoted):** under the **Arbitration and
  Conciliation Act 1996** an arbitral tribunal decides the dispute and its award
  is binding and enforceable in the same manner as a decree of a court, and may
  be set aside only on the limited grounds in **s. 34**; under Part III of the
  same Act a **conciliator** assists the parties and may formulate and
  reformulate terms of a possible settlement, and a settlement agreement so
  arrived at has the **same status and effect as an arbitral award**; under the
  **Mediation Act 2023** a mediator facilitates without deciding or proposing
  terms, and a **mediated settlement agreement** is final and binding and
  enforceable as if it were a judgment or decree of a court; under the **Legal
  Services Authorities Act 1987** an award of a Lok Adalat is deemed to be a
  decree of a civil court and **no appeal lies** against it. **Section 89 of the
  Code of Civil Procedure 1908** permits a court to refer a pending dispute to
  one of these methods.
- **Source:** the four Acts named — transcribed 7 Aug 2026, not re-fetched.
  **CHECK CLOSELY:** the distinction drawn in q-f2c1-035 between a conciliator
  (who *may propose* terms) and a mediator (who does not) is the pivot of that
  question and of the ADR pointer callout; please confirm it against the current
  text of the 1996 Act and the Mediation Act 2023 before initialling.
- **Used in:** q-f2c1-034, q-f2c1-035, q-f2c1-036, cs-f2c1-01-a (option D),

## Reading a bare Act — proviso, explanation, illustration, definitions

- **Positions relied on (paraphrase of settled canons of construction, no single
  statutory source):** a **proviso** carves an exception out of the provision to
  which it is attached and is construed strictly; an **explanation** clarifies
  or extends the meaning of the main provision and cannot be read against it; an
  **illustration** is an aid to understanding and cannot control or cut down the
  language of the section; a definition using **"means"** is exhaustive, one
  using **"includes"** is extensive, and "means and includes" is exhaustive but
  enlarged; a **non-obstante clause** overrides what it names, while "subject
  to" subordinates; and "shall" is ordinarily mandatory while "may" is enabling.
- **Illustrative provisions used are FICTIONAL.** The "section 12", "section 14
  with its proviso, explanation and illustration", and the "sections 30 and 41"
  used in the notes, the vignettes and the second case set are **invented for
  teaching** and are labelled as such in the text. They are not drawn from any
  real enactment, and the reviewer should confirm that each remains clearly
  labelled fictional so that no student cites them.
- **Used in:** q-f2c1-037, q-f2c1-038, q-f2c1-039, q-f2c1-040, cs-f2c1-02-a,
  cs-f2c1-02-b, cs-f2c1-02-c, d-f2c1-06 (all points), notes §6 (vignette 1),

## Custom, usage and personal law

- **Positions relied on (paraphrase of settled doctrine):** a custom is
  recognised as law where it is ancient, continuous and uniform, certain,
  reasonable, and not opposed to any statute or to public policy; where a
  statute occupies the field the statute prevails; usage of trade is read into a
  commercial contract where the parties are silent; personal laws govern
  marriage, divorce, adoption, guardianship, succession and maintenance and
  differ by community.
- **Scope boundary:** no substantive personal law is stated or examined; the
  topic appears only as an item in the list of sources.
