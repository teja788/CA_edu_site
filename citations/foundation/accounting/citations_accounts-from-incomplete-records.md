## Single entry is not a system — the double entry / single entry / no entry mixture

- **Position relied on (paraphrase, not quoted):** what is loosely called the
  "single entry system" is not a system at all but a mechanically defective,
  unscientific way of keeping books, in which some transactions are recorded on
  both sides, some on one side only and some not at all. In practice double
  entry survives for **personal accounts** (debtors and creditors), a one-sided
  running record is kept for **cash**, and most **real and nominal** accounts
  are not maintained. The records vary from firm to firm, with no uniform
  pattern.
- **Source:** ICAI Foundation SM Paper 1, Ch 9 (Accounts from Incomplete
  Records), May 2026 edition — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f1c9-001, d-f1c9-01 skeleton points 1–2; notes §1 (the
  three-bullet breakdown and the §1 "pointer" callout on whether single entry

## Defects of incomplete records

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** because the books are incomplete no
  trial balance can be extracted, so there is no arithmetical check on their
  accuracy; gross profit cannot be ascertained, there being no Trading Account,
  so the trader cannot compare his margin year on year or price on an informed
  basis; the financial position shown rests on estimates and physical counts
  rather than ledger balances and so is not reliable; frauds and errors go
  undetected; and the accounts are not acceptable to tax authorities, bankers
  or an incoming partner without reconstruction.
- **Source:** SM Ch 9, May 2026 ed. — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f1c9-002, d-f1c9-01 skeleton points 3–5; notes §1 (the
  defects paragraph).

## Who may keep incomplete records; the position of companies

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** the practice survives among small sole
  proprietors and small partnership firms whose volume of transactions does not
  justify a full set of books, and is chosen for cheapness, simplicity and
  privacy. It is not open to a company, which is required to maintain proper
  books of account on the double entry basis whatever its size or turnover, nor
  to any entity whose accounts are subject to statutory audit.
- **Source:** SM Ch 9, May 2026 ed. — reference_only (the underlying

## Statement of Affairs vs Balance Sheet, and capital as the balancing figure

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** a Statement of Affairs is built from
  estimates, physical counts, vouchers and the proprietor's memory, and capital
  in it is the **balancing figure**, `Capital = Assets − Liabilities`. A Balance
  Sheet is built from ledger balances already proved by a trial balance, and
  its capital comes from an independently maintained Capital Account. The
  consequence the SM draws out is one of reliability: an asset or liability
  omitted from a Statement of Affairs silently alters capital and nothing
  complains, whereas the same omission would throw a trial balance out. The two
  documents also differ in purpose — one is prepared to *find* capital, the
  other to *present* a position already known.
- **Source:** SM Ch 9, May 2026 ed. — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f1c9-004, q-f1c9-005 (capital as assets less liabilities),
  q-f1c9-041 (an omitted opening liability inflating opening capital and so
  understating the profit), d-f1c9-02 skeleton (all four points); notes §2
  (comparison table, the `Capital = Assets − Liabilities` formula line and the
  §2 "mistake" callout on the heading), worked examples 1 (WN 1–2) and 5 (WN 1).

## The net worth method — formula and its rearrangements

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** under the net worth (statement of
  affairs) method, profit is the movement in capital corrected for the
  proprietor's own dealings:
  `Profit = Closing capital + Drawings − Further capital introduced − Opening capital`.
  Drawings are added back because the closing capital is already net of them,
  the amounts withdrawn having been earned during the year; further capital is
  deducted because it swelled the closing capital without any trading effort
  behind it. A negative result is a loss. The same relation is rearranged to
  yield closing capital, drawings or further capital where one of those is the
  figure the question hides.
- **Source:** SM Ch 9, May 2026 ed. — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f1c9-006, q-f1c9-007, q-f1c9-008, q-f1c9-009 (the loss case),
  q-f1c9-010 (why fresh capital is deducted), q-f1c9-012 (closing capital),
  q-f1c9-013 (drawings), q-f1c9-014 (further capital), d-f1c9-03 skeleton
  points 1–3; notes §3 (the four `formula` lines), worked example 1 (WN 3).

## What counts as drawings; goods withdrawn are taken at cost

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** drawings are not confined to cash. Goods
  taken for personal use, the proprietor's life insurance premium or household
  bills paid out of business funds, and income tax on his personal income are
  all drawings, income tax of a sole proprietor being a personal and not a
  business expense. **Goods withdrawn are recorded at cost** — they are deducted
  from purchases and added to drawings — because no sale has taken place and no
  profit may be recognised on a withdrawal by the owner.
- **Source:** SM Ch 9, May 2026 ed. — reference_only (consistent with the
  treatment carried forward from SM Ch 7 on final accounts).
- **Used in:** q-f1c9-044; notes §3 (the §3 "pointer" callout) and the "Common
  mistakes" entry on income tax and insurance premium.

## Adjustments run through closing capital, and the method's limitation

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** year-end adjustments in a net-worth
  question are applied to the **closing capital** before the formula is used —
  depreciation not yet provided and bad debts or a provision for doubtful debts
  reduce it, outstanding expenses add a liability, prepaid expenses and accrued
  income add an asset, and any asset or liability omitted from the first draft
  is brought in. Interest on capital and interest on drawings, where the
  question calls for them, are dealt with after the profit has been arrived at.
  The method's inherent limitation is that it yields the net profit as a single
  figure: neither gross profit nor the individual expenses can be seen, so no
  Trading and Profit and Loss Account can be produced from it.
- **Source:** SM Ch 9, May 2026 ed. — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f1c9-011 (four adjustments, including an omitted asset added
  back), q-f1c9-015 (the limitation), d-f1c9-03 skeleton points 4–5; notes §4.

## The conversion method — meaning, order of work, and the closing check

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** conversion is the reconstruction of the
  missing double entry from surviving evidence — the cash book, the debtors and
  creditors records, the bill file, invoices and counterfoils — so that a proper
  Trading and Profit and Loss Account and Balance Sheet can be prepared. The
  working order is: opening Statement of Affairs, then the cash and bank
  summary, then the Total Debtors and Total Creditors Accounts, then the bills
  accounts (and a memorandum trading account where a gross profit ratio must be
  used), and finally the final accounts. Closing capital built up as
  `opening capital + net profit + fresh capital − drawings` must equal
  `assets − liabilities` in the Balance Sheet; because no trial balance exists,
  that agreement is the only arithmetical proof that the reconstruction is
  complete. Properly performed, the conversion method and the net worth method
  give the same net profit on the same facts; the difference between them is
  that only conversion shows how the profit arose.
- **Source:** SM Ch 9, May 2026 ed. — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f1c9-042 (net worth vs conversion), q-f1c9-045 (the closing
  check), d-f1c9-04 skeleton (all six points); notes §5 and worked example 5
  (WN 6, where the two ₹4,13,000 figures are made to agree).

## Reconstructing the cash book; splitting cash sales from credit sales

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** the cash book is a ledger account for
  cash, so `opening balance + receipts − payments = closing balance`, and any
  single unknown in that line — most often the cash sales or the proprietor's
  drawings — is taken as the balancing figure. Capital payments (furniture,
  machinery) and receipts that are not income (a loan taken) still pass through
  the cash book, and a contra between cash and bank must not be counted as both
  a receipt and a payment in a combined summary. Only credit sales pass through
  the Total Debtors Account and only cash sales through the cash book, so
  `total sales = cash sales + credit sales`; the same split applies to
  purchases, cash purchases being obtained from the cash book alone.
- **Source:** SM Ch 9, May 2026 ed. — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f1c9-029 (missing cash sales), q-f1c9-030 (drawings as the
  balancing figure), q-f1c9-031 and q-f1c9-032 (the cash/credit split and why
  cash sales are a balancing figure), q-f1c9-040 (cash purchases),
  d-f1c9-04 skeleton point 2; notes §6, worked example 5 (WN 2–3).

## Total Debtors Account — structure and the items on each side

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** the Total Debtors Account is debited
  with the opening balance, credit sales, bills receivable dishonoured, cheques
  received earlier and now dishonoured, and any interest charged on overdue
  accounts; it is credited with cash and cheques received, discount allowed,
  sales returns, bad debts written off, bills receivable received from
  customers, any transfer (set-off) to the creditors ledger, and the closing
  balance. Credit sales are ordinarily the balancing figure. Cash sales never
  enter the account, since they create no debtor.
- **Source:** SM Ch 9, May 2026 ed. — reference_only.

## Total Creditors Account — structure and the items on each side

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** the Total Creditors Account is credited
  with the opening balance, credit purchases and bills payable dishonoured, and
  debited with payments, discount received, purchase returns, bills payable
  accepted in favour of suppliers, any transfer (set-off) from the debtors
  ledger, and the closing balance. Credit purchases are ordinarily the
  balancing figure, and cash purchases never enter the account. An acceptance
  given to a supplier is a **debit** here because it settles the book debt and
  moves the same liability into bills payable.
- **Source:** SM Ch 9, May 2026 ed. — reference_only.

## Set-off between the debtors and creditors ledgers

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** where the same person is both a customer
  and a supplier, the smaller balance is transferred against the larger. The
  transfer reduces both balances, and so appears on the **credit** of the Total
  Debtors Account and the **debit** of the Total Creditors Account — one
  transaction, two accounts, opposite sides.
- **Source:** SM Ch 9, May 2026 ed. — reference_only.

## Bills Receivable and Bills Payable Accounts; where a dishonour is routed

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** the Bills Receivable Account is debited
  with the opening balance and the bills received from customers during the
  year, and credited with collections at maturity, bills discounted, bills
  endorsed to creditors, bills dishonoured and the closing balance. The Bills
  Payable Account is credited with the opening balance and the bills accepted
  during the year, and debited with payments at maturity, bills dishonoured and
  the closing balance. A dishonour always leaves the bills account and returns
  to the personal account: a dishonoured bill receivable to the **debit** of the
  Total Debtors Account (and so it reduces the credit sales that fall out as the
  balancing figure), and a dishonoured bill payable to the **credit** of the
  Total Creditors Account (reducing the computed credit purchases). Discounting
  does not release the customer, so a discounted bill that is dishonoured is
  charged back to him in the same way.
- **Source:** SM Ch 9, May 2026 ed. — reference_only (consistent with the bills
  of exchange treatment in the companion chapter).
- **Used in:** q-f1c9-025 (bills received), q-f1c9-026 (bills accepted),
  q-f1c9-023 and q-f1c9-027 (the dishonour legs), q-f1c9-028 (routing of a
  discounted bill dishonoured), d-f1c9-05 skeleton points 4–5; notes §9 (both
  `formula` lines and the "Where dishonour goes" paragraph), worked example 3

## Gross profit ratio and the memorandum trading account; loss of stock

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** where purchases, sales or closing stock
  is missing and a gross profit ratio is available, a Trading Account is drawn
  up as a working — a **memorandum** trading account — which lies outside the
  double entry and is never posted or balanced into the ledger; the three known
  figures are entered and the fourth emerges as the balancing figure. The
  standard relations are
  `cost of goods sold = opening stock + net purchases + direct expenses − closing stock`
  and `gross profit = net sales − cost of goods sold`. Its commonest application
  is to establish the stock on the date of a fire or theft, the loss of stock
  being that stock less any salvage, and the insurance claim the lower of the
  loss and the policy amount.
- **Source:** SM Ch 9, May 2026 ed. — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f1c9-033 (closing stock), q-f1c9-034 (sales), q-f1c9-035
  (loss of stock in a fire), q-f1c9-038 (purchases), q-f1c9-039 (nature of the
  memorandum account), d-f1c9-06 skeleton points 1–2; notes §10, worked
  example 4.

## Mark-up on cost vs margin on sales, and the conversion between them

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** a gross profit percentage means nothing
  until its base is known. **Mark-up** expresses gross profit as a percentage of
  **cost**; **margin** expresses the same rupee profit as a percentage of
  **sales**. Since sales exceed cost wherever there is a profit, the mark-up
  percentage is always the larger of the two. The conversions are
  `margin = mark-up ÷ (100 + mark-up)` and `mark-up = margin ÷ (100 − margin)`,
  so that (for example) 25% on cost is 20% on sales and 33⅓% on cost is 25% on
  sales.
- **Source:** SM Ch 9, May 2026 ed. — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f1c9-036 (sales from a mark-up), q-f1c9-037 (why the mark-up
  percentage is the larger), and as the deliberate wrong-base distractor in
  q-f1c9-033, q-f1c9-034 and q-f1c9-038; d-f1c9-06 skeleton points 3–4; notes
  §11 (the conversion `formula` line, the ratio table and the §11 "mistake"
  callout), worked example 4 (WN 3 contrasts the two bases).
