## Meaning of inventories, and the three categories

- **Position relied on (paraphrase, not quoted):** inventories are assets held
  for sale in the ordinary course of business, in the process of production for
  such sale, or in the form of materials or supplies to be consumed in the
  production process or in the rendering of services. The classification of an
  asset therefore depends on what the enterprise does with it, not on the nature
  of the asset itself — an identical computer is a fixed asset to a cloth
  merchant and stock-in-trade to a computer dealer.
- **Source:** AS 2 (Valuation of Inventories), definitions; ICAI Foundation SM
  Paper 1, Ch 4 (Inventories), May 2026 edition — both reference_only.

## Lower of cost and net realisable value, compared item by item

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** inventories are measured at the lower of
  cost and net realisable value. The comparison is ordinarily made **item by
  item**; grouping is permitted only for items that are similar or related to
  each other and cannot practicably be evaluated separately (for example, items
  in the same product line having similar purposes and similar gross margins).
  It is **not** appropriate to write down inventories on the basis of a
  classification such as all finished goods, or on the basis of the whole
  business, because that would set an unrealised gain on one item against a real
  loss on another. Where net realisable value exceeds cost, the item stays at
  cost and no gain is recognised.
- **Source:** AS 2, measurement and net-realisable-value paragraphs; SM Ch 4,
  May 2026 ed. — reference_only.

## Components of cost — purchase, conversion and other costs

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** the cost of inventories comprises the
  cost of purchase, the cost of conversion and other costs incurred in bringing
  the inventories to their **present location and condition**. The cost of
  purchase is the purchase price **less** trade discounts, rebates, duty
  drawbacks and other similar items, **plus** duties and taxes that are not
  subsequently recoverable from the taxing authorities, **plus** freight inward
  and other costs directly attributable to the acquisition. The cost of
  conversion is direct labour plus a systematic allocation of production
  overheads: variable overheads on actual production and **fixed production
  overheads on the normal capacity** of the plant, the amount left unallocated
  in a period of abnormally low production being recognised as an expense of
  that period.
- **Source:** AS 2, cost-of-purchase and cost-of-conversion paragraphs; SM Ch 4,
  May 2026 ed. — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f1c4-014, q-f1c4-015, q-f1c4-016, q-f1c4-018, q-f1c4-019,

## Trade discount deducted; cash discount and recoverable taxes excluded

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** trade discounts, rebates and duty
  drawbacks are deducted in arriving at the cost of purchase. Duties and taxes
  that will subsequently be recovered from the taxing authorities — GST for
  which input tax credit is available — are not part of cost. Cash discount, an
  allowance for prompt settlement, is a financing item credited to income and is
  **not** deducted from the cost of inventories; this is the long-standing
  textbook position carried into the SM's own worked patterns and consistent
  with the trade-vs-cash discount treatment taught in Ch 2.
- **Source:** AS 2, cost-of-purchase paragraph; SM Ch 4, May 2026 ed., read with
  the Ch 2 treatment of discounts — reference_only.

## Exclusions from cost — abnormal waste, storage, administration, selling, interest

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** the following are excluded from the cost
  of inventories and recognised as expenses of the period: abnormal amounts of
  wasted materials, labour or other production costs; **storage costs, unless
  those costs are necessary in the production process prior to a further
  production stage**; administrative overheads that do not contribute to
  bringing the inventories to their present location and condition; and selling
  and distribution costs. Interest and other borrowing costs are likewise
  ordinarily excluded, AS 16 permitting capitalisation only in the case of a
  qualifying asset. Normal, unavoidable process or handling loss is treated
  differently from abnormal loss: it carries no cost of its own and its cost is
  absorbed by the good units that survive, raising their cost per unit.
- **Source:** AS 2, exclusions paragraph, read with AS 16; SM Ch 4, May 2026
  ed., for the normal/abnormal loss treatment — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f1c4-015 (storage, fire loss, commission and interest

## Net realisable value — definition and the treatment of selling costs

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** net realisable value is the estimated
  selling price in the ordinary course of business **less** the estimated costs
  of completion and the estimated costs necessary to make the sale. It is an
  entity-specific exit value, not a general market price and not replacement
  cost. Costs of selling — commission, packing for despatch, freight outward —
  are excluded from the cost of inventories but **are** deducted in arriving at
  net realisable value.
- **Source:** AS 2, definitions and net-realisable-value paragraphs; SM Ch 4,
  May 2026 ed. — reference_only.

## Materials: the write-down rule and replacement cost as a measure of NRV

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** materials and other supplies held for use
  in the production of inventories are **not written down below cost** if the
  finished products in which they will be incorporated are expected to be sold
  at or above cost. Where a decline in the price of materials indicates that the
  cost of the finished products will exceed their net realisable value, the
  materials are written down to net realisable value; in such circumstances the
  **replacement cost of the materials may be the best available measure** of
  their net realisable value.
- **Source:** AS 2, net-realisable-value paragraphs dealing with materials; SM
  Ch 4, May 2026 ed. — reference_only.

## Cost formulas — specific identification, FIFO and weighted average; LIFO not permitted

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** the cost of inventories of items that are
  **not ordinarily interchangeable**, and of goods or services produced and
  segregated for specific projects, is assigned by **specific identification**
  of their individual costs; specific identification is inappropriate where
  there are large numbers of ordinarily interchangeable items, because it would
  allow predetermined effects on profit. The cost of all other inventories is
  assigned by the **first-in first-out** or the **weighted average cost**
  formula, the weighted average being computed either periodically or as each
  additional shipment is received (a moving average), depending on the
  circumstances of the enterprise. **LIFO is not among the permitted formulas**
  under the revised AS 2.
- **Source:** AS 2, cost-formulas paragraphs; SM Ch 4, May 2026 ed. —
  reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f1c4-030, q-f1c4-031, q-f1c4-032, q-f1c4-033, q-f1c4-034,

## Techniques of measurement — standard cost and the retail method

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** techniques such as the **standard cost
  method** and the **retail method** may be used for convenience if the results
  approximate the actual cost. Standard costs take into account normal levels of
  consumption of materials and supplies, labour, efficiency and capacity
  utilisation, and are **regularly reviewed** and, if necessary, revised in the
  light of current conditions. The **retail method** is often used in the retail
  trade for measuring inventories of large numbers of rapidly changing items
  that have similar margins and for which it is impracticable to use other
  costing methods: the cost of the inventory is determined by **reducing its
  sale value by the appropriate percentage gross margin**, an average percentage
  ordinarily being used for each retail department.
- **Source:** AS 2, techniques-of-measurement paragraphs; SM Ch 4, May 2026 ed.
  — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f1c4-037, q-f1c4-038, q-f1c4-039, d-f1c4-04 skeleton points
  4–5; notes §8 (both technique paragraphs and the `formula` line) and worked
  example 4, including working note 3 on a margin quoted on cost.

## Periodic and perpetual inventory systems

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** under the **periodic** (physical) system
  no continuous record of stock is kept; the closing stock is ascertained by a
  physical count at the period end and the cost of goods sold is a residual —
  opening stock plus net purchases plus direct expenses less closing stock — so
  that shrinkage, pilferage and wastage are absorbed silently within it. Under
  the **perpetual** (continuous) system a stores or stock ledger records every
  receipt and issue, the stock figure and the cost of goods sold are available
  at any time, and the physical count becomes a check: any difference between
  the record balance and the count is identified and written off as a shortage,
  the **physically verified** figure being the one carried to the balance sheet.
  This is the SM's presentation of the two systems rather than a position of
  AS 2 itself.
- **Source:** SM Ch 4, May 2026 ed. — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f1c4-040, q-f1c4-041, q-f1c4-042, d-f1c4-05 skeleton (all six
  points); notes §9 (the comparison table, the `formula` line and the paragraph
  on the shortage).

## Year-end cut-off — ownership, not possession

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** the physical count must be adjusted so
  that the closing stock reflects **ownership**, not possession. Goods in
  transit at the year end are included where title has passed to the buyer.
  Goods lying unsold with a consignee remain the **consignor's** stock, valued
  at cost plus a proportionate share of non-recurring expenses; goods received
  on consignment from another party are excluded. Goods sent on **sale or
  return** remain the seller's stock at cost until the buyer signifies approval
  or the time limit for rejection expires, and any sale wrongly recorded must be
  reversed. Goods sold and invoiced but awaiting collection by the customer are
  excluded, and goods held for third parties — customers' goods left for repair,
  goods held as an agent — are never the enterprise's inventory. This is the
  SM's cut-off treatment, consistent with the consignment and sale-or-return
  positions taught elsewhere in the Foundation syllabus.
- **Source:** SM Ch 4, May 2026 ed. — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f1c4-043, q-f1c4-044, q-f1c4-045, d-f1c4-06 skeleton (all six
  points); notes §10 (the ownership table and the sale-or-return pointer

## Effect of a closing-stock error, and its counterbalancing nature

- **Position relied on (paraphrase):** the closing stock figure enters both the
  Trading Account (as a credit, raising gross profit) and the Balance Sheet (as
  a current asset), so a single error misstates profit and net assets by the
  same amount and in the same direction. Because this year's closing stock is
  next year's opening stock — a debit in next year's Trading Account — the error
  reverses itself in the following period and the **cumulative** profit of the
  two years is unaffected; each individual year's profit and the first year's
  balance sheet nevertheless remain misstated.
- **Source:** SM Ch 4, May 2026 ed., read with the final-accounts treatment of
  closing stock — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f1c4-004, q-f1c4-005, q-f1c4-006, q-f1c4-007; notes §2 (both
  bullets, the `formula` line and the §2 "mistake" callout).
