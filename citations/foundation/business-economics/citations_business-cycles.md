## Meaning and definition of a business cycle

- **Result relied on:** a business cycle (trade cycle) is the recurring, wave-like
  fluctuation in the overall (aggregate) level of economic activity — output,
  income, employment and the general price level — over a period of years; it is a
  macroeconomic, economy-wide phenomenon, recurrent but not strictly periodic.
- **Source:** standard macroeconomics; SM Ch 5 introductory treatment —

## The four phases and their order

- **Result relied on:** a business cycle passes through four phases in a fixed
  order — from the trough: recovery (revival/upturn) → expansion (prosperity/boom/
  upswing) up to the peak → recession (contraction/downturn) → depression (slump)
  down to the trough — and then repeats. Expansion is the rising phase; recession
  is the fall from the peak; depression is the lowest phase; recovery is the rise
  from the trough.
- **Source:** standard; SM Ch 5 — reference_only.

## Features of the four phases

- **Result relied on:** in expansion/boom, output, employment, income, prices,
  investment and profits rise, credit expands and confidence is high, with the
  peak marked by near-full capacity and bottlenecks in labour, materials and
  finance; in recession all these fall and pessimism grows, with credit
  contracting; in depression/trough they reach their lowest, with heavy
  unemployment, idle capacity, low prices, losses, tight credit and deep
  pessimism; in recovery/revival investment revives, employment and income rise,
  prices firm up, credit expands and confidence returns. A boom ends because
  resources run out and bottlenecks stall further growth.
- **Source:** standard; SM Ch 5 — reference_only.

## Turning points: peak and trough

- **Result relied on:** the peak (upper turning point) is the top of the cycle,
  where expansion ends and recession begins; the trough (lower turning point) is

  place where the direction of aggregate activity reverses.

## Characteristics of business cycles

- **Result relied on:** business cycles are recurrent but not strictly periodic
  (varying length, depth and force); synchronous and pervasive (spreading across
  the whole economy and moving the sectors together — the synchronism stressed by
  Wesley Mitchell); wave-like and self-reinforcing/cumulative (a movement, once
  started, feeds on itself); international in scope (transmitted between countries
  through trade and capital flows); felt by all sectors but unequally, with
  capital-goods and durable-goods industries fluctuating far more than
  consumer-necessity industries; and largely self-generating, each phase creating
  the conditions for the next.
- **Source:** standard macroeconomics; SM Ch 5 — reference_only. The synchronism
  point is conventionally attributed to Wesley Mitchell's empirical work on
  business cycles.
- **Used in:** q-f4c5-023, q-f4c5-024, q-f4c5-025, q-f4c5-026, q-f4c5-027,
  q-f4c5-028, q-f4c5-029, q-f4c5-030; notes §5 and its pointer callout.

## Why capital-goods industries fluctuate more

- **Result relied on:** capital-goods and durable-goods industries swing more over
  the cycle than consumer-necessity industries for two reasons — their purchase is
  postponable (an old machine or an old durable can be made to last through a
  slump and the purchase deferred), and the accelerator magnifies the swing
  (investment responds to the change in consumer demand, so even a small dip in
  consumption can turn the demand for new capital goods sharply negative).
- **Source:** standard; SM Ch 5 and the standard accelerator principle —
  reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f4c5-027, q-f4c5-049; notes §5 and worked example 6.

## Theories of the business cycle and their economists

- **Attributions relied on (the load-bearing part of this file):**
  - **Monetary (over-expansion of credit) theory — R. G. Hawtrey:** the cycle is a
    purely monetary phenomenon; expansion of bank credit and money drives the
    boom, contraction of credit brings the slump.
  - **Innovations theory — J. A. Schumpeter:** clusters of entrepreneurial
    innovations set off a wave of investment and expansion, whose exhaustion brings
    the downturn.
  - **Over-investment theory — F. A. Hayek:** easy credit and optimism push
    investment in capital goods beyond what consumption can sustain, so the
    over-built capacity collapses and ends the boom.
  - **Under-consumption theory — Hobson (and, historically, Malthus):** excessive
    saving and too little consumption leave demand short of output, triggering the
    downswing.
  - **Psychological theory — A. C. Pigou:** waves of business optimism and
    pessimism over-extend the boom and deepen the slump.
  - **Effective-demand / investment theory — J. M. Keynes:** cycles are driven by
    fluctuations in aggregate (effective) demand, led by volatile investment (the
    marginal efficiency of capital and expectations).
  - **Sunspot (weather) theory — W. S. Jevons:** cycles in sunspots cause cycles in
    weather and harvests, and so in agricultural incomes and trade — an external
    cause.
  - **Multiplier–accelerator interaction — P. A. Samuelson and J. R. Hicks:** the
    multiplier (investment raising income) and the accelerator (rising income
    inducing investment) feed on each other to generate a self-sustaining cycle.
- **Source:** standard history of macroeconomic thought; SM Ch 5 —
  reference_only. These are the conventional textbook attributions.
- **Used in:** q-f4c5-039, q-f4c5-040, q-f4c5-041, q-f4c5-042, q-f4c5-043,
  q-f4c5-044, q-f4c5-045, q-f4c5-046; notes §8 (the theory-vs-proponent table and
  its mistake callout), and worked example 5. The Keynes and Hawtrey/Hayek

## Relevance of business cycles to business decision-making

- **Result relied on:** knowledge of the business cycle informs a firm's decisions
  on demand forecasting and production planning, investment and capacity,
  inventory management, pricing and credit policy, financing and cash management,
  employment and cost control, and diversification/risk management. The
  through-line is timing: firms should act counter to the cycle — cautiously near
  the peak, boldly near the trough — and capital-goods and durable firms, whose
  demand swings most, must plan most carefully.
- **Source:** standard managerial-economics treatment; SM Ch 5 — reference_only.

# Reviewer's checklist — conventions chosen where practice varies

These are editorial calls, not sourced positions. Each one changes what a student
is taught, and each needs a human to confirm it against the SM.

1. **Phase names and synonyms.** The chapter names the four phases expansion
   (also prosperity/boom/upswing), recession (contraction/downturn), depression
   (slump/trough) and recovery (revival/upturn), and treats the **peak** and
   **trough** as the two turning points. Some texts use "prosperity" as the head
   name for the boom phase, or draw a five-part diagram that names the peak and
   trough as phases in their own right. Confirm the SM's naming; the keys turn on
   the standard four-phase scheme and the peak/trough turning points, which every
   edition shares.

2. **The external/internal split of causes.** The chapter sorts causes into
   external (exogenous) — wars, technology/innovation, population, weather/sunspots
   — and internal (endogenous) — investment/effective demand, bank credit,
   over-investment, expectations, multiplier-accelerator, under-consumption. A few
   texts treat Schumpeter's innovations as endogenous (entrepreneurs acting within
   the system) rather than as an external technological shock. The notes place
   innovations on the external side as a technology shock but also list it in the
   theory table; if the SM treats innovations as endogenous, §6 and q-f4c5-034
   should be checked, though q-f4c5-043 (Schumpeter = innovations) is unaffected.

3. **Attribution of the under-consumption theory.** The theory table names Hobson
   and Malthus for under-consumption. No bank question turns on this attribution
   (the under-consumption line is used only as background in §7 and the §8 table),
   so if the SM attributes it differently, the table entry can be aligned without
   touching any key.

4. **The Hawtrey / Hayek distinction.** The single most important convention: the
   **monetary theory is Hawtrey's** and the **over-investment theory is Hayek's**.
   The two names are lookalikes and the chapter builds a memory hook and a trap
   question (q-f4c5-046) on the distinction. This must not be reversed; q-f4c5-045
   and q-f4c5-046 depend on it directly.

5. **Depth of the multiplier-accelerator treatment.** The notes explain the
   multiplier and the accelerator in words and attribute their interaction to
   Samuelson and Hicks, but do **not** derive the interaction algebraically or
   present the four cases of the accelerator-multiplier model. Confirm this matches
   the Foundation depth; if the SM requires the algebra, §7-§8 need an addition,
   but no bank question depends on it (q-f4c5-044 turns only on the attribution).

6. **"Recurrent but not periodic" wording.** The chapter stresses that cycles are
   recurrent but **not periodic**, and q-f4c5-023 and q-f4c5-030 depend on that
   phrasing. Some texts loosely call cycles "periodic"; confirm the SM uses the
   "recurrent but not periodic / not strictly regular" formulation before the draft
   badge comes off, since the distractors are built on it.
