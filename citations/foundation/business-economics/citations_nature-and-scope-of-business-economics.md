## The four definitions of economics and their authors

- **Result relied on:** economics has been defined as the **science of wealth**
  (Adam Smith, *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations*,
  1776); as the study of mankind in the ordinary business of life, concerned
  with the attainment and use of the **material requisites of well-being**, i.e.
  human **welfare** (Alfred Marshall, *Principles of Economics*, 1890); as
  **the science which studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and
  scarce means which have alternative uses** (Lionel Robbins, 1932); and as the
  study of how scarce productive resources are used to produce commodities and
  distribute them for consumption **over time** — the **growth** definition
  (Paul Samuelson). Adam Smith is conventionally called the **father of
  economics**.
- **Source:** standard history of economic thought, in the elementary form in
  which Foundation states it; SM Ch 1 — reference_only. The Robbins sentence is a
  direct quotation of a definition in general circulation and is attributed, not

## Business (managerial) economics

- **Result relied on:** business economics, also called managerial economics, is

  a widely quoted description due to **Spencer and Siegelman** calls it the

## Scarcity, choice and opportunity cost

- **Result relied on:** unlimited wants set against limited resources that have
  alternative uses force **choice**, and every choice carries an **opportunity
  cost**, defined as the value of the **next best alternative forgone**. The
  opportunity cost is the single most valuable alternative given up, not the sum
  of all alternatives; it need not be a money cost; and it is faced by every
  decision-maker — an individual, a firm or a government.
- **Source:** standard; SM Ch 1 — reference_only.

## The Production Possibility Curve

- **Result relied on:** the Production Possibility Curve (also called the
  production possibility frontier or **transformation curve**) shows the
  **maximum combinations of two goods** producible with given resources and
  technology, all resources being **fully and efficiently employed**. Its
  assumptions are two goods, fixed resources, fixed technology, and resources
  that are fully and efficiently used but **not equally efficient** in both uses.
  It **slopes downward** (more of one good means less of the other) and is
  **concave to the origin** because the **marginal opportunity cost increases**.
  A point **on** the curve is efficient / fully employed; a point **inside** is
  inefficient (idle or misused resources); a point **outside** is unattainable
  with present resources and technology and becomes reachable only through
  **growth**, which shifts the curve outward. The slope at a point is the
  **marginal rate of transformation**.
- **Source:** standard microeconomic treatment of the PPC; SM Ch 1 —
  reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f4c1-029 to q-f4c1-037; notes §6 (the schedule worked note and
  the "mistake" callout) and worked examples 3, 4 and 5. The verifier's `PPF`
  schedule and `marginal_costs()` reproduce the increasing-opportunity-cost
  pattern (4, 6, 8, 10).

## Positive and normative economics

- **Result relied on:** **positive economics** deals with what **is, was, or will
  be** — factual, objective statements that can in principle be tested;
  **normative economics** deals with what **ought to be** — value judgements that
  cannot be settled by facts alone. Words such as *ought, should, must, fair,
  desirable* signal a normative statement.

## The basic economic systems

- **Result relied on:** a **capitalist (market) economy** has private ownership
  of the means of production, answers the central problems through the **price
  mechanism**, is driven by the **profit motive**, and features **consumer
  sovereignty**. A **socialist (centrally planned / command) economy** has state
  ownership, answers the central problems through a **central planning
  authority** guided by **social welfare**, and offers limited consumer choice. A
  **mixed economy** has private and public sectors **coexisting**, with the
  government correcting market failures and reducing inequality; **India** is the
  standard example.
- **Source:** standard; SM Ch 1 — reference_only.

## Scope and role of business economics, and its fundamental concepts

- **Result relied on:** the scope of business economics is usually mapped to

  decisions and policies, profit management, and capital management. Its
  **fundamental concepts** are the **opportunity cost principle**, the
  **incremental (marginal) principle** (a decision is worthwhile if extra revenue

## Basic terms — utility, price and market

- **Result relied on:** **utility** is the want-satisfying power of a good and is
  subjective; **price** is the exchange value of a good expressed in money per
  unit; a **market** in economics is an **arrangement** through which buyers and
  sellers interact to exchange at a price, not necessarily a physical place, and
  may be local, national or global.
