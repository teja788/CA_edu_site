## Functions of money

- **Result relied on:** money is anything generally accepted as a means of payment
  and in the settlement of debts. Its two **primary** functions are the medium of
  exchange (which removes the barter double coincidence of wants) and the measure
  (unit) of value; its two **secondary** (derived) functions are the store of
  value and the standard of deferred payment.
- **Source:** standard monetary economics; SM Ch 8 Unit 1 — reference_only.

## The quantity theory of money: Fisher and Cambridge

- **Results relied on, in their conventional statements:**
  - Fisher's equation of exchange: **MV = PT**, where M is the quantity of money,
    V the transactions velocity of circulation, P the general price level and T
    the volume of transactions. With V and T treated as fixed in the short run,
    P moves in direct proportion to M; rearranged, P = MV ÷ T.
  - The Cambridge cash-balance equation: **M = kPY**, where k is the proportion of
    nominal income held as cash balances, P the price level and Y real income.
    The Cambridge k is the reciprocal of velocity, **k = 1 ÷ V**, so the two
    formulations are one theory seen from opposite sides.
- **Source:** standard monetary economics (Fisher; Marshall–Pigou Cambridge
  approach); SM Ch 8 Unit 1 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f4c8-005, q-f4c8-006, q-f4c8-007, q-f4c8-011; notes §2 and its
  mistake callout, and worked example 6.

## The Keynesian liquidity-preference theory

- **Results relied on:** Keynes' demand for money rests on three motives — the
  **transactions** and **precautionary** motives, which depend on the level of
  income, and the **speculative** motive, which depends inversely on the rate of
  interest (a high interest rate means a low speculative demand for money, and a
  low rate a high one). The total demand for money therefore rises with income and
  falls as the interest rate rises. At a very low interest rate the speculative
  demand can become perfectly elastic — the **liquidity trap** — so further
  increases in money are absorbed as idle balances.
- **Source:** standard macroeconomics (Keynes, liquidity preference); SM Ch 8
  Unit 1 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f4c8-008, q-f4c8-009, q-f4c8-010, q-f4c8-012; notes §3 and its
  pointer.

  speculative motive to the interest rate and the transactions / precautionary
  motives to income, since q-f4c8-009 and q-f4c8-010 turn on that split.

## Friedman's restatement

- **Result relied on:** Friedman restated the quantity theory as a modern theory
  of the **demand for money**, treating money as one asset among many and the
  demand for it as a **stable function** of permanent income (the chief variable),
  the returns on competing assets, and tastes. The monetarist conclusion is that
  changes in the money supply are the dominant force behind changes in money
  income and prices.
- **Source:** standard (Friedman, monetarism); SM Ch 8 Unit 1 — reference_only.

## The RBI monetary aggregates M1–M4

- **Institutional definitions relied on (as stated by the RBI), reference_only:**
  - **M1** (narrow money) = currency with the public + demand deposits with banks
    + other deposits with the RBI.
  - **M2** = M1 + savings deposits with post-office savings banks.
  - **M3** (broad money) = M1 + time (fixed) deposits with banks.
  - **M4** = M3 + all deposits with post-office savings banks (excluding National
    Savings Certificates).
  - Liquidity falls from M1 to M4 while the size of the aggregate rises.

## High-powered money and the money multiplier

- **Results relied on:** high-powered money (reserve money / the monetary base,
  H) = currency held by the public + bankers' deposits (reserves) with the RBI +
  other deposits with the RBI. The **money multiplier** m = money supply ÷ high-
  powered money (MS ÷ H), so MS = m × H and H = MS ÷ m. The multiplier is normally
  greater than 1 because a rupee of base supports several rupees of deposits.
- **Source:** standard monetary economics; SM Ch 8 Unit 2 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f4c8-021, q-f4c8-022, q-f4c8-023, q-f4c8-024, q-f4c8-032,

## Credit creation by commercial banks

- **Results relied on:** a **primary deposit** is the cash a customer pays in; a
  **derivative deposit** is a deposit a bank creates by lending. With a required
  reserve ratio r, banks lend (1 − r) of each deposit, the loan returns as a
  deposit, and the process repeats; the deposits form a geometric series summing
  to **total deposits (credit) created = initial deposit ÷ r = initial deposit ×
  (1 ÷ r)**. The **credit (deposit) multiplier = 1 ÷ r**. The **derivative
  deposits** (the new money) = total − initial deposit. Required reserves against a
  stock of deposits = deposits × r.
- **Source:** standard banking theory; SM Ch 8 Unit 2 — reference_only. The
  geometric-series result is the standard deposit-multiplier derivation.
- **Used in:** q-f4c8-025, q-f4c8-026, q-f4c8-027, q-f4c8-028, q-f4c8-029,
  q-f4c8-030, q-f4c8-031, q-f4c8-034, q-f4c8-035; notes §8 (the two formula
  blocks and the working note) and worked examples 2, 4 and 8.
- **Spot-checked by:** _(blank until a human checks)_ — confirm the SM defines

## The limitations of credit creation

- **Result relied on:** the credit multiplier 1 ÷ r is a **maximum**, reached only
  under ideal conditions. Actual credit is pulled below it by a higher reserve
  ratio, a cash drain (the public holding part of each loan as currency), excess
  reserves held by cautious banks, a shortage of creditworthy borrowers with sound
  securities, and the overall monetary policy of the RBI.
- **Source:** standard banking theory; SM Ch 8 Unit 2 — reference_only.

## Quantitative (general) instruments of monetary policy

- **Institutional facts relied on (instrument NAMES as used by the RBI),
  reference_only:** the quantitative instruments are the **Cash Reserve Ratio
  (CRR)** — cash reserves held with the RBI; the **Statutory Liquidity Ratio
  (SLR)** — liquid assets (cash, gold, approved securities) held by the bank with
  itself; the **repo rate** — the rate at which the RBI lends short-term to banks
  against securities; the **reverse repo rate** — the rate at which the RBI
  borrows from banks; the **bank rate** — the RBI's longer-term lending rate;
  **open market operations (OMO)** — the RBI's buying and selling of government
  securities; the **Marginal Standing Facility (MSF)** — overnight borrowing above
  the normal limit at a rate slightly above the repo rate; and the **Liquidity
  Adjustment Facility (LAF)** — the day-to-day repo / reverse-repo framework.
  A **rise** in any rate or ratio, or a **sale** of securities, reduces the money
  supply; a **cut**, or a **purchase** of securities, increases it.
- **Source:** RBI monetary-policy instrument names (institutional facts), used
  reference_only; SM Ch 8 Unit 3 restates them. Any illustrative rate is a
  teaching figure, **not a current RBI rate**.

## Expansionary versus contractionary policy

- **Result relied on:** an **expansionary (easy / cheap-money)** policy increases
  the money supply and lowers the cost of credit — the RBI cuts the CRR, SLR, repo
  and bank rate and buys securities — and is used against recession and
  unemployment. A **contractionary (tight / dear-money)** policy reduces the money
  supply and raises the cost of credit — the RBI raises the CRR, SLR, repo and bank
  rate and sells securities — and is used against inflation.
- **Source:** standard; SM Ch 8 Unit 3 — reference_only.

# Reviewer's checklist — conventions chosen where practice varies

These are editorial calls, not sourced positions. Each one changes what a student
is taught, and each needs a human to confirm it against the SM.

1. **The primary / secondary split of the functions of money.** The chapter names
   the medium of exchange and the measure of value as the **primary** functions,
   and the store of value and the standard of deferred payment as the
   **secondary** (derived) functions. q-f4c8-001 and q-f4c8-003 turn on this
   split. If the SM lists the functions without the primary / secondary
   distinction, §1 and those two keys should be re-checked, though the answers
   ("medium of exchange" as primary, "standard of deferred payment" as secondary)
   are the standard descriptions.

2. **The two multipliers: MS ÷ H versus 1 ÷ r.** The chapter keeps the **money
   multiplier** (money supply ÷ high-powered money) distinct from the **credit /
   deposit multiplier** (1 ÷ r). The verifier follows the same split: it computes
   MS ÷ H for q-f4c8-021/022/023 and 1 ÷ r for q-f4c8-027/032/033. Confirm the SM
   treats the simple deposit multiplier 1 ÷ r and does not, at Foundation level,
   require the full currency-and-reserve money-multiplier formula
   m = (1 + c) ÷ (r + c); the notes mention that the two are close cousins but
   keep the computations to the simple forms.

3. **Total credit versus derivative deposits.** The chapter defines **total
   deposits (credit) created = initial deposit ÷ r** (which INCLUDES the original
   primary deposit) and **derivative deposits = total − initial** (the new money
   only). q-f4c8-028 (total = 50,000) and q-f4c8-029 (derivative = 40,000) depend
   on this convention, and the distractors are built from the other reading.
   Confirm the SM uses "total deposits created / total credit" for the ÷ r figure;
   if it labels the ÷ r figure "derivative deposits" instead, the wording of §8
   and the two stems must be aligned (the keys already separate the two values, so
   only the labels would move).

4. **Classic M1–M4 aggregates rather than NM1–NM3.** The chapter uses the classic
   monetary aggregates M1, M2, M3, M4 with the standard compositions, because that
   is what the Foundation SM teaches. If the current SM edition instead centres on
   the "new" monetary aggregates (NM1–NM3) or on reserve money / NDTL definitions,
   §6 and q-f4c8-016 to q-f4c8-020 should be checked against that presentation.

5. **Illustrative rates are teaching numbers, not RBI rates.** Every repo rate,
   CRR, reserve ratio and reserve-money figure in the notes and the bank is chosen
   to make the arithmetic clean (a reserve ratio of 20% giving a multiplier of 5,
   for example). None is a current or historical RBI policy rate, and none should
   be read as one. Confirm no stem accidentally implies a live rate before the
   draft badge comes off.

6. **Rounding in the numerical answers.** Every computational stem is built so the
   exact multiplier, total or price is a clean fraction or whole number; the
   verifier uses exact `Fraction` arithmetic and never rounds. Where an option
   quotes a decimal (for example "0.2" for 1/5 or "12.5" for the ratio), the
   decimal is only in the printed option text, and the verifier maps to the exact
   fraction. Confirm the SM's own rounding style matches if the draft badge is to
   come off.
