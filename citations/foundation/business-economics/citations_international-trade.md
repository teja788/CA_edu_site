## Why countries trade, and absolute advantage

- **Result relied on:** countries trade because they differ in resources,
  climate, skills and technology, so specialisation raises world output. A
  country has an absolute advantage (Adam Smith) in a good when it can produce
  more of it from the same resources, or use fewer resources per unit, and should
  specialise there.
- **Source:** standard international economics (Smith, 1776); SM Ch 9 Unit 1 —
  reference_only.

## Comparative advantage, opportunity cost and the gains from trade

- **Results relied on:** comparative advantage (David Ricardo, 1817) — a country
  has a comparative advantage in the good it can produce at the lower opportunity
  cost, where the opportunity cost of one unit of a good is the quantity of the
  other good given up to make it. Trade benefits both countries even when one has
  the absolute advantage in both goods, provided the opportunity costs differ.
  The two countries' comparative advantages fall on opposite goods. Both gain only
  if the exchange ratio settles between the two countries' opportunity-cost ratios.
- **Source:** standard (Ricardian comparative-cost theory); SM Ch 9 Unit 1 —
  reference_only.

## Heckscher–Ohlin and the terms of trade

- **Results relied on:** the Heckscher–Ohlin (factor-endowment) theory — a
  country exports the good that uses its abundant (and therefore cheap) factor
  intensively and imports the good that uses its scarce factor intensively
  (labour-abundant → labour-intensive exports; capital-abundant →
  capital-intensive exports). The (commodity / net barter) terms of trade =
  (index of export prices ÷ index of import prices) × 100; a value above 100 is a
  movement in the country's favour.
- **Source:** standard (Heckscher and Ohlin); SM Ch 9 Unit 1 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f4c9-009, q-f4c9-010, q-f4c9-011; notes §3 and worked example 3.

## Instruments of trade policy: tariffs, quotas and non-tariff barriers

- **Results relied on:** free trade is trade free of government-imposed barriers;
  protection uses instruments of trade policy. A tariff is a tax on a traded
  (usually imported) good — a **specific** tariff is a fixed sum per unit, an **ad
  valorem** tariff a percentage of value — and an import tariff raises the domestic
  price (price after specific tariff = world price + tariff; price after ad valorem
  tariff = value × (1 + rate)), benefiting producers and the government and
  burdening consumers, with a net cost to the economy. A quota is a direct
  quantitative limit on imports; it raises the domestic price like a tariff but
  usually raises no revenue for the government. Other non-tariff barriers include
  subsidies to domestic producers, voluntary export restraints, and administrative
  or technical barriers.
- **Source:** standard trade-policy analysis; SM Ch 9 Unit 2 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f4c9-012, q-f4c9-013, q-f4c9-014, q-f4c9-015, q-f4c9-016,

## The WTO, its principles, and regional blocs

- **Institutional facts relied on (reference_only):** the General Agreement on
  Tariffs and Trade (GATT) provided the trade-rules framework from 1948; the World
  Trade Organization (WTO) came into existence on 1 January 1995, out of the
  Uruguay Round, absorbing and extending GATT, and is based in Geneva. The WTO's
  functions include administering trade agreements, providing a forum for
  negotiations, settling disputes, reviewing national trade policies and assisting
  developing countries. Its two non-discrimination principles are
  most-favoured-nation treatment (an advantage granted to one member must be
  extended to all members) and national treatment (imported goods are treated no
  less favourably than like domestic goods once inside the market). Regional trade
  blocs rise in integration from a free-trade area to a customs union (with a
  common external tariff) to a common market (free factor movement) to an economic
  union.
- **Source:** WTO / GATT institutional history and rules — reference_only; SM Ch 9
  Unit 3.

## The exchange rate: nominal, real, effective, and its determination

- **Results relied on:** the foreign-exchange rate is the price of one currency in
  terms of another. The **nominal** rate is the plain market rate; the **real**
  rate is the nominal rate adjusted for the two countries' price levels
  (competitiveness); the **effective** (nominal effective) rate is a trade-weighted
  average against a basket of currencies. Under a **floating** system the rate is
  set by the demand for and supply of foreign exchange — demand for foreign
  exchange comes from importers and residents paying abroad (downward-sloping),
  supply from exporters and foreign inflows (upward-sloping), with equilibrium
  where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. Currency conversion multiplies
  by the rate from dollars to rupees and divides from rupees to dollars; a cross
  rate multiplies through the common currency.
- **Source:** standard open-economy macroeconomics; SM Ch 9 Unit 4 —
  reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f4c9-029, q-f4c9-030, q-f4c9-031, q-f4c9-032, q-f4c9-033,
  q-f4c9-041; notes §9, §10 and worked examples 5 and 6.

## Appreciation, depreciation, devaluation, and fixed versus floating systems

- **Results relied on:** appreciation = fewer units of home currency per unit of
  foreign currency (home currency stronger); depreciation = more units per unit of
  foreign currency (home currency weaker). A depreciation makes exports cheaper to
  foreigners and imports dearer at home (tending to raise exports and curb
  imports); an appreciation does the reverse and helps importers. A **devaluation**
  is a deliberate downward change of an official rate under a **fixed** system (and
  a **revaluation** the deliberate upward change), whereas a **depreciation** /
  **appreciation** is a market movement under a **floating** system. Under a fixed
  system the central bank pegs and defends the rate with reserves; a floating
  system leaves the rate to the market (a managed float is the middle ground).
- **Source:** standard; SM Ch 9 Unit 4 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f4c9-034, q-f4c9-035, q-f4c9-036, q-f4c9-037, q-f4c9-038,
  q-f4c9-039, q-f4c9-040; notes §11 and worked example 7.

## International capital movements: FDI versus FPI

- **Results relied on:** foreign direct investment (FDI) is investment giving the
  foreign investor a lasting interest and a degree of control in a business in the
  host country — tied to real, productive assets, long-term and relatively stable,
  and usually bringing technology, management skill and jobs. Foreign portfolio
  investment (FPI) is investment in financial assets (shares and bonds) without
  control, seeking a financial return; it is liquid and short-term and can leave
  quickly ("hot money"). The dividing line is control.
- **Source:** standard international finance; SM Ch 9 Unit 5 — reference_only.

## The balance of payments and the balance of trade

- **Results relied on:** the balance of payments is a systematic record of all
  economic transactions between the residents of a country and the rest of the
  world over a period; on double-entry principles it always balances in total, and
  a "surplus" or "deficit" refers to particular accounts. The **current account**
  records goods, services, income and current transfers (and contains the balance
  of trade); the **capital (and financial) account** records capital flows — FDI,
  FPI, external loans, banking capital and changes in reserves — and finances any
  current-account gap. The **balance of trade** is the goods-only figure
  (exports of goods − imports of goods). The current-account balance = balance of
  trade + net services + net income + net current transfers. A country can run a
  trade deficit yet a current-account surplus if invisibles cover the goods gap.
- **Source:** standard (IMF balance-of-payments structure, conceptual); SM Ch 9
  Unit 5 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f4c9-045, q-f4c9-046, q-f4c9-047, q-f4c9-048, q-f4c9-049,
  q-f4c9-050; notes §13 and worked example 8.
