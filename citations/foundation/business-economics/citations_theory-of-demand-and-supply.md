## The law of demand and the shape of the demand curve

- **Result relied on:** ceteris paribus, quantity demanded varies inversely with
  the good's own price, so the demand curve slopes downward from left to right.
  The downward slope is explained by the law of diminishing marginal utility and
  by the income and substitution effects of a price change.
- **Source:** standard microeconomics; SM Ch 2 — reference_only.

## Market demand and the determinants

- **Result relied on:** market demand is the horizontal summation of individual
  demands — at each price, the sum of the quantities all buyers demand. Demand
  depends on the good's own price, the buyer's income (positively for normal
  goods, negatively for inferior goods), the prices of related goods (substitutes

## Movement along versus a shift of the curve

- **Result relied on:** a change in the good's own price produces a **movement
  along** the demand curve — a change in **quantity demanded** (extension when
  price falls, contraction when it rises). A change in any determinant other than
  the own price produces a **shift** of the whole curve — a change in **demand**
  (rightward = increase, leftward = decrease). The identical distinction applies
  on the supply side.
- **Source:** standard; SM Ch 2 — reference_only.

## Exceptions to the law of demand

- **Result relied on:** the recognised exceptions are Giffen goods (strongly
  inferior staples for which the negative income effect outweighs the
  substitution effect, so quantity demanded rises with price), Veblen or
  conspicuous goods (luxuries bought for status, whose demand can rise with
  price), goods bought on speculation (buyers buy more when they expect the price
  to rise further), and articles of necessity in emergencies. The Giffen good is
  the strict theoretical exception.

## Utility analysis: diminishing marginal utility, equilibrium and consumer surplus

- **Results relied on:** marginal utility of the nth unit = TU(n) − TU(n − 1);
  the law of diminishing marginal utility (marginal utility falls as more is
  consumed); total utility is maximum when marginal utility is zero, rising while
  MU is positive and falling while MU is negative; the cardinal consumer
  equilibrium condition is the law of equi-marginal utility, MUx ÷ Px = MUy ÷ Py;
  and consumer surplus is the excess of what a buyer is willing to pay over what
  the buyer actually pays.

## Indifference-curve analysis

- **Results relied on:** an indifference curve joins bundles giving equal
  satisfaction; indifference curves slope downward, are convex to the origin
  (a diminishing marginal rate of substitution), never intersect, and a higher
  curve means higher satisfaction. The budget line shows the affordable
  combinations given income and the two prices, with slope equal to the price
  ratio. Ordinal consumer equilibrium is the tangency of the budget line with the
  highest attainable indifference curve, where the marginal rate of substitution
  equals the price ratio.
- **Source:** standard (Hicks–Allen ordinal utility theory); SM Ch 2 —
  reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f4c2-017, q-f4c2-018; notes §8 and its pointer.

## Price elasticity of demand: definition, methods and degrees

- **Results relied on, in their conventional statements:**
  - Price elasticity of demand e = (percentage change in quantity demanded) ÷
    (percentage change in price). Because quantity moves opposite to price the
    ratio is negative, and it is conventionally quoted as an **absolute value**.
  - **Percentage (proportionate) method** — each percentage change is computed
    from the **original** value.
  - **Arc method** — e = [ (Q₂ − Q₁) ÷ (Q₁ + Q₂) ] ÷ [ (P₂ − P₁) ÷ (P₁ + P₂) ],
    using the average of the two bases; used when the price change is large.
  - **Point method** — e = |slope of the demand curve| × (P ÷ Q); for a linear
    demand Q = a − bP the slope term is b, so e = b × (P ÷ Q). Elasticity varies
    along a straight-line demand curve even though its slope is constant.
  - **Five degrees:** e = 0 perfectly inelastic (vertical); 0 &lt; e &lt; 1
    inelastic; e = 1 unit elastic; e &gt; 1 elastic; e = ∞ perfectly elastic
    (horizontal).
- **Source:** standard microeconomics; SM Ch 2 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f4c2-019, q-f4c2-020, q-f4c2-021, q-f4c2-022, q-f4c2-023,

## Elasticity and total revenue

- **Result relied on:** total revenue = price × quantity. For elastic demand

## Income elasticity and cross elasticity

- **Results relied on, with the sign kept:**
  - Income elasticity of demand = (percentage change in quantity demanded) ÷
    (percentage change in income). Positive → normal good (greater than 1 →
    luxury; between 0 and 1 → necessity); negative → inferior good.
  - Cross elasticity of demand for X with respect to the price of Y = (percentage

    substitutes; negative → complements; zero → unrelated.

## Supply, the law of supply, and elasticity of supply

- **Results relied on:** supply is the quantity sellers are willing to offer at a
  given price over a period. The law of supply: ceteris paribus, quantity
  supplied varies **directly** with price, so the supply curve slopes upward. A
  change in the own price moves the seller along the curve; a change in input
  prices, technology, the prices of other goods, taxes and subsidies, the number
  of sellers, or expectations shifts the curve (rightward = increase, leftward =
  decrease). Elasticity of supply = (percentage change in quantity supplied) ÷
  (percentage change in price), normally positive; a vertical supply curve is
  perfectly inelastic (e = 0) and a horizontal one perfectly elastic (e = ∞);
  supply is more elastic the longer the time period.
- **Source:** standard; SM Ch 2 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f4c2-036, q-f4c2-037, q-f4c2-038, q-f4c2-039, q-f4c2-040,
  q-f4c2-041; notes §15 and §16.

## Market equilibrium and the effect of shifts

- **Results relied on:** market equilibrium is the price at which quantity
  demanded equals quantity supplied — the intersection of the demand and supply
  curves. It is found from linear demand and supply equations by setting the two
  quantities equal, solving for the equilibrium price, and substituting back for
  the equilibrium quantity. Above the equilibrium price a surplus (excess supply)
  pushes the price down; below it a shortage (excess demand) pushes the price up.
  A demand increase raises both equilibrium price and quantity; a demand decrease
  lowers both; a supply increase lowers price and raises quantity; a supply
  decrease raises price and lowers quantity — a demand shift moves price and
  quantity together, a supply shift moves them oppositely; when both curves shift,
  only one of the two is unambiguously determined.
- **Source:** standard microeconomics; SM Ch 2 — reference_only. The linear
  demand/supply algebra is the standard simultaneous-equation method.
- **Used in:** q-f4c2-043, q-f4c2-044, q-f4c2-045, q-f4c2-046, q-f4c2-047,
  q-f4c2-048, q-f4c2-049, q-f4c2-050; notes §17, §18 and worked examples 7 and 8.

# Reviewer's checklist — conventions chosen where practice varies

These are editorial calls, not sourced positions. Each one changes what a student
is taught, and each needs a human to confirm it against the SM.

1. **Sign versus absolute value of elasticity.** The chapter quotes **price**
   elasticity of demand as an **absolute value** (the stems say "in magnitude"),
   because its sign is always negative, but keeps the **sign** of income and cross
   elasticity, because there the sign classifies the good. The verifier follows
   the same split: it takes `abs(...)` for the price-elasticity ids and keeps the
   sign for the income and cross-elasticity ids. If the SM quotes price elasticity
   as a negative number throughout, the §9–§11 wording (not the keys) should be
   aligned; the income and cross-elasticity sign convention must not be changed,
   because q-f4c2-028, q-f4c2-030 and q-f4c2-031 depend on it.

2. **Which method the exam wants.** The notes teach three measures — percentage
   (original base), arc (average base) and point (slope × P/Q). The bank asks for
   a named method in every computational elasticity question (q-f4c2-020 and
   q-f4c2-033 percentage, q-f4c2-021 arc, q-f4c2-023 point), so no answer is
   ambiguous. Confirm the SM presents all three; if it omits the arc method,
   q-f4c2-021 can be re-cast as a percentage-method item without changing any
   other question.

3. **How many exceptions to the law of demand.** The chapter lists four families
   (Giffen, Veblen, speculative, emergency) and names the **Giffen good** as the
   strict theoretical exception. If the SM's list differs (some editions add
   "ignorance" or "necessaries of life" as separate heads, or treat Veblen goods
   under "prestige goods"), §5 and q-f4c2-010/011/012 should be checked for
   wording, though the keys turn only on the standard Giffen and Veblen
   descriptions.

4. **Depth of the indifference-curve treatment.** The notes cover the properties,
   the budget line and the tangency equilibrium, but do **not** derive the demand
   curve from a price-consumption curve or decompose the price effect into income
   and substitution effects on an indifference-curve diagram. Confirm this matches
   the Foundation depth; if the SM requires the decomposition, §8 needs an
   addition, but no bank question depends on it.

5. **Rounding in elasticity answers.** Every computational stem is built so the
   exact elasticity or equilibrium value is a clean fraction or whole number; the
   verifier uses exact `Fraction` arithmetic and never rounds. Where an option
   quotes a rounded decimal (for example "0.67" for 2/3, or "1.67" for 5/3), the
   rounding is only in the printed option text, and the verifier maps to the exact
   fraction. Confirm the SM's own rounding style matches if the draft badge is to
   come off.

6. **Both-curves-shift ambiguity.** The §18 pointer states that when demand and
   supply both shift, only one of price and quantity is unambiguous without the
   sizes of the shifts. q-f4c2-050 deliberately tests only a single-curve
   (demand) shift, where both effects are determinate. Confirm the SM treats the
   double-shift case at Foundation level; if not, the pointer is background only
   and can be trimmed.
