## The three fiscal functions (Musgrave)

- **Result relied on:** Richard Musgrave classified the budgetary role of
  government into three fiscal functions — the **allocation** function (providing
  public and merit goods and correcting externalities, where the market
  mis-allocates), the **distribution** function (redistributing income and wealth
  through progressive taxes and transfers), and the **stabilization** function
  (using the budget to smooth the business cycle and control inflation and
  unemployment).
- **Source:** standard public finance (Musgrave); SM Ch 7 Unit 1 —
  reference_only.

## Market failure: meaning and causes

- **Result relied on:** market failure is the situation in which the market, on
  its own, fails to allocate resources efficiently (it produces the wrong
  quantities, not that the market ceases to exist). Its standard causes are
  public goods, externalities, imperfect competition (market power) and
  imperfect/asymmetric information; perfect competition is efficient and is
  therefore not a cause.
- **Source:** standard microeconomics/public finance; SM Ch 7 Unit 2 —

## Public, private and merit goods

- **Result relied on:** a public good is **non-rival** (one person's use does not
  reduce what is left for others) and **non-excludable** (non-payers cannot be
  shut out), and its non-excludability creates the **free-rider problem** that
  leaves it under-supplied by the market, so the government provides it. A private
  good is rival and excludable. A merit good is one whose social benefit exceeds
  private demand (education, healthcare); it is usually excludable and rival but
  is encouraged or subsidised because private demand under-states its worth.
- **Source:** standard; SM Ch 7 Unit 2 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f4c7-011, q-f4c7-012, q-f4c7-013, q-f4c7-014; notes §4, its
  mistake callout, and worked example 3.
- **Spot-checked by:** _(blank until a human checks)_ — confirm the SM treats
  merit goods as a category distinct from public goods, and does not classify a
  merit good as a public good.

## Externalities

- **Result relied on:** an externality is a cost or benefit falling on a third
  party and not reflected in the market price. A **negative** externality means
  social cost exceeds private cost, so the market **over-produces** (a polluting
  factory); a **positive** externality means social benefit exceeds private
  benefit, so the market **under-produces** (vaccination). Externalities arise in
  both production and consumption.
- **Source:** standard; SM Ch 7 Unit 2 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f4c7-015, q-f4c7-016, q-f4c7-017, q-f4c7-018; notes §5 and worked
  example 4.

## Imperfect competition and asymmetric information

- **Result relied on:** a monopoly or firm with market power **restricts output
  and charges a price above marginal cost**, an inefficient (under-produced)
  outcome, so market power is a source of market failure. **Imperfect
  information** means both sides lack full knowledge; **asymmetric information**
  means one side knows more than the other (the used-car seller, the insured
  person), which distorts the market and is a recognised source of market
  failure.
- **Source:** standard; SM Ch 7 Unit 2 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f4c7-019, q-f4c7-020; notes §6 and its mistake callout.

## Government intervention to correct market failure

- **Result relied on:** the remedies pair with the causes — a pure **public

  externality** by a **tax** on (or regulation of) the harmful activity; a
  **positive externality** by a **subsidy**; **imperfect competition** by
  regulation and competition law; and **asymmetric information** by disclosure

## The government budget and its receipts

- **Result relied on:** the budget is a statement of the government's estimated
  receipts and expenditure for a financial year. **Revenue receipts** (taxes and
  non-tax revenue) neither create a liability nor reduce an asset; **capital
  receipts** create a liability (borrowing) or reduce an asset (disinvestment,
  loan recovery). **Borrowing is therefore a capital receipt, never revenue** —
  the point that makes the fiscal-deficit definition work.
- **Source:** standard public finance; SM Ch 7 Unit 3 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f4c7-022, q-f4c7-023; notes §8 and its mistake callout.

## Tax and non-tax revenue; taxation

- **Results relied on:**
  - Government revenue is **tax** revenue (a compulsory payment with no direct
    service in return) plus **non-tax** revenue (interest, dividends and profits,
    fees, fines and charges). A **fee** is charged for a specific service and is
    **non-tax** revenue, not a tax.
  - A **direct** tax is one whose burden **cannot be shifted** (income tax,
    corporation tax); an **indirect** tax is levied on goods and services and its
    burden **can be shifted** in the price (GST, customs).
  - A tax is **progressive** when the average rate **rises** with income,
    **proportional** when the average rate is **constant**, and **regressive**
    when the average rate **falls** as income rises.
  - **Adam Smith's four canons of taxation:** equality (ability to pay),
    certainty, convenience, economy.
  - **Impact** of a tax is on the person who first pays it (the point of levy);
    **incidence** is on the person who finally bears it and cannot shift it. They
    fall on the same person for a direct tax and on different persons for an
    indirect tax.
- **Source:** standard public finance (including Adam Smith's canons); SM Ch 7
  Unit 3 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f4c7-024, q-f4c7-025, q-f4c7-026, q-f4c7-027, q-f4c7-028,
  q-f4c7-030, q-f4c7-031, q-f4c7-032; notes §9, §10 and worked examples 5 and 6.

## Public expenditure and public debt

- **Results relied on:** **revenue expenditure** neither creates an asset nor
  reduces a liability (salaries, subsidies, and **interest payments**); **capital
  expenditure** creates an asset or reduces a liability (roads, dams, repayment of
  principal). The older split is **developmental (plan)** versus
  **non-developmental** expenditure. **Public debt** is classified as **internal**
  (owed to lenders within the country, repaid in domestic currency) or
  **external** (owed abroad, serviced in foreign currency); governments borrow for
  capital projects, stabilisation and temporary gaps, and **redemption** is the
  repayment of debt.
- **Source:** standard; SM Ch 7 Unit 3 — reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f4c7-029; notes §11, §12 and the primary-deficit treatment in
  §13.
- **Spot-checked by:** _(blank until a human checks)_ — confirm the SM classifies
  interest payments as revenue expenditure (only repayment of principal is

## The budget deficits

- **Results relied on, in their conventional formulas:**
  - **Revenue deficit** = revenue expenditure − revenue receipts.
  - **Fiscal deficit** = total expenditure − total receipts **excluding
    borrowings**; it equals the government's borrowing for the year.
  - **Primary deficit** = fiscal deficit − interest payments.
- **Source:** standard Indian public-finance definitions; SM Ch 7 Unit 3 —
  reference_only.

## Fiscal policy

- **Results relied on:** fiscal policy is the government's use of **taxation and
  expenditure** (and borrowing) to influence the economy, distinct from the
  central bank's **monetary policy**. Its objectives are growth, full employment,
  price stability, equitable distribution and overall stabilisation. Its
  **instruments** are taxation, public expenditure, public debt and the budget.
  **Expansionary** policy (cut taxes, raise spending, widening the deficit) raises
  aggregate demand and suits a recession; **contractionary** policy (raise taxes,
  cut spending, narrowing the deficit) lowers aggregate demand and suits demand-
  pull inflation. **Automatic stabilizers** (a progressive tax, unemployment
  benefit) act with no new decision, whereas **discretionary** policy is a
  deliberate, announced change. Government spending is a component of aggregate
  demand, so a rise in it increases aggregate demand directly.
- **Source:** standard macroeconomics/public finance; SM Ch 7 Unit 4 —
  reference_only.
- **Used in:** q-f4c7-042, q-f4c7-043, q-f4c7-044, q-f4c7-045, q-f4c7-046,
  q-f4c7-047, q-f4c7-048, q-f4c7-049, q-f4c7-050; notes §14, §15, §16, §17 and
  worked example 8.

# Reviewer's checklist — conventions chosen where practice varies

These are editorial calls, not sourced positions. Each one changes what a student
is taught, and each needs a human to confirm it against the SM.

1. **Taxation figures are illustrative, not current law.** Every rupee amount,
   slab rate and income in the chapter and the bank (worked example 6,
   q-f4c7-030, q-f4c7-031, q-f4c7-032) is an invented teaching number. The chapter
   states this in §10 and worked example 6. No figure should be read as the tax
   schedule in force for any assessment year, and the frontmatter omits
   `law_as_on_date` for exactly this reason. Confirm the SM's own examples, if the
   draft badge is to come off, but the concepts (progressive/proportional/
   regressive, impact/incidence) do not depend on the specific numbers.

2. **The fiscal-deficit definition.** The chapter defines the fiscal deficit as
   total expenditure minus total receipts **excluding borrowings**, and states
   that it equals the year's borrowing. The verifier follows the same definition
   for q-f4c7-034, q-f4c7-036, q-f4c7-038 and q-f4c7-039. If the SM presents the
   fiscal deficit through an alternative but equivalent statement (for example, as
   the excess of expenditure over revenue receipts plus non-debt capital
   receipts), the wording of §13 can be aligned; the keys are unaffected, because
   the two statements give the same number.

3. **Interest payments as revenue expenditure.** The chapter treats interest
   payments as revenue expenditure and singles them out in the primary deficit
   (fiscal deficit − interest). q-f4c7-035, q-f4c7-037 and q-f4c7-041 depend on
   this. Confirm the SM classifies interest the same way (only repayment of
   principal being capital expenditure).

4. **How many causes of market failure, and their labels.** The chapter lists
   four causes — public goods, externalities, imperfect competition (market
   power), and imperfect/asymmetric information. Some editions fold merit and
   demerit goods, or incomplete markets, in as separate heads. If the SM's list
   differs, §3 and q-f4c7-010 should be checked for wording, though the key of
   q-f4c7-010 turns only on the standard point that **perfect** competition is not
   a cause.

5. **Merit good versus public good.** The chapter keeps merit goods separate from
   public goods, describing a merit good as usually excludable and rival but
   under-demanded relative to its social benefit. q-f4c7-014 and the §4 mistake
   callout depend on this separation. Confirm the SM does not treat merit goods as
   a species of public good.

6. **Finance Commission at conceptual depth only.** The chapter names the Finance
   Commission as the body that recommends tax devolution but gives no award
   shares, Article numbers or Commission periods, on the view that Foundation
   testing is conceptual. q-f4c7-008 turns only on identifying the correct body.
   Confirm this matches the SM's depth; if the SM expects the current award
   percentages, §2 would need an addition, but no bank question depends on it.

7. **Rounding in the tax computations.** Every computational stem is built so the
   exact answer is a clean whole number or fraction; the verifier uses exact
   `Fraction` arithmetic and never rounds. Where an option quotes a percentage
   (for example "7.5%"), the verifier maps to the exact fraction (3/40). The
   average tax rate in worked example 6 (5.42%) is a rounded display of an exact
   fraction and is not a bank answer. Confirm the SM's rounding style matches if
   the draft badge is to come off.
