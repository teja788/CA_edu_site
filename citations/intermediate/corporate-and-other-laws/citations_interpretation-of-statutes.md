## Meaning of interpretation and construction; causes of uncertainty

- **Formulation relied on (own words):** interpretation is the process of
  ascertaining the meaning the legislature has expressed in the words it used;
  construction is the process of drawing conclusions about situations the words
  do not address in terms. The two are used interchangeably in practice. The
  causes of uncertainty relied on are ambiguity, vagueness, incomplete
  drafting, internal conflict, error of language and changing context; and the
  governing constraint is that the court finds what the legislature said and
  does not make the law.
- **Source:** standard doctrine as conventionally stated; ICAI SM P2 Ch 14
  (May 2026 ed.) scope. Written from memory 27 Aug 2026; nothing re-fetched,
  nothing quoted.

## Classification — legal (authentic / usual) and doctrinal; grammatical and logical

- **Formulation relied on (own words):** legal or authoritative interpretation
  is either **authentic** (given by the legislature itself, through an
  interpretation clause, an Explanation or a declaratory Act) or **usual**
  (given by courts in deciding cases); **doctrinal** interpretation is the work
  of jurists and commentators and is persuasive only, and is subdivided into
  **grammatical** (working on the words) and **logical** (working on the
  intention, object, setting and consequences). The sequencing relied on:
  grammatical construction governs, and logical construction is reached only
  where the grammatical result is ambiguous, absurd, repugnant, or defeats the
  object of the Act.
- **Source:** standard doctrine; ICAI SM P2 Ch 14 scope. **Reviewer note:**
  editions differ slightly in whether grammatical/logical is presented as a
  sub-division of doctrinal interpretation or as a free-standing pair. Both

- **Used in:** q-i2c14-003, q-i2c14-004, notes §2 (table and mistake callout),

## Literal / grammatical rule; casus omissus

- **Conventional formulation:** where the words of a statute are plain, clear
  and unambiguous, they must be given their ordinary, natural and grammatical
  meaning, whatever the consequence; the letter of the law is the best evidence
  of its intention; nothing is added and nothing is subtracted; and a *casus
  omissus* — a case the statute has omitted — cannot be supplied by the court.
  Ancillary positions relied on: ordinary popular sense, except technical sense
  in a trade, science or art; a statutory definition governs throughout unless
  the context otherwise requires; the same word is presumed to bear the same
  meaning throughout; hardship or inconvenience is no ground for departure.
- **Case anchors (named as anchors only, nothing quoted):** the "dog in the
  library" style of illustration is a textbook staple; the *Sussex Peerage* and
  *Whiteley v Chappell* lines of authority are the conventional anchors for the
  plain-meaning and casus-omissus points respectively. **No facts, head-note or
  judicial sentence from any of them is reproduced** — the worked illustration
  in the notes (Scenario 1, goat and bear cub) is invented for this site.
- **Source:** standard doctrine; ICAI SM P2 Ch 14 scope. Written from memory

## Golden rule

- **Conventional formulation:** the grammatical and ordinary sense of the words
  is to be adhered to unless it leads to some absurdity, repugnance or
  inconsistency with the rest of the instrument, in which case the grammatical
  and ordinary sense may be modified so as to avoid that absurdity or
  inconsistency — **but no further**. Narrow application (choosing between two
  meanings a word already bears) and wider application (modifying the single
  ordinary meaning where the literal result is repugnant to the purpose of the
  Act) are both relied on.
- **Case anchors (named as anchors only):** *Grey v Pearson* is the classic
  anchor for the formulation; the murderer-inheriting line (conventionally
  anchored to *Re Sigsworth*) is the standard illustration of the wider
  application. **Nothing from either is quoted**; the illustration in the notes
  (Scenario 2, "issue" under a model Succession (Regulation) Act) is invented.
- **Reviewer note:** the load-bearing point for marks is the **extent of the

## Mischief rule — the rule in Heydon's case

- **Conventional formulation:** four matters are to be discerned and
  considered — (i) what was the common law (the prior law) before the making of
  the Act; (ii) what was the mischief and defect for which the prior law did not
  provide; (iii) what remedy the legislature has resolved and appointed; and
  (iv) the true reason of the remedy. The court then makes such construction as
  will **suppress the mischief and advance the remedy**, and suppress subtle
  inventions and evasions for the continuance of the mischief.
- **Case anchor (named as an anchor only):** **Heydon's case**. The four
  questions are stated above **in this chapter's own paraphrase**; the
  sixteenth-century wording of the resolution is **not reproduced**, and no
  later judgment applying the rule is quoted. The worked illustration
  (Scenario 3, a model Rent Security Act 2024 and constructive eviction) is
  invented for this site.
- **Limits relied on:** no insertion of words; the reading must be one the
  language can bear; the rule cannot overturn a clear provision; it has little
  force in penal and taxing statutes; and the mischief must come from
  legitimate sources rather than from judicial policy preference.
- **Used in:** q-i2c14-010, q-i2c14-011, q-i2c14-012, q-i2c14-016,
  d-i2c14-02 (all four points), notes §5 and Scenario 3, §7 comparison table,
  §8 table, common-mistakes item 5, one-page revision summary.
- **Spot-checked by:** _(blank)_

## Harmonious construction

- **Conventional formulation:** a statute must be read as a whole; where two
  provisions of the same statute appear to conflict, they must be so construed
  that effect is given to both, and neither is reduced to a **dead letter** or
  rendered otiose. Order of resolution relied on: reconcile by confining each
  provision to its own field; failing that, the **special** provision governs
  the facts it specifically addresses; a **non-obstante** clause overrides to
  the extent of the inconsistency; and, as a counsel of despair within one Act,
  the later provision may be preferred.
- **Case anchors:** none named in the notes. The doctrine is stated
  generically, and the illustration (Scenario 4, sections 20 and 44 of a model
  Co-operative Societies Act, and the federal-society delegate vote) is
  invented for this site. **Reviewer note:** the SM commonly illustrates this
  rule from constitutional-law material; nothing of that kind is used here.
- **Used in:** q-i2c14-013, q-i2c14-014, cs-i2c14-01 (sub c), notes §6 and
  Scenario 4, §8 table, common-mistakes item 6, one-page revision summary.

## Purposive construction and its relationship to the mischief rule

- **Formulation relied on (own words):** a provision is read in the light of the
  purpose or object the legislature sought to achieve, so as to serve rather
  than frustrate it. Purposive construction is presented as the **wider, modern
  successor** of the mischief rule, differing in three respects — focus (object
  rather than defect), no requirement of a prior law, and a wider range of
  materials. Limit relied on: purpose cannot displace clear words, the court may
  choose only among meanings the words can bear, and the purpose must be drawn
  from the statute.
- **Case anchors:** none named; the discussion is generic and doctrinal.
- **Reviewer note:** editions vary in whether purposive construction is
  presented as a **separate primary rule** or as a gloss on the mischief rule.
  q-i2c14-015 is drafted so that the answer is correct on either presentation,
  but if the SM treats them as identical, confirm the three-difference table in

## Noscitur a sociis

- **Conventional formulation:** *noscitur a sociis* — a word is known by the
  company it keeps; the meaning of a doubtful word is gathered from the words
  associated with it, associated words being presumed to be used in a related
  sense. It applies to any string of associated words and requires no
  general-words-after-specific-words structure. Ejusdem generis is a
  **specialised application** of it.
- **Illustration relied on:** the "place of public resort, dance hall, cinema,
  theatre or circus" style of list, and whether a library falls within it. This
  formulation is a textbook staple; the wording used in the notes is this
  chapter's own, and the market-shed and handcart variants are invented.

## Ejusdem generis

- **Conventional formulation:** where general words follow an enumeration of
  specific words, the general words are confined to things of the **same genus**
  as the specific words preceding them. **Four conditions** relied on:
  (i) an enumeration of specific words; (ii) the subjects of the enumeration
  constitute a genus or category; (iii) the genus is not exhausted by the
  enumeration; and (iv) the general words follow the specific words, with no
  contrary legislative intention. **Non-application** relied on: no genus;
  genus exhausted; contrary intention ("whatsoever", "whether or not of the same
  kind as the foregoing", or a definition of the general expression); general
  words preceding rather than following the enumeration; and where the
  restriction would defeat the object of a remedial provision.
- **Case anchors:** none named in the notes. All worked material is invented —
  the market-fee clause in the chapter opening and Scenario 5, and the
  Municipal Markets Act 2024 in cs-i2c14-01.
- **Reviewer note:** the **number and grouping of the conditions** varies between
  textbooks (some state three, some five, by splitting or merging the
  contrary-intention limb). **q-i2c14-017 and d-i2c14-03 award marks on the
  four-condition statement above** — please align it with the edition notified
  for the attempt before initialling.
- **Used in:** q-i2c14-017, q-i2c14-018, q-i2c14-019, q-i2c14-020,
  q-i2c14-022, cs-i2c14-01 (subs a and b), d-i2c14-03 (points 2–4), notes §9,

## Expressio unius est exclusio alterius

- **Conventional formulation:** the express mention of one thing implies the
  exclusion of another. Relied on **as a maxim of inference, not a rule of
  law**, and stated with its three standard defeaters — an enumeration that is
  plainly illustrative ("such as", "including"); an omission explicable as an
  oversight; and a mention made *ex abundanti cautela*, out of abundant caution.
- **Case anchors:** none named; the illustration (an appeal provision in a model

## Ut res magis valeat quam pereat

- **Conventional formulation:** let the thing have effect rather than perish — a
  construction that makes the provision effective and workable is preferred to
  one that renders it useless, nugatory or unconstitutional. Corollaries relied
  on: courts lean against a reading that makes a section unworkable in practice,
  and against a reading that renders part of a statute redundant, every word
  being presumed to have been used for a purpose (surplusage is not to be
  presumed).
- **Links relied on:** this maxim supports **reading down** under the
  presumption of constitutionality (§11) and supplies the reason why an
  exhausted genus defeats ejusdem generis (§9).
- **Used in:** q-i2c14-024, q-i2c14-028 (explanation of the reading-down limb),
  cs-i2c14-01 (sub b, distractor B), notes §10 and Scenario 6 (point b),
  one-page revision summary.

## Generalia specialibus non derogant

- **Conventional formulation:** general provisions do not derogate from special
  ones; where a general and a special provision cover the same ground, the
  **special prevails** on the matter it specifically addresses and the general
  governs the rest. Relied on as operating **both within one statute** (as part
  of harmonious construction) **and between two statutes**, where it is
  reinforced by the presumption against implied repeal: a later general Act is
  not presumed to repeal an earlier special Act by implication.
- **Case anchors:** none named; the illustration (a later general Licensing Act
  against an earlier special market-trader provision) is invented.
- **Used in:** q-i2c14-014, q-i2c14-025, cs-i2c14-01 (sub c), notes §6, §10 and
  Scenario 6 (point d), one-page revision summary.

## Contemporanea expositio

- **Conventional formulation:** *contemporanea expositio est optima et
  fortissima in lege* — contemporaneous exposition is the best and strongest in
  law. The meaning given to a statute by those who administered it at or near
  the time of its enactment, and the long usage that followed, is strong
  evidence of its meaning. **Three limits relied on:** it applies mainly to
  **ancient or old statutes**; it yields at once to clear words; and it has
  little or no application to a modern statute the court can read for itself.
  Departmental circulars and settled administrative practice are its usual
  vehicles and do not bind courts.
- **Case anchors:** none named.
- **Used in:** q-i2c14-026, cs-i2c14-01 (sub d, distractor D), notes §10 and
  §15 (usage and practice row), one-page revision summary.

## Strict construction — penal statutes

- **Conventional formulation:** a penal provision is construed strictly: an act
  is not an offence unless it falls clearly within the words; the definition of
  an offence is not extended by analogy or by considerations of policy; a
  genuine ambiguity is resolved **in favour of the accused**; and **mens rea** is
  presumed unless excluded. Counter-limit relied on: strict construction does
  not license a perverse or unnatural reading, and the severity of the penalty
  is no reason to narrow words that plainly cover the conduct.
- **Case anchors:** none named; the illustration (an offence of removing bulk
  fuel without a delivery note) is invented.
- **Used in:** q-i2c14-031, cs-i2c14-02 (sub d), notes §12, one-page revision
  summary.

## Strict construction — taxing statutes

- **Conventional formulation:** in a taxing statute one looks simply at what is
  clearly said; there is **no room for any intendment**, **no equity about a
  tax**, and nothing is to be read in or implied. The four-part breakdown relied
  on: a **charging** provision is construed strictly and the subject not caught
  by the letter goes free; an **exemption or deduction** is construed strictly
  with the burden on the **assessee**, but is given full effect once he is shown
  to be within it; **machinery and procedural** provisions are construed
  **liberally** so as to make the charge workable; and while tax planning within
  the law is legitimate, a colourable device is not, and anti-avoidance
  provisions are read purposively.
- **Case anchors:** the "no equity about a tax" formulation is conventionally
  anchored to *Cape Brandy Syndicate v IRC*; it is **paraphrased here, not
  quoted**, and the case is not named in the notes or the bank. The Bulk Fuel
  Cess Act 2024 used in cs-i2c14-02 is invented.
- **Reviewer note:** the **machinery-liberal** limb is the half most often
  omitted from student answers and is examined directly in q-i2c14-032 and
  cs-i2c14-02-c. Confirm the SM states it.
- **Used in:** q-i2c14-032, cs-i2c14-02 (subs a, b and c), notes §12,

## Liberal construction — beneficial and welfare legislation; remedial versus penal

- **Conventional formulation:** where a statute is passed for the benefit of a
  class and a provision is **capable of two constructions**, the construction
  more favourable to the class protected is adopted, so that the benefit is not
  defeated by a narrow reading. **Two limits relied on:** the approach operates
  only where two constructions are genuinely open, so it cannot rewrite an
  eligibility condition; and a **penal provision inside a welfare Act** is still
  construed strictly. **Remedial versus penal** relied on: a remedial provision
  gives an individual a remedy for a wrong; a penal provision punishes a public
  wrong; the same Act commonly contains both, each carrying its own canon; and
  where doubtful, ask who receives the sanction.
- **Case anchors:** none named.
- **Reviewer note:** the qualification "**capable of two constructions**" is
  load-bearing for q-i2c14-033, whose distractors are all versions of the same
  overstatement. Confirm the SM carries the qualification.
- **Used in:** q-i2c14-033, q-i2c14-016 (welfare framing), notes §12,
  common-mistakes item 11, one-page revision summary.

## Mandatory and directory provisions

- **Conventional formulation:** a mandatory (imperative) provision must be
  complied with exactly and non-compliance invalidates the act; a directory
  provision requires substantial compliance only and non-compliance leaves the
  act valid, though another consequence may follow. **"Shall" and "may" are not
  conclusive** — the court decides from the object and scheme of the statute.
  **Tests relied on:** the object of the provision; whether the statute states
  the consequence (a declaration of voidness → mandatory; a penalty alone →
  usually directory); prejudice to a person the provision protects; serious
  general inconvenience where the default is that of a public officer; public
  duty versus an individual right or safeguard; and negative or prohibitory
  language as a strong indicator of a mandatory provision.
- **Case anchors:** none named; all three worked provisions (Scenario 7, a model
  Municipal Assessment Act) and the section 19 notice provision in cs-i2c14-03
  are invented.
- **Reviewer note:** SM editions differ in how many tests they enumerate and in
  whether they present the "consequence stated by the statute" test as separate
  from the "object" test. **d-i2c14-06 awards 2.5 marks on the four-test
  statement** in the skeleton; align it with the notified edition.
- **Used in:** q-i2c14-034, q-i2c14-035, cs-i2c14-03 (sub d), d-i2c14-06 (all
  points), notes §13 and Scenario 7, common-mistakes item 1, one-page revision
  summary.

## Internal aids to interpretation

- **Formulations relied on (own words), with the weight attached to each:**
  **long title** — part of the Act, a guide to general scope and object;
  **short title** — identification only; **preamble** — part of the Act, key to
  object and policy, never against plain words; **headings** — usable on
  ambiguity, cannot control a section; **marginal notes** — weakest aid,
  generally no part of the Act, with marginal notes in the Constitution as the
  recognised exception; **definition clause** — binding, subject to "unless the
  context otherwise requires", with **means** (restrictive/exhaustive),
  **includes** (extensive/illustrative), **means and includes** (exhaustive) and
  **is deemed to be** (legal fiction, for the stated purpose only);
  **proviso** — carves an exception out of the provision to which it is
  attached, is not to be read as an independent enactment and cannot enlarge the
  section; **Explanation** — clarifies, may widen or narrow, does not stand
  apart from its section; **illustrations** — show intended working, cannot
  modify or extend, and the section prevails on conflict; **Schedules** — part
  of the Act, but the enacting section prevails on conflict; **non-obstante
  clause** — overrides to the extent of the inconsistency, with "**subject
  to**" as its mirror; **punctuation** — minor value, cannot control meaning;
  **saving clause** — preserves existing rights, pending proceedings or an
  earlier law.
- **Statutes mentioned only as carriers of a drafting device:** the Indian
  Contract Act 1872, the Indian Evidence Act and the Indian Penal Code are named
  in one line of notes §14 as Acts that carry statutory **illustrations**. **No
  provision of any of them is stated or relied on**, and no bank question turns
  on their contents.
- **Case anchors:** none named. The Heritage Buildings (Protection) Act 2024
  used in cs-i2c14-03 is invented, including its preamble, proviso, Explanation,
  marginal note and Schedule.
- **Reviewer note:** the **marginal-note exception for the Constitution** and
  the **Schedule-versus-section conflict rule** are the two positions most
  likely to be stated differently between editions. q-i2c14-036 avoids both by
  testing the preamble; cs-i2c14-03-c mentions the constitutional exception only
  inside an option explanation, not in a stem.
- **Used in:** q-i2c14-036, q-i2c14-037, cs-i2c14-03 (subs a, b and c),
  d-i2c14-04 (all six points), notes §14 with its means/includes table and both
  callouts, common-mistakes items 7, 8, 9 and 13, one-page revision summary.

## External aids to interpretation

- **Formulations relied on (own words), with limits:** **dictionaries** — give
  all possible meanings, context selects, never over a statutory definition or a
  trade meaning; **textbooks and commentaries** — persuasive only; **historical
  setting** — establishes the mischief, cannot displace clear words;
  **Statement of Objects and Reasons** — admissible for background, mischief and
  purpose, **not** to construe the section, because the Bill may have been
  amended in passage; **committee, commission and Law Commission reports** —
  evidence of the mischief, not of the meaning of the enacted words;
  **parliamentary debates** — limited use, the mover's speech for object and
  mischief only, individual opinions not decisive; **statutes in pari materia**
  — read together as one system, but a definition in one Act does not
  automatically govern another; **later statutes** — may show the legislature's
  understanding, and a declaratory or amending Act may settle the point, but
  otherwise cannot change the meaning the earlier Act bore; **foreign
  decisions** — persuasive, weight depending on similarity of language and
  social conditions; **usage and practice** — strong for old statutes under
  contemporanea expositio, weak for modern ones; **departmental circulars** —
  not binding, cannot add to or subtract from the statute.
- **Governing limit relied on:** aids are admitted to resolve ambiguity and **no
  aid may be used against the plain and unambiguous words of the statute**; the
  order of operations is plain words, then internal aids, then external
  material.
- **Case anchors:** none named. The Indian position on the admissibility of
