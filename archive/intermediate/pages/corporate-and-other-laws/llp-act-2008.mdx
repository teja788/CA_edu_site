Two chartered accountants, Nandita and Ashwin, have run a consultancy firm in Pune for eleven years.
Every client engagement is signed by whoever is free that week; every professional-indemnity claim
lands on both of them without limit; and the firm cannot own the office it works from, because a
partnership firm is not a person. On 1 April 2026 they convert the firm into **Nandita Ashwin LLP**.

Almost everything about how they work together stays the same — they still decide by agreement, still
share profits as they choose, still pay no separate tax on the entity's distribution. But four things
change on the day the Registrar signs the certificate. The LLP now **owns** the office in its own
name. Ashwin is **no longer personally liable** for a negligent report Nandita signs. Somebody must be
a **designated partner** and answer to the Registrar for every filing. And a set of **dates** now
exists — Form 11 by 30 May, Form 8 by 30 October — that never existed for the firm.

That is the whole chapter: an entity with the **internal freedom of a partnership** and the
**external discipline of a body corporate**. For the exam it is a **definitions-and-clocks chapter**.
Marks come from the bright lines — two partners, two designated partners, one resident for **120 days
in the financial year**, ₹25 lakh and ₹40 lakh, 30 days, 60 days, six months — and from knowing
**which document is silent** before you reach for the First Schedule.

## 1. Nature of an LLP — sections 3 and 4

**Section 3(1)** — an LLP is a **body corporate** formed and incorporated under this Act, and is a
**legal entity separate from its partners**. **Section 3(2)** — it has **perpetual succession**.
**Section 3(3)** — **any change in the partners does not affect the existence, rights or liabilities
of the LLP**.

Read those three sub-sections together and every practical consequence follows. The LLP contracts in
its own name, sues and is sued in its own name (s. 14), owns property in its own name, and survives
the death, retirement or insolvency of every one of its partners. A partnership firm has none of
that: it is a *compendious name* for the partners, and a change in the partners is a change in the
firm.

**Section 4** removes the obvious cross-reference: **the provisions of the Indian Partnership Act
1932 do not apply to an LLP**, save as otherwise provided. So no implied authority under s. 19 of the
1932 Act, no s. 25 joint-and-several liability of partners, no registration under Chapter VII of that
Act. An examiner who asks "can an LLP partner bind a co-partner personally?" is testing s. 4 plus
s. 26, and the answer is no.

**The business.** Section 11(1) allows **two or more persons associated for carrying on a lawful
business with a view to profit** to subscribe to an incorporation document. Two limbs matter:
**lawful business** (s. 2(1)(e) defines "business" to include every trade, profession, service and
occupation) and **with a view to profit** — so a not-for-profit association cannot be an LLP.

<div class="formula">Body corporate · separate legal entity · perpetual succession · change in partners is irrelevant to the LLP · Indian Partnership Act 1932 does NOT apply</div>

**The small LLP — s. 2(1)(ta), inserted in 2021.** A small LLP is one in respect of which —

| Limb | Ceiling | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Contribution** of partners | does not exceed **₹25 lakh** | or such higher amount as may be prescribed, **not exceeding ₹5 crore** |
| **Turnover** as per the Statement of Account and Solvency for the **immediately preceding financial year** | does not exceed **₹40 lakh** | or such higher amount as may be prescribed, **not exceeding ₹50 crore** |

The two limbs are joined by **and** — a small LLP must be inside **both** ceilings. Small LLPs get
lower fees and a lighter penalty regime; nothing else about the Act changes for them.

<Callout kind="amendment" label="AMENDMENT-CHECK · the small-LLP ceilings are designed to move">

    with statutory outer limits of <strong>₹5 crore</strong> (contribution) and <strong>₹50 crore</strong>
    (turnover). The operative ceilings of <strong>₹25 lakh and ₹40 lakh</strong> are therefore
    <strong>notification-level</strong> and can be raised without amending the Act. Learn the
    <em>structure</em> (contribution limb <strong>and</strong> turnover limb, turnover taken from the
    <strong>preceding</strong> financial year) and re-verify the figures.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. LLP compared with a partnership and a company

This table earns marks on its own and settles half the MCQs in the chapter.

| Feature | Partnership firm (1932 Act) | LLP (2008 Act) | Company (2013 Act) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Governing law | Indian Partnership Act 1932 | LLP Act 2008; **1932 Act excluded by s. 4** | Companies Act 2013 |
| Legal status | **not** a separate legal entity | **body corporate**, separate legal entity (s. 3) | body corporate, separate legal entity |
| Registration | optional (but s. 69 disabilities follow) | **compulsory** — it exists only on registration | **compulsory** |
| Perpetual succession | no — a change in partners changes the firm | **yes** (s. 3(2)–(3)) | yes |
| Minimum members | 2 | **2 partners** (s. 6) | 2 private / 7 public / 1 OPC |
| Maximum members | 50 (Companies Act rules) | **no maximum** | 200 private / no limit public |
| Liability of a member | **unlimited**, joint and several | **limited to contribution**, subject to ss. 28(2) and 30 | limited to unpaid amount on shares |
| Who binds the entity | every partner is agent of the firm **and of the other partners** | every partner is agent **of the LLP but not of other partners** (s. 26) | Board / authorised officers |
| Internal governance | partnership deed | **LLP agreement**; First Schedule where silent (s. 23) | Act, memorandum and articles — far less freedom |
| Property | held by partners jointly | held **by the LLP** | held by the company |
| Audit | not required by the 1932 Act | **threshold-based** (Rule 24) | **compulsory** for every company |
| Annual filing with a public registry | none | **Form 8 and Form 11** | financial statements and annual return |
| Conversion out / merger | not available | possible under the Act | possible |

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>The one-line answer to "why an LLP".</strong> It carries the
    <strong>organisational flexibility of a partnership</strong> — mutual rights fixed by agreement,
    no board, no statutory meetings, no share capital — with the <strong>limited liability and
    separate personality of a company</strong>. The price is <strong>public filing</strong>: the
    incorporation document, the partners, the Statement of Account and Solvency and the annual return
    are all open to inspection under s. 36. Say that sentence and then justify it with s. 3, s. 23
    and s. 28.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. Partners — sections 5, 6 and 22

**Section 22** — the persons who **subscribed their names to the incorporation document** become the
partners on incorporation; anybody else becomes a partner **in accordance with the LLP agreement**.

**Section 5 — who may be a partner.** Any **individual** or **body corporate** may be a partner. So a
company, another LLP or a foreign body corporate may be a partner; a **partnership firm cannot**,
because it is not a body corporate. An **individual** is **not capable** of becoming a partner if —

1. he has been found to be of **unsound mind** by a Court of competent jurisdiction and the finding
   **is in force**;
2. he is an **undischarged insolvent**; or
3. he has **applied to be adjudicated as an insolvent and his application is pending**.

Three traps. The unsoundness must be a **court finding still in force** — a doctor's certificate is
not enough, and a person since restored to sanity is not disqualified. A **discharged** insolvent is
**not** disqualified. And the third limb catches a person **before** any adjudication, merely because
his own application is pending. The disqualifications apply **only to individuals** — a body corporate
in liquidation is not caught by s. 5.

**Section 6 — minimum number.** Every LLP shall have **at least two partners**. There is **no maximum**.

**Section 6(2)** — where the number falls **below two** and the LLP **carries on business for more
than six months** while so reduced, the person who is **the only partner** during the time it so
carries on business after those six months, **and who has knowledge of the fact** that it is carrying
on business with him alone, is **personally liable for the obligations of the LLP incurred during
that period**.

<div class="formula">Sole partner + business continued beyond 6 months + knowledge → personal liability for obligations incurred in that continued period. The LLP itself does NOT dissolve.</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Three things s. 6(2) does not say.</strong> It does <strong>not</strong> dissolve the LLP —
    the body corporate survives; falling below two for more than six months is merely a
    <strong>ground</strong> on which the Tribunal <em>may</em> order winding up under s. 64.
    It does <strong>not</strong> reach obligations incurred <em>before</em> the six months expire.
    And it does <strong>not</strong> reach a sole partner who is genuinely unaware — <strong>knowledge
    is an element</strong>, exactly as cognisance is under s. 3A of the Companies Act 2013. Compare
    the two provisions in an answer and you look like you understand both.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 1 — the sole partner clock">
  <p>
    Anantha Consultants LLP has two partners. On <strong>14 May 2026</strong> one of them retires,
    leaving Vedika as the only partner. Knowing this, Vedika continues the business. On
    <strong>3 October 2026</strong> the LLP takes a ₹9,00,000 trade credit; on <strong>20 January
    2027</strong> it takes another of ₹15,00,000.
  </p>
  <p>
    The six months run from 14 May 2026 and expire on <strong>14 November 2026</strong>. The
    ₹9,00,000 credit was incurred <em>inside</em> the grace and is the LLP's obligation alone —
    Vedika's shield holds. The ₹15,00,000 credit was incurred <em>after</em> the grace, while she was
    still the only partner and knew it, so she is <strong>personally liable for that ₹15,00,000</strong>.
    Had she inducted a second partner on 10 November 2026, s. 6(2) would never have engaged at all.
    Note what is <em>not</em> a consequence: the LLP is not dissolved and the earlier credit does not
    become hers.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Designated partners — sections 7 to 10

Designated partners are the **compliance face** of an LLP. Every LLP has partners; only some of them
carry the statutory duty to file, and the personal exposure that goes with it.

**Section 7(1)** — every LLP shall have **at least two designated partners who are individuals**, and
**at least one of them shall be a resident in India**. Where **all** the partners are bodies
corporate, or where the partners are a mixture of individuals and bodies corporate, **at least two
individuals who are partners, or nominees of those bodies corporate, shall act as designated
partners**.

**The residence test — the single most amendment-sensitive line in this chapter.** The Explanation to
s. 7 defines "**resident in India**" as a person who has **stayed in India for not less than 120 days
during the financial year**.

<div class="formula">Designated partners: at least TWO · both INDIVIDUALS · at least ONE resident = stay of ≥ 120 days in the FINANCIAL YEAR (1 April to 31 March)</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>The residence test is 120 days in the financial year — not 182 days in the preceding
    year.</strong> Before the 2021 Amendment the Explanation read "not less than
    <strong>182 days during the immediately preceding one year</strong>". The Amendment changed
    <em>both</em> halves: the <strong>number of days</strong> (182 → 120) and the
    <strong>reference period</strong> (preceding one year → <strong>the financial year</strong>).
    Older material, and any option offering 182 days or "the preceding twelve months", is stale.
    Do not confuse this with the resident-director test under s. 149(3) of the Companies Act 2013,
    which is <strong>182 days in the financial year</strong>.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 7(2)–(3) — who they are.** If the **incorporation document specifies** who is to be a
designated partner, those persons are the designated partners. If it states that **each of the
partners from time to time** is a designated partner, then every partner is one. A partner may
become or cease to be a designated partner **in accordance with the LLP agreement**.

**Section 7(4) — consent.** An individual shall **not** become a designated partner unless he has
given his **prior consent** to act as such, to the LLP, in the prescribed form and manner.
**Section 7(5)** — the LLP shall **file the particulars** of every individual who has given that
consent **with the Registrar within 30 days of his appointment**.

**Section 7(6) — DPIN.** Every designated partner shall obtain a **Designated Partner Identification
Number (DPIN)** from the Central Government, and **ss. 153 to 159 of the Companies Act 2013 apply
mutatis mutandis**. In practice the DIN and DPIN systems are unified, so a person already holding a
DIN uses it.

**Section 8 — liabilities of designated partners.** Unless expressly provided otherwise, a designated
partner is —

- **responsible** for doing all acts, matters and things required to be done by the LLP for
  compliance with the Act, **including filing every document, return and statement**, and such other
  things as are specified in the **LLP agreement**; and
- **liable to all penalties imposed on the LLP** for any contravention of those provisions.

**Section 9 — filling a vacancy.** An LLP may appoint a designated partner **within 30 days of a
vacancy arising for any reason**, and the consent and filing requirements of s. 7(4)–(5) apply. The
proviso is the trap: if **no designated partner is appointed**, or if at any time there is **only
one** designated partner, **each partner is deemed to be a designated partner** — with the s. 8
liabilities that follow.

**Section 10 — punishment.** Contravention of s. 7(1) (the two-designated-partner requirement) makes
the **LLP and every partner** liable to a penalty; contravention of s. 7(4)–(5), s. 8 or s. 9 makes
the **LLP and every designated partner** liable. Since 2021 these are **penalties adjudicated by the
Registrar** under s. 68A, not fines imposed by a court.

<Callout kind="amendment" label="AMENDMENT-CHECK · fine became penalty, and the rupee figures moved">
  <p>
    The 2021 Amendment <strong>decriminalised</strong> most compliance defaults across the Act:
    ss. 10, 13(4), 25(4)–(5), 34(5) and 35(2) now speak of a <strong>penalty</strong> on the LLP and
    its partners or designated partners, adjudicated by an <strong>adjudicating officer (the
    Registrar)</strong> under <strong>s. 68A</strong>, with an appeal to the <strong>Regional
    Director within 60 days</strong>. Small LLPs and start-up LLPs pay a <strong>reduced</strong>
    penalty. The <strong>rupee figures and daily rates</strong> have been recast and are not
    examined in this chapter's bank — learn <em>who</em> is liable and <em>who</em> adjudicates, and
    verify any figure before quoting it.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 2 — designated partners, residence and a vacancy">
  <p>
    Meghdoot Infratech LLP has four partners: two individuals (Rahul and Sanya) and two bodies
    corporate. The incorporation document names Rahul and Sanya as designated partners. During
    FY 2026-27 Rahul is in India for <strong>96 days</strong> and Sanya for <strong>41 days</strong>.
    Sanya resigns as a designated partner on <strong>5 October 2026</strong>.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>1 · The residence limb fails.</strong> At least one designated partner must have stayed in
    India for <strong>120 days in the financial year</strong>. Rahul is <strong>24 days short</strong>
    and Sanya is nowhere near. The LLP contravenes s. 7(1) from the moment the financial year makes
    the shortfall certain, and the LLP <em>and every partner</em> face a penalty under s. 10.
    <strong>2 · The vacancy.</strong> Section 9 allows <strong>30 days</strong> from 5 October 2026 —
    so a replacement must be appointed by <strong>4 November 2026</strong>, with prior consent under
    s. 7(4) and the particulars filed under s. 7(5). <strong>3 · The default position.</strong> While
    only Rahul remains, the LLP has <em>one</em> designated partner, so under the proviso to s. 9
    <strong>every partner — including both bodies corporate — is deemed to be a designated
    partner</strong> and carries the s. 8 liabilities. <strong>4 · What does not happen.</strong> The
    LLP is not dissolved and its contracts are not void.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Incorporation — sections 11, 12 and 14

**Section 11(1)** — **two or more persons associated for carrying on a lawful business with a view to
profit** subscribe their names to an **incorporation document**, which is filed with the **Registrar
of the State in which the registered office is to be situated**, together with —

- a **statement in the prescribed form** made by an **advocate**, a **Chartered Accountant**, a
  **Cost Accountant** or a **Company Secretary** who is **engaged in the formation** of the LLP,
  **and** by **anyone who subscribed** his name to the incorporation document, that **all the
  requirements of the Act and the rules in respect of incorporation and matters precedent and
  incidental thereto have been complied with**.

**Section 11(2)** — the incorporation document must be in the prescribed form (filed as **FiLLiP**),
and must state the **name** of the LLP, the **proposed business**, the **address of the registered
office**, the **name and address of each person who is to be a partner** on incorporation, the **name
and address of the persons who are to be designated partners**, and such other information as is
prescribed.

**Section 11(3)** — a person who makes the statement of compliance **knowing it to be false**, or
**not believing it to be true**, is punishable with **imprisonment up to two years and with fine of
not less than ₹10,000 up to ₹5,00,000**. Note that s. 11(3) has **not** been decriminalised: a false
statement of compliance is one of the few surviving imprisonment offences in the Act.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Two signatures, not one.</strong> The statement of compliance under s. 11(1)(c) needs a
    <strong>professional engaged in the formation</strong> <em>and</em> a <strong>subscriber to the
    incorporation document</strong>. Options offering "an advocate alone", "any practising chartered
    accountant" (the professional must be <em>engaged in the formation</em>) or "a designated partner
    alone" are all wrong. Compare s. 7(1)(b) of the Companies Act 2013, which uses the same
    two-signature architecture.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 12 — registration.** The Registrar **registers** the incorporation document and issues a
**certificate that the LLP is incorporated by the name specified**. The Registrar may **accept the
statement of compliance as sufficient evidence** of compliance. The certificate is **signed by the
Registrar and authenticated by his official seal**, and is **conclusive evidence** that the LLP is
incorporated by the name specified in it.

**Section 14 — effect of registration.** On registration the LLP is, **by its name**, capable of —

1. **suing and being sued**;
2. **acquiring, owning, holding and developing or disposing of property**, movable or immovable,
   tangible or intangible;
3. **having a common seal, if it decides to have one**; and
4. doing and suffering such other acts and things as **bodies corporate may lawfully do and suffer**.

The common seal is **optional**, and the list in s. 14 is the practical content of the separate
personality declared by s. 3.

## 6. Registered office and the name of an LLP — sections 13 and 15 to 17

**Section 13(1)** — every LLP shall have a **registered office** to which all communications and
notices may be addressed and where they shall be received. **13(2)** — a document may be served on an
LLP, or on a partner or designated partner, by **post under a certificate of posting**, by
**registered post**, or in any other prescribed manner, at the **registered office** and at any other
address specifically declared by the LLP for the purpose. **13(3)** — an LLP may change the place of
its registered office and **file notice of the change with the Registrar**; the change **takes effect
only upon that filing**. **13(4)** — contravention makes the **LLP and every partner** liable to a
**daily penalty** subject to a maximum (rules- and amendment-sensitive).

**Section 15 — the name.**

- **15(1)** — every LLP shall have either the words "**limited liability partnership**" or the
  acronym "**LLP**" as the **last words of its name**.
- **15(2)** — no LLP shall be registered by a name which in the opinion of the **Central Government**
  is **(a) undesirable**, or **(b) identical with or too nearly resembling** the name of any **other
  partnership firm, LLP or body corporate**, or a **registered trade mark**, or a trade mark that is
  the **subject matter of a pending application** under the Trade Marks Act 1999.

Note how much wider the s. 15(2)(b) comparison set is than the Companies Act equivalent: it reaches
**partnership firms** and **pending trade-mark applications** as well as companies and registered
marks.

**Section 16 — reservation.** A person may apply to the Registrar to reserve a name **for a proposed
LLP** or as **the name to which an existing LLP proposes to change**. If satisfied that the name is
not liable to rejection on any s. 15(2) ground, the Registrar **reserves it for three months from the
date of intimation** by the Registrar.

**Section 17 — rectification of name (substituted by the 2021 Amendment).** Where, through
inadvertence or otherwise, an LLP is registered — on **first registration** or on **registration by a
new name** — with a name **identical with or too nearly resembling**:

- **(a)** that of **any other LLP or a company**; or
- **(b)** a **registered trade mark** of a proprietor under the Trade Marks Act 1999, as is likely to
  be mistaken for it,

then, **on an application of that LLP or of the proprietor**, the **Central Government may direct**
the LLP to change its name **within three months of the date of the direction**. A **trade-mark
proprietor's** application is maintainable only **within three years** of the incorporation,
registration or change of name of the LLP.

**Section 17(2)** — on changing its name, the LLP shall **within 15 days** give notice of the change
to the **Registrar** along with the Central Government's order; the Registrar carries out the
consequential changes in the certificate of incorporation, and **within 30 days of that change in the
certificate the LLP shall change its LLP agreement**.

**Section 17(3)** — on default in complying with the direction, the **Central Government allots a new
name** in the prescribed manner; the Registrar **enters the new name** in place of the old and issues
a **fresh certificate of incorporation** with it, which the LLP must thereafter use. The proviso
preserves the LLP's freedom to change that allotted name later under s. 16.

<div class="formula">s. 17 clocks: direction → change within 3 MONTHS · change → notice to Registrar within 15 DAYS · certificate changed → LLP agreement amended within 30 DAYS · default → Central Government ALLOTS a name</div>

<Callout kind="amendment" label="AMENDMENT-CHECK · s. 17 was rewritten, and the approver matters">
  <p>
    The pre-2021 s. 17 gave the aggrieved <strong>entity or proprietor 24 months</strong> to apply and
    allowed the Central Government to direct a change within <strong>three months</strong>, with a
    <em>fine</em> for default. The substituted section keeps the three months but adds the
    <strong>three-year</strong> limit for a trade-mark proprietor and replaces the fine with
    <strong>allotment of a name by the Central Government</strong> — the same architecture as
    s. 16(3) of the Companies Act 2013. The <strong>power is exercised by the Central Government</strong>;
    in practice it stands <strong>delegated to the Regional Director</strong>, and an appeal against
    an adjudicated penalty lies to the Regional Director under s. 68A. Confirm the delegation and the
    prescribed format of the allotted name (rules-level) before relying on either.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. The LLP agreement and the First Schedule — section 23

**Section 23(1)** — save as otherwise provided by the Act, the **mutual rights and duties of the
partners**, and of the **LLP and its partners**, are governed by the **LLP agreement**.
**23(2)** — the agreement **and any changes in it** shall be **filed with the Registrar** in the
prescribed form. **23(3)** — an agreement **made before incorporation** between the subscribers may
impose obligations on the LLP, **provided it is ratified by all the partners after incorporation** —
the LLP's answer to the pre-incorporation contract problem.

**23(4) — the fallback.** **In the absence of agreement as to any matter**, the mutual rights and
duties are determined by the provisions **relating to that matter** in the **First Schedule**.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>The First Schedule is matter-by-matter, not all-or-nothing.</strong> Section 23(4) applies
    "in the absence of agreement <em>as to any matter</em>". So an LLP agreement that fixes profit
    shares at 60:40 but says nothing about admitting a new partner takes <strong>its own</strong>
    profit ratio and the <strong>First Schedule's</strong> unanimity rule for admission. The Schedule
    fills <em>gaps</em>; it never overrides a term the partners have actually agreed. Options saying
    "the First Schedule applies only where there is no LLP agreement at all" are wrong.
  </p>
</Callout>

**First Schedule — the default rules worth memorising:**

| Matter | Default rule where the agreement is silent |
|---|---|
| Capital, profits and losses | all partners share **equally** — irrespective of what each contributed |
| Indemnity to a partner | the LLP **indemnifies** each partner for payments made and personal liabilities incurred in the **ordinary and proper conduct** of the business, or in anything **necessarily done to preserve** the business or property |
| Indemnity to the LLP | every partner **indemnifies the LLP** for any loss caused by **his fraud** in the conduct of the business |
| Management | **every partner may take part** in the management |
| Remuneration | **no partner is entitled to remuneration** for acting in the business or management |
| Admission of a new partner | **no person may be introduced as a partner without the consent of all existing partners** |
| Ordinary decisions | by a **resolution passed by a majority in number** of the partners; **each partner has one vote** |
| Change in the nature of business | requires the **consent of all the partners** |
| Minutes | decisions to be **recorded in the minutes within 30 days** and kept at the **registered office** |
| Information | each partner shall render **true accounts and full information** of all things affecting the LLP to any partner or his legal representatives |
| Competing business | a partner who carries on a **competing business** without consent must **account for and pay over** the profits |
| Personal benefit | a partner must **account for any benefit** derived without consent from a transaction concerning the LLP, or from use of its **property, name or business connection** |
| Expulsion | **no majority can expel a partner** unless a power to do so is conferred by **express agreement** |
| Disputes | referred to **arbitration** under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996 |

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>The four defaults that decide most questions.</strong> <strong>Equal</strong> shares
    regardless of contribution · <strong>no remuneration</strong> · <strong>unanimity</strong> to
    admit a partner and to change the nature of the business · <strong>majority in number, one vote
    each</strong> for everything else. A stem that gives you unequal contributions and then says the
    agreement is silent is testing the first of these, and the answer is <em>equal</em>, not
    <em>proportionate</em>.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 8. Cessation of partnership interest and registration of changes — sections 24 and 25

**Section 24(1) — resignation.** A person may cease to be a partner **in accordance with an agreement
with the other partners**, or, **in the absence of such agreement**, by giving a **notice in writing
of not less than 30 days to the other partners** of his intention to resign.

**Section 24(2) — automatic cessation.** A person **shall cease** to be a partner —

- **(a)** on his **death**, or on the **dissolution of the LLP**;
- **(b)** if he is **declared to be of unsound mind** by a competent court; or
- **(c)** if he has **applied to be adjudged an insolvent, or is declared insolvent**.

**Section 24(3) — the former partner and the outside world.** A former partner is still regarded, **in
relation to any person dealing with the LLP**, as a partner **unless** — (a) that person **has notice**
that he has ceased to be a partner; **or** (b) **notice of the cessation has been delivered to the
Registrar**. Either limb suffices: filing with the Registrar protects the former partner against the
whole world, individual notice protects him against that dealer.

**Section 24(4)** — cessation **does not by itself discharge** the former partner from any obligation
to the LLP, to the other partners or to any other person **incurred while he was a partner**.

**Section 24(5) — what he takes away.** Unless the LLP agreement provides otherwise, the former
partner — or the person entitled to his share **in consequence of his death or insolvency** — is
entitled to receive from the LLP **(a)** an amount **equal to the capital contribution actually made**,
and **(b)** his **right to share in the accumulated profits after deducting accumulated losses**,
determined **as at the date he ceased to be a partner**.

**Section 24(6)** — that person has **no right to interfere in the management** of the LLP. The estate
of a deceased partner is a **claimant**, not a partner.

<div class="formula">Cessation: agreement, else 30 DAYS written notice · death / unsound mind / insolvency are automatic · outsiders may still treat him as a partner until they have notice OR the Registrar has notice · past obligations survive</div>

**Section 25 — registration of changes.**

| Who | What | Clock |
|---|---|---|
| **Every partner** | inform the **LLP** of any change in his **name or address** | **within 15 days** of the change (s. 25(1)) |
| **The LLP** | file notice with the **Registrar** where a person **becomes or ceases** to be a partner | **within 30 days** of that date (s. 25(2)(a)) |
| **The LLP** | file notice with the **Registrar** of any change in a partner's **name or address** | **within 30 days** of the change (s. 25(2)(b)) |
| **A former partner** | may himself file the notice where he has reasonable cause to believe the LLP will not | on the LLP's failure (s. 25(6)) |

The notice must be **signed by a designated partner** and authenticated as prescribed, and where it
relates to an **incoming** partner it must contain that partner's **statement of consent**, signed by
him. Default makes the **LLP and every designated partner** liable to a penalty under s. 25(4), and a
defaulting **partner** liable under s. 25(5).

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 3 — a resignation, dated end to end">
  <p>
    Kalindi Retail LLP has five partners and an LLP agreement that is silent on resignation. On
    <strong>9 September 2026</strong> Ravi gives the other partners written notice of his intention to
    resign. On <strong>3 December 2026</strong> a separate change occurs: Ishaan is admitted as a new
    partner. A supplier who has dealt with the LLP for years, and who has heard nothing, extends
    ₹4,00,000 of credit on <strong>15 October 2026</strong> on the strength of Ravi's reputation.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>1 · When does Ravi cease?</strong> The agreement being silent, s. 24(1) requires
    <strong>not less than 30 days</strong> notice, so he ceases on <strong>9 October 2026</strong>.
    <strong>2 · The supplier.</strong> On 15 October Ravi has ceased — but under s. 24(3) the supplier
    may still regard him as a partner unless the supplier had notice or notice had reached the
    Registrar. Neither happened, so the supplier's position is protected. <strong>3 · The LLP's
    filing.</strong> Section 25(2)(a) gives <strong>30 days from 9 October</strong> — by
    <strong>8 November 2026</strong> — and Ravi may file it himself under s. 25(6) if the LLP does
    not. <strong>4 · Ishaan.</strong> The LLP must file notice of his admission within 30 days of
    3 December 2026 — by <strong>2 January 2027</strong> — with his signed statement of consent.
    <strong>5 · Ravi's past.</strong> Section 24(4) leaves every obligation he incurred while a partner
    exactly where it was.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Extent of liability — sections 26 to 31

This is the heart of the Act and the most heavily examined block. Work through it in the statutory
order and it becomes a single argument.

**Section 26 — agency.** Every partner is, **for the purpose of the business of the LLP**, the
**agent of the LLP**, **but not of the other partners**. Contrast s. 18 of the Indian Partnership Act
1932, under which a partner is the agent of the firm *and* of the other partners.

**Section 27(1) — when the LLP is NOT bound.** An LLP is **not bound** by anything done by a partner
in dealing with a person **if** —

- **(a)** the partner **in fact has no authority** to act for the LLP in doing that particular act;
  **and**
- **(b)** the person **knows that he has no authority**, **or** does not know or believe him to be a
  partner of the LLP.

Both limbs must be satisfied. Absence of authority **alone** does not release the LLP — the outsider's
state of mind is the second half of the test.

**Section 27(2)** — the LLP **is liable** where a partner is liable to any person as a result of a
**wrongful act or omission on his part in the course of the business of the LLP or with its
authority**.

**Section 27(3)** — an obligation of the LLP, whether arising in **contract or otherwise**, is
**solely the obligation of the LLP**. **Section 27(4)** — the liabilities of the LLP are met out of
**the property of the LLP**.

**Section 28 — the shield, and its two windows.** **28(1)** — a partner is **not personally liable,
directly or indirectly**, for an obligation referred to in s. 27(3) **solely by reason of being a
partner**. **28(2)** — but nothing in s. 27(3) or s. 28(1) affects the **personal liability of a
partner for his own wrongful act or omission**; and a partner is **not** personally liable for the
**wrongful act or omission of any other partner**.

<div class="formula">Partner's own wrong → he is liable AND (s. 27(2)) the LLP is liable · Another partner's wrong → only the LLP is liable · Contract of the LLP → only the LLP is liable, met out of LLP property</div>

**Section 29 — holding out.** A person who **by words spoken or written or by conduct represents
himself, or knowingly permits himself to be represented, to be a partner** in an LLP is **liable to
any person who has on the faith of that representation given credit to the LLP** — whether or not he
knew the representation reached that person. The proviso adds that where the **LLP receives credit**
as a result, the **LLP is liable to the extent of the credit received or the financial benefit
derived**, without prejudice to the holding-out person's liability. **29(2)** — where a business is
continued in the same LLP name after a partner's death, the continued use of that name or of the
deceased partner's name **does not of itself** make his legal representative or estate liable for
acts done after the death.

**Section 30 — unlimited liability for fraud.** Where an act is carried out by the **LLP**, or by any
of its **partners**, **with intent to defraud creditors** of the LLP or any other person, or **for any
fraudulent purpose**, the liability of the **LLP and of the partners who so acted** is **unlimited**
for all or any of the debts or other liabilities of the LLP. The proviso: where the act is carried out
by a **partner**, the LLP is liable **to the same extent as the partner unless the LLP establishes
that the act was without its knowledge or authority**. **30(2)** adds criminal liability for every
person knowingly a party, and **30(3)** entitles any person who suffers loss from affairs conducted in
a **fraudulent manner** to **compensation** from the LLP and the partner, designated partner or
employee concerned.

**Section 31 — whistle-blowing.** The Court or Tribunal may **reduce or waive** any penalty on a
partner or employee who provided **useful information** during an investigation, and the LLP must not
**discharge, demote or otherwise discriminate** against such a person.

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 4 — three claims against one LLP">
  <p>
    Bharat Structural Design LLP has four partners. <strong>(i)</strong> Partner Anil signs a
    negligent structural certificate; the client's building cracks and it sues for ₹2 crore.
    <strong>(ii)</strong> Partner Kavya, without any authority under the LLP agreement, orders
    ₹35 lakh of steel from a supplier who has never heard of the LLP and believes he is dealing with
    Kavya's own proprietorship. <strong>(iii)</strong> Partner Devraj, with two others, deliberately
    routes ₹80 lakh of client advances out of the LLP to defeat its creditors.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>(i)</strong> Anil's negligence is his <em>own</em> wrongful act in the course of the
    business: under <strong>s. 28(2)</strong> he is <strong>personally liable</strong>, and under
    <strong>s. 27(2)</strong> the <strong>LLP is also liable</strong>. The other three partners are
    <strong>not</strong> — s. 28(2) second limb. <strong>(ii)</strong> Kavya in fact had no authority,
    <em>and</em> the supplier did not know or believe her to be a partner of the LLP. Both limbs of
    <strong>s. 27(1)</strong> are met, so the <strong>LLP is not bound</strong>; the supplier's remedy
    is against Kavya. Change one fact — the supplier knew he was contracting with the LLP and had no
    idea of the authority limit — and limb (b) fails, so the LLP <em>is</em> bound.
    <strong>(iii)</strong> Section 30 applies: the liability of Devraj and the two partners who acted
    with him is <strong>unlimited</strong>, and the LLP is liable to the same extent unless it proves
    the act was <strong>without its knowledge or authority</strong>. The partners who took no part and
    knew nothing keep their s. 28(1) shield.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. Contributions — sections 32 and 33

**Section 32(1)** — a contribution may consist of **tangible, movable or immovable, or intangible
property or other benefit** to the LLP, **including money, promissory notes, other agreements to
contribute cash or property, and contracts for services performed or to be performed**. There is **no
minimum contribution** and no concept of share capital.

**Section 32(2)** — the **monetary value** of each partner's contribution shall be **accounted for and
disclosed in the accounts** of the LLP in the prescribed manner. So a services contribution must still
be valued and shown.

**Section 33(1)** — the obligation of a partner to contribute money, property or other benefit, or to
perform services, is **as per the LLP agreement**.

**Section 33(2)** — a **creditor** of the LLP who **extends credit or otherwise acts in reliance on an
obligation described in the LLP agreement**, **without notice of any compromise between the
partners**, may **enforce the original obligation against that partner**. A private arrangement
reducing a partner's promised contribution is good between the partners and worthless against a
creditor who lent on the strength of the recorded promise.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>Section 33(2) is a favourite one-mark point.</strong> The three elements are
    <strong>reliance</strong> on an obligation <strong>described in the LLP agreement</strong>, and
    the creditor's <strong>absence of notice</strong> of the compromise. Miss any one and the creditor
    is left with the compromised figure. Note also that the contribution ceiling for a
    <strong>small LLP</strong> (§1) and for the <strong>audit exemption</strong> (§11) are both read
    off this same contribution figure.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 11. Books, audit and annual filings — sections 34, 34A and 35

**Section 34(1) — books of account.** Every LLP shall maintain **proper books of account** relating to
its affairs **for each year of its existence**, **on a cash basis or an accrual basis** and according
to the **double entry system**, and shall maintain them **at its registered office** for the
prescribed period.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>An LLP may keep its books on the CASH basis.</strong> Section 34(1) says "on cash basis or
    accrual basis" — a genuine option that no company enjoys, since s. 128(1) of the Companies Act
    2013 requires the accrual basis and the double entry system. The <strong>double entry
    system</strong> is mandatory for an LLP too; only the <em>basis</em> is optional. Options
    asserting "accrual basis only" are wrong.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 34(2) — Statement of Account and Solvency.** Every LLP shall, **within six months from the
end of each financial year**, **prepare** a Statement of Account and Solvency for that year **as at
the last day of the year**, in the prescribed form, **signed by the designated partners**.

**Section 34(3) — filing it.** The Statement shall be **filed with the Registrar every year** within
the prescribed time. **Rule 24** prescribes **Form 8**, to be filed **within 30 days from the end of
six months of the financial year** to which the Statement relates.

**Section 34(4) — audit.** The accounts of LLPs shall be audited **in accordance with such rules as
may be prescribed**, with power in the Central Government to **exempt any class or classes of LLPs**.
**Rule 24** exempts an LLP whose **turnover does not exceed ₹40 lakh in any financial year** **and**
whose **contribution does not exceed ₹25 lakh** — so audit becomes **compulsory** the moment
**turnover exceeds ₹40 lakh OR contribution exceeds ₹25 lakh**.

<div class="formula">AUDIT is required if turnover &gt; ₹40 lakh <strong>OR</strong> contribution &gt; ₹25 lakh · SMALL LLP status needs contribution ≤ ₹25 lakh <strong>AND</strong> turnover ≤ ₹40 lakh</div>

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>The audit test is OR; the small-LLP test is AND.</strong> They use the same two figures and
    pull in opposite directions, which is exactly why examiners pair them. Audit is escaped only by
    staying under <em>both</em> limits, so crossing <em>either</em> limit triggers it. Small-LLP status
    is earned only by staying under <em>both</em>, so crossing <em>either</em> destroys it. Practical
    consequence: an LLP that stops being small is, in the same breath, an LLP that must be audited. An
    LLP may also <strong>voluntarily</strong> get its accounts audited even when exempt.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Section 34A — accounting and auditing standards (inserted in 2021).** The **Central Government may,
in consultation with the National Financial Reporting Authority, prescribe standards of accounting and
standards of auditing** as **recommended by the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India**, for a
class or classes of LLPs. The power is enabling: note the consultee (NFRA) and the recommending body
(ICAI), which is what gets asked.

**Section 35 — annual return.** Every LLP shall file an **annual return**, duly authenticated, with
the Registrar **within 60 days of the closure of its financial year** — prescribed as **Form 11**.
Default makes the **LLP and its designated partners** liable to a **daily penalty** up to a maximum.

**The two clocks, side by side.** An LLP's financial year runs **1 April to 31 March**.

| Filing | Statutory source | Time allowed | For FY 2026-27 (ending 31 March 2027) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Form 11 — Annual Return** | s. 35 | **60 days** from **closure of the financial year** | **30 May 2027** |
| **Form 8 — Statement of Account and Solvency** | s. 34(3) with Rule 24 | **30 days** from the **end of six months of the financial year** | **30 October 2027** |

<div class="working-note">
  <div class="working-note-label">Working note — deriving the two dates for FY 2026-27</div>
  Financial year closes <strong>31 March 2027</strong>.<br />
  <strong>Form 11:</strong> 31 March 2027 + 60 days → 30 April is day 30, 30 May is day 60 →
  <strong>30 May 2027</strong>.<br />
  <strong>Form 8:</strong> six months from the end of the FY → <strong>30 September 2027</strong>
  (September has 30 days), then + 30 days → <strong>30 October 2027</strong>.<br />
  Sanity check: while the financial year ends on 31 March, Form 8 always lands on <strong>30
  October</strong> and Form 11 on <strong>30 May</strong>.
</div>

<Callout kind="amendment" label="AMENDMENT-CHECK · Form 8, Form 11 and the audit thresholds are rules-level">
  <p>
    The <strong>60 days for the annual return sits in s. 35(1)</strong> and is Act-level. Everything
    else in this section is <strong>LLP Rules 2009</strong>: the <strong>form numbers</strong>, the
    <strong>30 days after six months</strong> for Form 8, the <strong>eight-year</strong> preservation
    of books, and the <strong>₹40 lakh / ₹25 lakh audit thresholds in Rule 24</strong>. Rules-level
    numbers move by notification, and the Ministry has repeatedly extended filing dates for
    particular years by circular. Learn the structure, verify the figures for your attempt, and never
    cite a filing <em>extension</em> as the law.
  </p>
</Callout>

**Preservation.** Rule 24 requires the books of account to be **preserved for eight years** from the
date on which they are made. **Section 36** makes the **incorporation document**, the **names of
partners and changes in them**, the **Statement of Account and Solvency** and the **annual return**
available for **public inspection** on payment of the prescribed fee — which is the price an LLP pays
for limited liability. **Section 37** punishes a **false statement** in any return, account or other
document, and **s. 38** empowers the Registrar to **require information or explanation** from any
present or former partner or designated partner.

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 5 — accounts, audit and the two clocks">
  <p>
    Suryodaya Handlooms LLP has a contribution of ₹18,00,000. Its turnover for FY 2026-27 is
    ₹52,00,000, up from ₹31,00,000 in FY 2025-26. It keeps its books on the cash basis at the
    residence of a designated partner.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>1 · Audit.</strong> Turnover ₹52 lakh <strong>&gt;</strong> ₹40 lakh, so audit is
    <strong>compulsory</strong> for FY 2026-27 under Rule 24 — the contribution limb being satisfied
    is irrelevant, because the test is <strong>OR</strong>. <strong>2 · Small LLP.</strong> For the
    <em>following</em> year the s. 2(1)(ta) turnover limb is tested on the
    <strong>immediately preceding</strong> financial year, so the ₹52 lakh turnover of FY 2026-27
    means the LLP is <strong>not a small LLP</strong> in FY 2027-28, contribution notwithstanding.
    <strong>3 · Cash basis.</strong> Permitted by s. 34(1) — but the double entry system is not
    optional. <strong>4 · Location.</strong> Section 34(1) requires the books to be kept
    <strong>at the registered office</strong>; keeping them at a partner's residence is a
    contravention. <strong>5 · The dates.</strong> Form 11 by <strong>30 May 2027</strong>; Form 8 by
    <strong>30 October 2027</strong>; books relating to FY 2026-27 preserved until
    <strong>31 March 2035</strong>.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 12. Assignment, investigation and compounding — sections 42, 43 and 39

**Section 42 — a partner's transferable interest.** The rights of a partner to a **share of the
profits and losses** and to **receive distributions** in accordance with the LLP agreement are
**transferable, wholly or in part**. But:

- **42(2)** — the transfer does **not by itself** cause the **disassociation of the partner** or the
  **dissolution and winding up** of the LLP; and
- **42(3)** — the transfer does **not by itself entitle the transferee or assignee** to **participate
  in the management or conduct of the activities** of the LLP, or to **access information** concerning
  its transactions.

<div class="formula">Assignable: the ECONOMIC rights (profits, losses, distributions). Not assignable by that act: MEMBERSHIP, management, and access to information. The assignor remains a partner with all his duties.</div>

**Section 43 — investigation.** The **Central Government appoints inspectors** to investigate the
affairs of an LLP and report **(a)** where the **Tribunal**, by order, declares that the affairs ought
to be investigated; **(b)** where the LLP itself applies, on a resolution that its affairs ought to be
investigated; or **(c)** where the Central Government is of opinion, on the material before it, that
there are circumstances suggesting that the business is being or has been conducted **with intent to
defraud** creditors or others, or for a **fraudulent or unlawful purpose**, or in a manner
**oppressive to any of its partners**, or that the LLP was formed for a fraudulent or unlawful
purpose, or that the persons concerned in the formation or management have been guilty of **fraud,
misfeasance or other misconduct**, or that the partners have **not been given all the information**
they might reasonably expect. **Section 43(2)** allows **not less than one-fifth of the total number
of partners** to apply. Sections 44 to 54 carry the inspectors' powers, the report and the
consequences.

**Section 39 — compounding (substituted in 2021).** The **Regional Director**, or any other officer
**not below the rank of a Regional Director** authorised by the Central Government, may **compound any
offence under this Act which is punishable with fine only**, by collecting a sum **not exceeding the
maximum fine** provided for the offence and **not less than the minimum**. The proviso bars
compounding where a **like offence** by the LLP or its partners was compounded **within the preceding
three years**. Compounding is not available for an offence punishable with imprisonment, or with
imprisonment and fine.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>Keep the four decision-makers apart.</strong> <strong>Registrar</strong> — registration,
    strike off, and (as adjudicating officer under s. 68A) <em>penalties</em>. <strong>Regional
    Director</strong> — <em>compounding</em> under s. 39, and appeals against s. 68A adjudication.
    <strong>Central Government</strong> — the name direction under s. 17, appointment of inspectors
    under s. 43, and the standards power under s. 34A. <strong>Tribunal (NCLT)</strong> — compromise
    and arrangement (ss. 60 to 62) and winding up (s. 64).
  </p>
</Callout>

## 13. Conversion into an LLP — the Second, Third and Fourth Schedules

Sections 55 to 57 permit three conversions, each governed by its own Schedule.

| Converting entity | Enabling section | Schedule | Core eligibility condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Firm** (registered or not, under the 1932 Act) | s. 55 | **Second Schedule** | the partners of the LLP must comprise **all the partners of the firm and no one else** |
| **Private company** | s. 56 | **Third Schedule** | **no security interest** in its assets subsisting or in force at the time of application, **and** the partners of the LLP must comprise **all the shareholders of the company and no one else** |
| **Unlisted public company** | s. 57 | **Fourth Schedule** | the **same two conditions** as for a private company |

<div class="formula">Firm → LLP: same partners, nobody new, nobody dropped. Company → LLP: NO subsisting security interest + every shareholder becomes a partner and no one else. A LISTED public company cannot convert at all.</div>

**Section 58 — registration and effect.** On being satisfied that the Schedule's requirements are met,
the **Registrar registers the conversion** and issues a **certificate of registration** in the
prescribed form. The LLP must, **within 15 days of the date of registration**, **inform the Registrar
of Firms or the Registrar of Companies** with which it was registered about the conversion and the
particulars of the LLP.

**On and from the date of registration** —

1. there is **an LLP by the name specified in the certificate**;
2. **all tangible and intangible property vested in the firm or company, all assets, interests,
   rights, privileges, liabilities and obligations, and the whole of the undertaking** are
   **transferred to and vest in the LLP without further assurance, act or deed**; and
3. the **firm or company is deemed to be dissolved** and its name **removed from the register** of the
   Registrar of Firms or the Registrar of Companies.

**Continuity, from the Schedules.** Every **pending proceeding** by or against the firm or company may
be continued by or against the LLP; any **conviction, ruling, order or judgment** in its favour or
against it may be enforced by or against the LLP; every **existing agreement, contract and deed** has
effect as if the LLP were a party in place of the firm or company; every **contract of employment**
continues; and every **approval, permit or licence** issued under any other Act is transferred in the
LLP's name **subject to the provisions of that other Act** — which is why a licence under a statute
that forbids transfer does **not** survive automatically.

**Notice of conversion in correspondence.** For a period of **twelve months commencing not later than
fourteen days after the date of registration**, **every official correspondence** of the LLP must bear
**(a)** a statement that it was, as from the date of registration, **converted from a firm / private
company / unlisted public company into an LLP**, and **(b)** the **name and registration number** (if
applicable) of the entity from which it was converted. Default carries a fine on the LLP and its
designated partners.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>The three most-missed conversion points.</strong> <strong>(1)</strong> The
    <em>no-security-interest</em> condition applies to <strong>company</strong> conversions only — it
    is nowhere in the Second Schedule. <strong>(2)</strong> Conversion is <strong>all-or-nothing on
    the roll</strong>: bringing in one new person, or leaving out one existing partner or shareholder,
    defeats eligibility. Restructure <em>first</em>, convert afterwards. <strong>(3)</strong> A
    <strong>listed</strong> public company is outside ss. 55 to 57 altogether. Note also that
    liabilities <strong>are</strong> transferred — conversion is not an escape route from the firm's
    or company's debts.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 6 — Nandita and Ashwin convert, and a third party appears">
  <p>
    Nandita and Ashwin's Pune firm has two partners. They want their associate Farah, who is not a
    partner of the firm, to be a partner of the LLP from day one, and the firm's office premises are
    mortgaged to a bank. Separately, a private company client of theirs, Trilochan Systems Private
    Limited, has four shareholders and a subsisting charge over its receivables, and wants to convert.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>1 · The firm.</strong> The Second Schedule requires the LLP's partners to be
    <strong>all the partners of the firm and no one else</strong>. Including Farah at conversion
    <strong>defeats eligibility</strong>. The fix is sequencing: convert with Nandita and Ashwin
    alone, then admit Farah under the LLP agreement (s. 22) and file the s. 25(2)(a) notice within
    30 days. <strong>2 · The mortgage.</strong> The no-security-interest bar sits in the
    <strong>Third and Fourth Schedules only</strong>, so the firm's mortgaged premises are
    <strong>no obstacle</strong>; the mortgage simply travels with the property on vesting.
    <strong>3 · The company.</strong> Trilochan Systems <em>is</em> caught: the charge over its
    receivables must be <strong>satisfied or released before the application</strong>, and all four
    shareholders — and only they — must become partners. <strong>4 · Afterwards.</strong> Each
    converted entity has <strong>15 days</strong> to inform its former Registrar, and must carry the
    conversion statement on <strong>every official correspondence for twelve months</strong> beginning
    not later than fourteen days after registration.

## 14. Foreign LLPs, compromise, winding up and strike off

**Section 59 — foreign LLPs.** The **Central Government may make rules** for provisions in relation to
the **establishment of a place of business by foreign LLPs within India** and the carrying on of their
business here, by **applying or incorporating, with appropriate modifications, the provisions of the
Companies Act** or such other regulatory mechanism as may be prescribed. A "foreign limited liability
partnership" is defined by **s. 2(1)(m)** as an LLP **formed, incorporated or registered outside
India** which **establishes a place of business within India**. The section is enabling only — the
detail is rules-level.

**Sections 60 to 62 — compromise, arrangement or reconstruction.** Where a **compromise or
arrangement** is proposed **between an LLP and its creditors**, or **between an LLP and its
partners**, the **Tribunal** may, on the application of the LLP, of any creditor or partner, or of the
liquidator in a winding up, order a **meeting** of the creditors or partners. If a majority
representing **three-fourths in value** of the creditors or partners present and voting agrees, the
compromise or arrangement, **if sanctioned by the Tribunal, is binding on all** of them and on the LLP
(and on the liquidator and contributories in a winding up). Section 61 lets the Tribunal supervise the
carrying out and give directions, or order winding up; **s. 62** deals with **reconstruction or
amalgamation** of LLPs, including transfer of the undertaking and dissolution without winding up.

**Section 63 — modes of winding up.** An LLP may be wound up **voluntarily** or **by the Tribunal**.

**Section 64 — winding up by the Tribunal.** An LLP may be wound up by the Tribunal —

- **(a)** if the **LLP decides** that it be wound up by the Tribunal;
- **(b)** if, for a period of **more than six months**, the **number of partners is reduced below
  two**;
- **(c)** if the LLP is **unable to pay its debts**;
- **(d)** if the LLP has acted **against the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India, the
  security of the State or public order**;
- **(e)** if the LLP has made **default in filing** with the Registrar the **Statement of Account and
  Solvency** or the **annual return** for **any five consecutive financial years**; or
- **(f)** if the **Tribunal is of opinion that it is just and equitable** that the LLP be wound up.

**Section 65** empowers the Central Government to make **rules for winding up and dissolution**, and
**s. 67** lets it apply provisions of the Companies Act to LLPs by notification. **Sections 67A to
67C**, inserted in 2021, provide for **Special Courts** for the speedy trial of offences: an offence
punishable with **imprisonment of three years or more** is triable by the **Special Court**, and every
other offence by a **Metropolitan Magistrate or a Judicial Magistrate of the first class**.
**Section 68A**, also inserted in 2021, empowers the Central Government to appoint **adjudicating
officers** (in practice, Registrars) to **adjudge penalties**, with an **appeal to the Regional
Director within 60 days** of receipt of the order.

**Strike off — s. 75 with Rule 37.** Where the **Registrar has reasonable cause to believe** that an
LLP **is not carrying on business or operation** in accordance with the Act, he may **strike its name
off the register**, after giving the LLP a reasonable opportunity of being heard. An LLP may also
**apply itself** where it has **ceased commercial activity** for the prescribed period, with the
consent of all partners. Striking off is an **administrative** route out; it is not winding up, and
the liability of every designated partner, partner and of the LLP continues and may be enforced as if
the LLP had not been dissolved.

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

1. **Using 182 days for the residence test.** It is **120 days during the financial year** (s. 7,
   Explanation, as amended in 2021). The 182-day test belongs to s. 149(3) of the Companies Act 2013.
2. **Treating the audit test as AND and the small-LLP test as OR.** They are the other way round.
   Audit: turnover **&gt; ₹40 lakh OR** contribution **&gt; ₹25 lakh**. Small LLP: contribution
   **≤ ₹25 lakh AND** turnover **≤ ₹40 lakh**.
3. **Applying the First Schedule wholesale.** Section 23(4) fills gaps **matter by matter**; an
   agreement that covers a matter always prevails on that matter.
4. **Splitting profits in the contribution ratio when the agreement is silent.** The First Schedule
   says **equally**, whatever the contributions.
5. **Saying the LLP dissolves when it falls to one partner.** It does not. Section 6(2) removes the
   sole partner's shield after six months, and s. 64(b) merely gives the Tribunal a **ground**.
6. **Forgetting the knowledge element.** Both s. 6(2) (the sole partner) and s. 27(1)(b) (the
   outsider) turn on what somebody **knew**.
7. **Reading s. 27(1) as one limb.** The LLP escapes only if the partner had **no authority AND** the
   outsider knew that, or did not know or believe him to be a partner.
8. **Confusing the Form 8 and Form 11 clocks.** Form 11 — **60 days from the close of the FY**
   (**30 May**). Form 8 — **30 days from the end of six months of the FY** (**30 October**).
9. **Assuming an assignee of profits becomes a partner.** Section 42(3) denies him management and
   information; the assignor stays a partner.
10. **Applying the no-security-interest condition to a firm.** It is in the **Third and Fourth**
    Schedules only.
11. **Adding a new person at the moment of conversion.** The converting entity's partners or
    shareholders must become the LLP's partners — **all of them, and no one else**.
12. **Citing the Indian Partnership Act 1932.** Section 4 shuts it out entirely.
13. **Mixing up the decision-makers.** Registrar (registration, strike off, penalty adjudication) ·
    Regional Director (compounding, appeals) · Central Government (name direction, inspectors,
    standards) · Tribunal (compromise, winding up).
14. **Quoting rupee penalties from memory.** The 2021 Amendment recast almost all of them.

## One-page revision summary

**Nature.** s. 3 — body corporate, separate legal entity, perpetual succession, change in partners
irrelevant. s. 4 — **Indian Partnership Act 1932 does not apply**. s. 11(1) — lawful business **with a
view to profit**. s. 2(1)(ta) — **small LLP**: contribution ≤ **₹25 lakh** **and** turnover of the
**preceding** FY ≤ **₹40 lakh** (both raisable by prescription up to ₹5 crore / ₹50 crore).

**Partners.** s. 22 — subscribers become partners; others per the LLP agreement. s. 5 — individual or
**body corporate**; an individual is disqualified if of **unsound mind by a subsisting court
finding**, an **undischarged insolvent**, or one whose **own insolvency application is pending**.
s. 6 — minimum **two**, **no maximum**; sole partner + **six months** of continued business +
**knowledge** → **personal liability** for obligations incurred in that period.

**Designated partners.** s. 7 — at least **two individuals**, at least **one resident** = stay of
**≥ 120 days in the financial year**; bodies-corporate LLPs use nominees; **prior consent** (7(4)),
particulars filed **within 30 days** (7(5)), **DPIN** (7(6), Companies Act ss. 153 to 159 applied).
s. 8 — responsible for **all compliance and filings**, and **liable to all penalties** on the LLP.
s. 9 — vacancy filled **within 30 days**; if none, or only one, **every partner is deemed** a
designated partner. s. 10 — penalty on the LLP and on partners or designated partners.

**Incorporation and name.** s. 11 — incorporation document (FiLLiP) plus a **statement of compliance
by a professional engaged in the formation AND a subscriber**; a knowingly false statement →
**imprisonment up to two years and fine ₹10,000 to ₹5,00,000**. s. 12 — the certificate is
**conclusive evidence**. s. 14 — sue and be sued, own property, **optional common seal**. s. 13 —
registered office; a change is effective **only on filing**. s. 15 — the name must end in **LLP** or
**limited liability partnership**; not **undesirable** or identical with or too nearly resembling a
**firm, LLP, body corporate or trade mark (registered or applied for)**. s. 16 — reservation for
**three months from intimation**. s. 17 — Central Government direction → change **within 3 months**;
notice to the Registrar **within 15 days**; LLP agreement amended **within 30 days**; a trade-mark
proprietor must apply **within 3 years**; default → **CG allots a name**.

**Agreement.** s. 23 — the LLP agreement governs and is filed with the Registrar; a
**pre-incorporation agreement** binds the LLP only if **ratified by all partners after
incorporation**; where the agreement is **silent on a matter**, the **First Schedule** supplies it —
**equal** shares, **no remuneration**, **every partner may manage**, **unanimity** to admit a partner
and to change the nature of the business, **majority in number with one vote each** for other matters,
minutes within **30 days**, no expulsion without express power, **arbitration** for disputes.

**Cessation and changes.** s. 24 — agreement, else **30 days written notice**; automatic on **death,
dissolution, unsound mind, insolvency**; a former partner is still a partner as to a dealer **until
that person has notice or the Registrar has notice**; **past obligations survive**; he takes his
**capital contribution actually made** plus his **share of accumulated profits less losses** at the
date of cessation, with **no right to interfere in management**. s. 25 — a partner informs the LLP of
a name or address change in **15 days**; the LLP files every change with the Registrar in **30 days**;
a former partner may file it himself.

**Liability.** s. 26 — agent of the **LLP, not of the other partners**. s. 27(1) — the LLP is not
bound only where the partner had **no authority AND** the outsider **knew that, or did not know or
believe him to be a partner**. s. 27(2) — the LLP is liable for a partner's **wrongful act in the
course of business**. s. 27(3)–(4) — obligations are **solely the LLP's**, met out of **LLP
property**. s. 28 — a partner is **not personally liable merely for being a partner**, but **is**
liable for **his own** wrongful act and **not** for another partner's. s. 29 — **holding out**; the
LLP is liable to the extent of the credit received; a deceased partner's estate is not liable merely
because the name continues. s. 30 — **unlimited** liability for the LLP and the partners who acted
**with intent to defraud**, unless the LLP proves absence of its knowledge or authority. s. 31 —
**whistle-blower** protection.

**Contribution.** ss. 32 and 33 — money, property, benefits, promissory notes, **contracts for
services**; the **monetary value is disclosed in the accounts**; the obligation is as per the
agreement; a **creditor who relied on the agreement without notice of a compromise** may enforce the
**original** obligation.

**Accounts and filings.** s. 34(1) — proper books, **cash or accrual**, **double entry**, at the
**registered office**; Rule 24 — preserved **eight years**. s. 34(2) — the Statement of Account and
Solvency is **prepared within six months** of the FY end and **signed by the designated partners**.
s. 34(3) with Rule 24 — **Form 8** within **30 days of the end of six months of the FY** →
**30 October**. s. 34(4) with Rule 24 — **audit if turnover &gt; ₹40 lakh OR contribution &gt; ₹25
lakh**. s. 34A — the CG prescribes **accounting and auditing standards** in consultation with
**NFRA**, as recommended by **ICAI**. s. 35 — **Form 11** annual return within **60 days of the
closure of the FY** → **30 May**. s. 36 — public inspection. ss. 37 and 38 — false statements, and the
Registrar's power to call for information.

**The rest.** s. 39 — **compounding by the Regional Director** of offences punishable with **fine
only**, barred where a like offence was compounded within **three years**. s. 42 — **economic rights
assignable**; no disassociation, no dissolution, **no management or information rights** for the
assignee. s. 43 — **investigation** by inspectors appointed by the **Central Government**, on a
Tribunal order, the LLP's own application, or the CG's opinion; **one-fifth of the partners** may
apply. ss. 55 to 58 with the **Second / Third / Fourth Schedules** — firm, private company, unlisted
public company; company conversions need **no subsisting security interest**; all partners or
shareholders and **no one else**; on registration everything **vests in the LLP** and the old entity
is **deemed dissolved**; inform the old Registrar in **15 days**; conversion statement on official
correspondence for **twelve months** beginning within **fourteen days**. s. 59 — **foreign LLPs**, by
rules. ss. 60 to 62 — **compromise or arrangement**, **three-fourths in value**, sanctioned by the
**Tribunal**. ss. 63 and 64 — **voluntary or Tribunal** winding up; the grounds include the LLP's own
decision, **below two partners for more than six months**, acts against the **sovereignty and
integrity of India**, default in filing **Form 8 or Form 11 for five consecutive financial years**,
and **just and equitable**. ss. 67A to 67C — **Special Courts**. s. 68A — **adjudication of
penalties** by the Registrar, with an appeal to the **Regional Director in 60 days**. s. 75 with Rule
37 — **strike off** by the Registrar, or on the LLP's own application.
