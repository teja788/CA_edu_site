Company law looks like a wall of sections, but almost every dispute in the Companies Act 2013
starts one step earlier, in the dictionary. Is this entity a *company* at all? If it is, is it
*private* or *public*? Is it *small*? Is that other company its *subsidiary*, or merely an
*associate*? Answer those questions wrongly and every downstream answer — how many directors,
which resolutions, whether an auditor must rotate, whether consolidated accounts are needed —
goes wrong with it. That is why the syllabus opens here, and why examiners love this chapter:
one badly-read definition costs a whole question.

So treat Chapter 1 as a set of **tests you apply**, not paragraphs you recite. Each definition
below is broken into its ingredients, the ingredients are then run against a short scenario
(the vignettes), and the traps — joint holders, employee-members, voting power versus share
capital, the deemed-public rule — are pulled out where you will meet them. The law here is
stated **as it stands on 28 February 2026**, which is the cut-off for the **Sept 2026** and
**Jan 2027** attempts. Two things in this chapter live in the Rules rather than the Act and
therefore move more often than the rest: the small-company thresholds and the listed-company
carve-outs. Both are flagged where they appear.

## 1. Where the Act applies — section 1

The Companies Act 2013 **extends to the whole of India** (s.1(2)). Its sections were brought
into force in tranches by notification, which is why you still meet references to "the date of
commencement" of a particular provision.

The application list in **s.1(4)** is the one that gets examined. The Act applies to:

| Applies to | Carve-in condition |
|---|---|
| Companies incorporated under this Act **or under any previous company law** | Unconditional — a 1956 Act company is governed by the 2013 Act today |
| **Insurance companies** | Except in so far as the 2013 Act is inconsistent with the Insurance Act 1938 / IRDA Act 1999 |
| **Banking companies** | Except in so far as inconsistent with the Banking Regulation Act 1949 |
| Companies engaged in the **generation or supply of electricity** | Except in so far as inconsistent with the Electricity Act 2003 |
| Any **other company governed by a special Act** for the time being in force | Except in so far as inconsistent with that special Act |
| Such **body corporate incorporated by any Act** as the Central Government may notify | Subject to the exceptions, modifications and adaptations in the notification |

Read the pattern, not the list. Banks, insurers and electricity companies are **not outside**
the Companies Act — they are **inside it**, with their sectoral statute winning wherever the two
collide, and only to the extent of the inconsistency. The rest of the Act applies to them.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>"Governed by a special Act" appears twice in this chapter, doing two different
    jobs.</strong> In s.1(4) it decides how much of the Companies Act applies to such a
    company. In s.2(85) it is an outright <em>disqualification</em> from small-company status.
    A stem about a banking company can therefore be testing either point — read whether it
    asks "does the Act apply?" or "is it a small company?"
  </p>
</Callout>

## 2. What a company is — sections 2(20) and 2(11)

**Company — s.2(20):** a company incorporated **under this Act or under any previous company
law**. The definition is deliberately circular and therefore strict: incorporation under the
company-law statute is the *only* route in. A partnership firm, an LLP, a co-operative society,
a Hindu Undivided Family business or a trust is never a "company" for the Act, however large it
is and however corporate it looks.

**Body corporate (or corporation) — s.2(11):** a wider bucket. It **includes a company
incorporated outside India**, but it **does not include** (i) a co-operative society registered
under any law relating to co-operative societies, and (ii) any other body corporate, not being
a company as defined in the Act, which the Central Government may notify.

So the concentric picture is:

- **Body corporate** = everything with corporate personality — Indian companies, foreign
  companies, LLPs, statutory corporations created by an Act — minus the two exclusions above.
- **Company** = the Indian, Companies-Act-registered slice of that.

Every company is a body corporate; most bodies corporate are not companies. Wherever the Act
wants to catch foreign parents and LLPs it says "body corporate" — which is exactly why the
Explanation to s.2(46) and s.2(87) tells you that, for the holding and subsidiary definitions,
"company" **includes any body corporate**.

## 3. The features that follow from incorporation

1. **Separate legal entity.** The company is a person in law distinct from its members. This is
   the foundation stone laid in *Salomon v A Salomon &amp; Co Ltd* (1897): a trader who converted
   his business into a company and took secured debentures for the price ranked ahead of the
   company's unsecured creditors, because the company — though he owned virtually all of it —
   was a different person from him.
2. **Perpetual succession.** Members die, become insolvent, or sell out; the company continues
   until it is wound up or struck off. "Members may come and members may go, but the company
   goes on" is the classroom line, and it is why a company's contracts survive a change of
   ownership.
3. **Limited liability** (for a company limited by shares): a member's exposure is capped at
   the unpaid amount on the shares held. Fully-paid shares mean no further liability at all. In
   a company limited by guarantee the cap is the guaranteed amount; an unlimited company gives
   no cap at all.
4. **Separate property.** The company's assets belong to the company, not to the members
   proportionately: a member with 99% of the shares has **no insurable interest** in a
   particular company asset and cannot sell it.
5. **Capacity to sue and be sued** in its own name — and conversely a member cannot generally
   sue for a wrong done to the company, the company being the proper plaintiff.
6. **Common seal — now optional.** The Companies (Amendment) Act 2015 made the common seal
   optional. Where a company has no seal, documents that would have been sealed are signed by
   **two directors, or by one director and the company secretary** where one is appointed. The
   seal is therefore no longer a defining feature; do not list it as one.
7. **Transferability of shares (s.44).** Shares are movable property, transferable in the
   manner provided by the articles. A **public** company's shares are freely transferable; a
   **private** company's articles must **restrict** transfer — but "restrict" means regulate
   (pre-emption, board refusal), not prohibit outright.
8. **Separation of ownership and management.** Members own; the Board manages. A member has no
   authority, as member, to act for the company.

<Callout kind="pointer">
  <p>
    <strong>The employee-shareholder point.</strong> Separate personality runs so deep that a
    person can be the controlling shareholder, the sole working director, <em>and</em> an
    employee of the same company at once — the position taken in <em>Lee v Lee's Air
    Farming</em> (1961), where the widow of a pilot who was the company's governing director
    still recovered as the widow of a "worker", because the contract was between two distinct
    persons. Expect this as a one-line MCQ.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. Private company — section 2(68)

A **private company** is a company which, **by its articles**, does three things:

1. **restricts the right to transfer its shares**;
2. **limits its number of members to 200** (this limb does not apply to a One Person Company);
   and
3. **prohibits any invitation to the public** to subscribe for any securities of the company.

The clause also opens with "having a minimum paid-up share capital as may be prescribed" — the
fixed ₹1 lakh figure was **omitted by the Companies (Amendment) Act 2015** and nothing has been
prescribed since, so **there is no minimum paid-up capital** for a private company today.

Two counting rules sit inside limb 2 and they are the entire trap:

- **Joint holders count as ONE member.** Where two or more persons hold one or more shares
  jointly, they are treated as a single member for this clause.
- **Employee-members are EXCLUDED.** Persons who are in the employment of the company are not
  counted; and persons who were formerly employees, were members while employed, and have
  *continued* as members after the employment ceased, are also not counted.

Three further points that decide MCQs: the cap counts **members**, not shareholdings (one
person with 5,000 shares is one member); **debenture-holders are not members** and never enter
the count; and it is the restriction on transfer plus the ban on public invitation — not size —
that earn a private company its exemptions.

### Vignette 1 — counting members for the 200 cap

*Nivara Interiors Private Limited* has on its register: 148 individual members holding in their
own names; 12 shareholdings each held jointly by 3 persons; 26 current employees who hold
shares; 9 ex-employees who acquired their shares while employed and have kept them since
resigning; and 40 debenture-holders. Has the company breached s.2(68)(ii)?

**Working note — build the count from the clause, not from the register.**

| Category | On the register | Counted | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Individual members | 148 | 148 | Ordinary members |
| 12 joint holdings × 3 persons | 36 persons | 12 | First proviso: joint holders = one member |
| Current employees who are members | 26 | 0 | Second proviso (A) |
| Ex-employees, members while employed, still members | 9 | 0 | Second proviso (B) |
| Debenture-holders | 40 | 0 | Not members at all |
| **Total for s.2(68)(ii)** | | **160** | |

**Answer.** 160 counted members, comfortably inside 200. Read literally off the register the
figure would look like 219 and the company would appear to have breached the cap — which is
exactly the wrong answer the examiner has priced into the options.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>The ex-employee exclusion has three conditions, and stems break one of them.</strong>
    The person must (a) have been in the employment of the company, (b) have become a member
    <em>while in that employment</em>, and (c) have continued as a member after leaving. A
    retired employee who buys shares <em>after</em> resigning fails condition (b) and
    <strong>counts</strong> towards the 200.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. Public company — section 2(71)

A **public company** is defined negatively and then extended:

- it is a company **which is not a private company**; and
- it has such **minimum paid-up share capital as may be prescribed** — again, the ₹5 lakh
  figure was omitted in 2015 and nothing is currently prescribed.

Then the proviso that produces more exam questions than the definition itself:

> A company which is a **subsidiary of a company, not being a private company**, shall be
> **deemed to be a public company** for the purposes of the Act even where such subsidiary
> company continues to be a private company **in its articles**.

Unpack it carefully. The parent must be a company that is *not* a private company — i.e. a
public company. If it is, its subsidiary is public for the Act's purposes **whatever its own
articles or its name say**. The subsidiary keeps its restrictive articles and its "Private
Limited" name; it simply loses the private-company relaxations — directors, quorum,
related-party and deposit provisions and the rest — because the Act now treats it as public.

### Vignette 2 — the private company that is really public

*Harita Ventures Limited* (a public, unlisted company) holds 68% of the equity share capital
and voting power of *Sunbeam Logistics Private Limited*, whose articles restrict share
transfer, cap members at 200 and prohibit public invitations. Sunbeam has 41 members. Which is
Sunbeam: private or public?

**Working note.** Step 1 — is Harita a private company? No; it is public. Step 2 — is Sunbeam a
subsidiary of Harita? Harita controls more than one-half of the total voting power (68% > 50%),
so yes, s.2(87)(ii) is satisfied. Step 3 — apply the proviso to s.2(71).

**Answer.** Sunbeam is a **public company for the purposes of the Companies Act 2013**, even
though its articles remain those of a private company and its name still ends in "Private
Limited". Its 41 members and its restrictive articles are irrelevant to the deeming; only the
identity of its holding company matters. Had Harita itself been a *private* company, the
proviso would not bite and Sunbeam would remain private.

## 6. One Person Company — section 2(62)

An **OPC** is "a company which has only **one person as a member**". It is a species of private
company, not a third category: it is formed as a private company, and it carries "(OPC)
Private Limited" in its name. The memorandum must name a **nominee** who, with prior written
consent, becomes the member on the subscriber's death or incapacity to contract.

For Chapter 1 you need the classification consequences:

- An OPC **is** a private company, so wherever the Act gives private companies an exemption the
  OPC gets it too, plus OPC-specific relaxations (no AGM, cash-flow statement not required in
  its financial statements, one director suffices).
- The **200-member limb of s.2(68) is expressly disapplied** to an OPC — an OPC could never
  satisfy it, because it has exactly one member.
- An OPC can be a **small company** if it meets the s.2(85) thresholds and is not caught by the
  exclusions; the two labels are independent and can sit on the same company.

## 7. Small company — section 2(85)

A **small company** is a company, **other than a public company**, which satisfies **both**
tests:

1. **paid-up share capital** does not exceed the prescribed amount; **and**
2. **turnover, as per the profit and loss account for the immediately preceding financial
   year**, does not exceed the prescribed amount.

The **currently prescribed limits are ₹4 crore paid-up capital and ₹40 crore turnover**. The
Act itself caps how high those prescriptions may go (₹10 crore and ₹100 crore respectively);
the operative figures sit in the Companies (Specification of Definitions Details) Rules.

And the exclusion list — **nothing in the clause applies to**:

- **(A) a holding company or a subsidiary company;**
- **(B) a company registered under section 8** (charitable objects); or
- **(C) a company or body corporate governed by any special Act.**

The exclusions are absolute. A subsidiary with ₹2 lakh of capital and no turnover at all is not
a small company, because it is a subsidiary. Small-company status is worth having: abridged
board's report, two board meetings a year, no mandatory auditor rotation, no cash-flow
statement, and lower penalties under s.446B.

<Callout kind="amendment" label="AMENDMENT-CHECK · ₹4 crore / ₹40 crore are Rules-level figures">
  <p>
    The ₹4 crore and ₹40 crore thresholds were set by the Companies (Specification of
    Definitions Details) Amendment Rules 2022 and are stated here as the position on
    <strong>28 February 2026</strong>. They have already been raised twice since 2013
    (₹50 lakh/₹2 crore in the Act, then ₹2 crore/₹20 crore, then ₹4 crore/₹40 crore) and can be
    raised again by notification without any amendment to the Act. Before the attempt, confirm
    the figures in force; the <em>structure</em> of the test (both limbs, plus the three
    exclusions) is what is stable and what carries most of the marks.
  </p>
</Callout>

### Vignette 3 — is it a small company?

Test each on the position as on 28 February 2026:

| Company | Paid-up capital | Turnover (preceding FY) | Other facts | Small? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Amrut Foods Pvt Ltd | ₹3.60 crore | ₹36 crore | None | **Yes** — both limbs met, no exclusion |
| Bellary Tools Pvt Ltd | ₹1.20 crore | ₹47 crore | None | **No** — turnover limb fails, though capital passes |
| Chandra Poly Pvt Ltd | ₹0.50 crore | ₹6 crore | Wholly-owned subsidiary of Deccan Ltd | **No** — a subsidiary is excluded outright |
| Drishti Foundation | ₹0.10 crore | ₹1 crore | Registered under s.8 | **No** — s.8 companies are excluded |
| Everest Textiles Ltd | ₹1.00 crore | ₹5 crore | Unlisted **public** company | **No** — the clause opens "other than a public company" |

**Working note.** The order of checking matters. First ask "is it a public company?" — if yes,
stop. Then run the three exclusions. Only then compute the two numerical limbs, and remember
they are cumulative: **both** must be satisfied, and turnover is measured for the **immediately
preceding** financial year, not the current one.

## 8. Government company, foreign company, listed company

**Government company — s.2(45).** A company in which **not less than 51%** of the paid-up share
capital is held by the Central Government, or by any State Government or Governments, or partly
by the Central and partly by one or more State Governments — **and includes a subsidiary
company of such a Government company**. Note the three details: the test is 51% of *paid-up
share capital*; Central and State holdings are **aggregated**; and the subsidiary of a
Government company is itself a Government company by inclusion, not by deeming.

**Foreign company — s.2(42).** Any **company or body corporate incorporated outside India**
which (a) has a **place of business in India**, whether by itself or through an agent,
physically or through **electronic mode**; **and** (b) **conducts any business activity in
India** in any other manner. Both limbs are needed. A foreign body corporate with a liaison
presence but no business activity, or one that sells into India with no place of business here,
falls outside — which is precisely what stems test.

**Listed company — s.2(52).** A company which has **any of its securities listed on any
recognised stock exchange**. "Any securities" — so a company whose debentures alone are listed
is prima facie a listed company; equity listing is not required. But the proviso (inserted by
the Companies (Amendment) Act 2020) lets the Central Government prescribe classes of companies
which, though listed, are **not to be considered listed companies**. The Rules currently carve
out, broadly:

- public companies that have **not** listed equity shares but have listed **non-convertible
  debt securities** or **non-convertible redeemable preference shares** issued on a **private
  placement** basis (or both);
- **private companies** that have listed their **non-convertible debt securities** on a private
  placement basis; and
- public companies whose equity shares are listed only on a **stock exchange in a permissible
  foreign jurisdiction** under s.23(3).

## 9. Holding, subsidiary and associate — the control definitions

This is the heart of the chapter and the source of most of its numerical questions.

**Holding company — s.2(46):** in relation to one or more other companies, a company **of which
such companies are subsidiary companies**. The Explanation adds that "company" here **includes
any body corporate** — so a foreign parent, or an LLP-style body corporate, can be a holding
company.

**Subsidiary company — s.2(87):** in relation to another company (the holding company), a
company in which the holding company —

- **(i) controls the composition of the Board of Directors**; **or**
- **(ii) exercises or controls more than one-half of the TOTAL VOTING POWER**, either on its
  own or together with one or more of its subsidiary companies.

The two limbs are **alternatives**. Either alone makes the company a subsidiary.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>It is total VOTING POWER, not total share capital.</strong> The Companies
    (Amendment) Act 2017 replaced "total share capital" in s.2(87)(ii) with "total voting
    power". So non-voting preference shares (which carry votes only on resolutions directly
    affecting them, s.47(2)) sit in the capital base but <em>not</em> in the voting base. A
    stem that gives you equity capital, preference capital and a percentage is asking you to
    strip the preference capital out before dividing. Answering off paid-up capital is the
    single most common error in this chapter.
  </p>
</Callout>

Three more pieces of s.2(87):

- **Board-composition control (Explanation (b)):** a company controls the composition of
  another's Board if it can, by exercising some power at its discretion, **appoint or remove
  all or a majority of the directors**. The right to appoint 4 of 9 directors is not control;
  the right to appoint 5 of 9 is.
- **The deemed chain (Explanation (a)):** a company is a subsidiary of the holding company
  **even if the control is exercised by another subsidiary** of that holding company. So if A
  controls B, and B controls C, then C is a subsidiary of A — without A holding a single share
  in C. Note what is *not* multiplied: you do not compute 60% × 55% = 33% and conclude "not a
  subsidiary". Control is tested step by step down the chain, not diluted through it.
- **The layers proviso:** prescribed classes of holding companies may not have layers of
  subsidiaries beyond the prescribed number. Under the Companies (Restriction on Number of
  Layers) Rules 2017 the ceiling is **two layers** of subsidiaries, with carve-outs (one layer
  consisting of a wholly-owned subsidiary is not counted; acquiring a foreign company with more
  layers permitted by that country's law is not a breach; banking, NBFC, insurance and
  Government companies are exempt). "Layer" means a subsidiary or subsidiaries.

**Associate company — s.2(6):** in relation to another company, a company in which that other
company has **significant influence**, but which is **not a subsidiary** of the company having
such influence — **and includes a joint venture company**. Significant influence means:

- **control of at least 20% of total voting power**; **or**
- **control of, or participation in, business decisions under an agreement.**

Read the three moving parts. "At least 20%" makes exactly 20.00% an associate — the boundary is
inclusive. The **"but not a subsidiary"** exclusion means the tests run in order: subsidiary
first, associate only if the subsidiary test fails. And the agreement limb means a 5% holding
with a contractual say in business decisions can still create an associate relationship.

### Vignette 4 — a shareholding chain

*Aravalli Holdings Ltd* holds 62% of the equity shares of *Bhima Ltd*. *Bhima Ltd* holds 55% of
the equity shares of *Chenab Ltd*. *Aravalli* separately holds 8% of *Chenab*. All equity
shares carry one vote each; none of the companies has issued preference shares. Classify each
relationship.

**Working note 1 — Aravalli and Bhima.** Aravalli controls 62% of Bhima's total voting power;
62% is more than one-half, so **Bhima is a subsidiary of Aravalli** under s.2(87)(ii).

**Working note 2 — Bhima and Chenab.** Bhima controls 55% of Chenab's voting power; more than
one-half, so **Chenab is a subsidiary of Bhima**.

**Working note 3 — Aravalli and Chenab.** Do **not** multiply 62% × 55% (= 34.1%) and conclude
"associate". Explanation (a) to s.2(87) deems a company a subsidiary of the holding company
where the control is that of another subsidiary of that holding company. Chenab is controlled
by Bhima, which is Aravalli's subsidiary; therefore **Chenab is a subsidiary of Aravalli**. The
direct 8% is irrelevant to the conclusion — and because Chenab *is* a subsidiary, it cannot
simultaneously be Aravalli's associate: s.2(6) excludes subsidiaries expressly.

**Answer.** Aravalli is the holding company of both Bhima and Chenab; Chenab is a
second-layer subsidiary; there is no associate relationship anywhere in the chain.

### Vignette 5 — voting power versus paid-up capital

*Kaveri Ltd* has issued 10,00,000 equity shares of ₹10 each (one vote per share) and 6,00,000
non-cumulative preference shares of ₹10 each carrying no voting rights except on resolutions
directly affecting preference shareholders. *Malwa Ltd* holds 5,40,000 equity shares and all
6,00,000 preference shares. Is Kaveri a subsidiary or an associate of Malwa?

**Working note 1 — the wrong base.** Paid-up share capital = ₹1,00,00,000 equity +
₹60,00,000 preference = ₹1,60,00,000. Malwa's holding by capital = ₹54,00,000 + ₹60,00,000 =
₹1,14,00,000, i.e. **71.25%**. This figure is a distractor: s.2(87)(ii) has not spoken of share
capital since the 2017 amendment.

**Working note 2 — the right base.** Total voting power = 10,00,000 votes (equity only; the
preference shares carry no ordinary vote). Malwa controls 5,40,000 of them = **54.00%**, which
is more than one-half.

**Answer.** Kaveri is a **subsidiary** of Malwa — on the correct base as well as the wrong one,
here. Change the facts so that Malwa holds 4,80,000 equity shares (48% of voting power) plus
all the preference shares: the capital base would still read 67.5% and tempt you into
"subsidiary", but the voting base is 48% — not more than one-half, so **not a subsidiary**;
and 48% is at least 20%, so Kaveri would be Malwa's **associate** instead. That single switch
is the examiner's favourite version of this question.

## 10. Financial year — section 2(41)

For **any company or body corporate**, the financial year is the period **ending on 31 March
every year**, in respect of which the financial statement is made up. Where the company is
incorporated **on or after 1 January**, its first financial year runs to the **31 March of the
following year** — so a company incorporated on 12 February 2026 has a first financial year of
roughly fourteen months, ending 31 March 2027. A company incorporated on 20 December 2025 gets
no such extension: its first financial year ends 31 March 2026.

The single exception is the **foreign-alignment proviso**. Where a company or body corporate is
a **holding company, a subsidiary or an associate company of a company incorporated outside
India**, and is required to follow a different financial year for **consolidation of its
accounts outside India**, it may apply to be allowed **any period as its financial year,
whether or not that period is a year**.

<Callout kind="amendment" label="AMENDMENT-CHECK · who grants the different financial year">
  <p>
    On the position as on <strong>28 February 2026</strong>, the application under the proviso
    to s.2(41) is made to the <strong>Central Government</strong>. Older notes, and any
    coaching material printed before the Companies (Amendment) Act 2019 was notified, say
    <em>Tribunal (NCLT)</em> — the jurisdiction was shifted from the Tribunal to the Central
    Government, with a transitional proviso preserving applications already pending before the
    Tribunal. The class of applicants also widened over time to include an <em>associate</em>
    company of a foreign company, alongside a holding company and a subsidiary. Both points are
    flagged for reviewer confirmation against the notified text before this chapter is marked
    verified; state the Central Government position in the exam.
  </p>
</Callout>

Two consequences worth remembering: the 1 April–31 March year is **mandatory** — a company
cannot adopt a calendar year merely because its foreign parent does, unless it goes through the
proviso; and the proviso is available **only** for the foreign-consolidation reason, never for
commercial convenience, seasonality or tax planning.

## 11. Lifting the corporate veil

The separate-entity principle is a shield, and courts remove it only when it is being used as a
sword. "Lifting" (or piercing) the veil means looking past the company at the persons behind it
— fixing them with liability, or attributing their characteristics to the company. The grounds
fall into two families.

**A. Judicial (case-law) grounds**, named by case and proposition only, facts summarised:

| Ground | Proposition | Anchor |
|---|---|---|
| **Fraud or improper conduct** | Where a company is formed or used as a device to evade an existing legal obligation, the court disregards it. An employee bound by a non-compete set up a company to solicit his old employer's customers; the company was treated as a mere cloak for him. | *Gilford Motor Co v Horne* (1933) |
| **Sham / façade to evade a contract** | A seller who had agreed to sell land transferred it to a company he controlled to defeat specific performance; the court ordered performance against both, the company being a mask for the seller. | *Jones v Lipman* (1962) |
| **Evasion of tax or revenue** | Where a company exists only to reduce the tax of its controller — income routed through companies whose funds returned to him as loans — the arrangement is disregarded for tax purposes. | *Sir Dinshaw Maneckjee Petit* (1927) |
| **Enemy character** | A company incorporated in one country but controlled by residents of an enemy country in wartime can be treated as an enemy for trading purposes; the court looked at the nationality of those in control. | *Daimler Co Ltd v Continental Tyre &amp; Rubber Co* (1916) |
| **Avoidance of welfare/statutory liability** | A subsidiary created to divert turnover and depress a bonus entitlement was ignored, the two entities being treated as one. | *Workmen of Associated Rubber Industry Ltd* (1985) |
| **Agency or economic-reality reasoning** | Where the statutory or regulatory context requires it, a court may look at who really owns and controls the company, while emphasising that the corporate veil is lifted only where the statute or the justice of the case demands. | *LIC v Escorts Ltd* (1986) |

**B. Statutory grounds.** The Act itself pierces in specific places — misstatement in a
prospectus (ss.34–35), incorporation obtained by furnishing false or incorrect information
(s.7(7)), fraudulent conduct of business in winding up (s.339), and the investigation
provisions that let the Registrar or an inspector reach beneficial owners (ss.216 and 219). In
these cases you do not argue equity: you cite the section.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>"Lifting the veil" is the exception, and the exam wants you to say so.</strong> The
    default answer to "can the members be made liable?" is <em>no</em> — <em>Salomon</em>.
    Only after stating the rule do you turn to the ground on which the facts justify departing
    from it, and name it precisely: fraud, evasion of an existing obligation, enemy character,
    revenue evasion, or an express statutory provision. A commercial failure, a
    one-man company, or a wholly-owned subsidiary is <strong>not</strong> by itself a reason to
    lift.
  </p>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

1. **Counting joint holders separately.** Two or more persons holding shares jointly are ONE
   member for the 200-member cap — a proviso inside s.2(68)(ii), not a general rule of company
   law.
2. **Counting employee-members.** Current employees who hold shares, and ex-employees who
   became members while employed and stayed on, are excluded. An ex-employee who buys in after
   leaving is counted.
3. **Using paid-up capital for s.2(87)(ii).** The test has been **total voting power** since
   the 2017 amendment. Strip out non-voting preference capital before you divide.
4. **Multiplying down a chain.** 60% of B and B's 55% of C does not make 33% of C. Explanation
   (a) deems C a subsidiary of A outright. Percentages are never multiplied under s.2(87).
5. **Calling a chain company an "associate".** s.2(6) excludes subsidiaries. Test subsidiary
   first; only if that fails do you ask about 20% significant influence.
6. **Reading "at least 20%" as "more than 20%".** Exactly 20.00% of total voting power creates
   an associate relationship. Compare s.2(87), which needs **more than** one-half — the two
   clauses use deliberately different comparators.
7. **Forgetting the deemed-public proviso.** A "Private Limited" subsidiary of a public company
   is a public company for the Act. Its name and its articles do not save it.
8. **Treating small-company status as purely numerical.** A holding or subsidiary company, a
   s.8 company, a special-Act company and any public company are out regardless of size.
9. **Applying the small-company turnover test to the current year.** The clause says the
   **immediately preceding** financial year, per the profit and loss account.
10. **Saying the Companies Act does not apply to banks and insurers.** It applies; the sectoral
    Act prevails only to the extent of inconsistency.
11. **Listing the common seal as an essential feature.** Optional since the 2015 amendment.
12. **Reading s.2(42) with "or".** A foreign company needs a place of business in India **and**
    business activity in India.

## One-page revision summary

| Concept | The test, in one line | Clause |
|---|---|---|
| Application of the Act | Whole of India; companies under this or any previous company law; banks, insurers, electricity and special-Act companies **subject to** their own statutes | s.1(2), s.1(4) |
| Company | Incorporated under this Act or any previous company law | s.2(20) |
| Body corporate | Wider; includes foreign companies; excludes co-operative societies and notified bodies | s.2(11) |
| Private company | Articles: restrict transfer + cap members at 200 (joint holders = 1; employee/ex-employee members excluded) + bar public invitation | s.2(68) |
| Public company | Not private; **plus** a subsidiary of a non-private company is deemed public whatever its articles say | s.2(71) |
| OPC | One member only; a private company by species; nominee named in the memorandum | s.2(62) |
| Small company | Not public; paid-up capital ≤ ₹4 crore **and** turnover (preceding FY) ≤ ₹40 crore; never a holding/subsidiary, s.8 or special-Act company | s.2(85) + Rules |
| Government company | ≥ 51% of paid-up capital with Central and/or State Government(s); includes its subsidiary | s.2(45) |
| Foreign company | Incorporated outside India **and** place of business in India **and** business activity in India | s.2(42) |
| Listed company | Any securities listed on a recognised stock exchange, minus the prescribed carve-outs (privately-placed debt, etc.) | s.2(52) + Rule 2A |
| Holding company | A company whose subsidiaries the others are; "company" includes any body corporate | s.2(46) |
| Subsidiary | Control of Board composition **OR** more than one-half of **total voting power**; control through another subsidiary counts; max 2 layers | s.2(87) |
| Associate | Significant influence = **at least 20%** of total voting power **or** control of/participation in business decisions by agreement; not a subsidiary; includes a joint venture | s.2(6) |
| Financial year | Ends 31 March; incorporated on/after 1 January → first FY ends 31 March of the following year; foreign-consolidation exception on application to the **Central Government** | s.2(41) |
| Corporate veil | Rule: separate entity (*Salomon*). Exceptions: fraud/evasion of obligation, revenue evasion, enemy character, agency/façade, plus express statutory provisions | Case law + ss.7(7), 34–35, 339 |

Learn the tests in the order the Act applies them — public first, then the exclusions, then the
numbers; subsidiary first, then associate — and this chapter becomes procedural rather than
memorial. The two figures to re-check before the attempt are ₹4 crore and ₹40 crore.
