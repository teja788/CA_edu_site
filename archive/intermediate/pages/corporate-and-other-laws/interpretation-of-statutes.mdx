A State levy reads: **"Every dealer who sells vegetables, fruits, flowers and other perishable
articles in the notified market shall pay a market fee of one per cent of turnover."** Meera runs a
stall selling cut flowers and packaged cheese. The assessing officer taxes her whole turnover; she
says the cheese is not covered. Both sides are reading the same eleven words. He reads "other
perishable articles" at its widest. She reads it as coloured by the three words that precede it —
farm produce sold loose in a mandi, not a dairy product in a sealed pack. Nothing more than a rule of
construction decides who pays.

That is the whole chapter. **Every other paper in your syllabus tells you what the law says; this one
tells you how anybody knows what it says.** It is short, it is entirely conceptual, and it is the one
chapter where a well-organised answer of eight lines can carry full marks — because the examiner is
testing whether you can *name the rule, state it accurately, say when it is reached for, and apply it
to a clause you have never seen*.

For the exam this is a **rule-plus-illustration chapter**. Marks come from four things: the **correct
name** of the rule or maxim, a **one-sentence accurate statement** of it, the **trigger** (what makes
a court reach for it), and the **limit** (what it cannot do). Never write a rule without its limit —
the limit is where half the marks live.

  </p>

## 1. Interpretation and construction

**Interpretation** is the process of ascertaining the meaning the legislature has expressed in the
words it used. **Construction** is the process of drawing conclusions about a case that the words do
not address in terms — filling the gap the language leaves, from the language itself and its setting.
Judges and textbooks use the two words interchangeably and no exam mark turns on separating them, but
the difference is worth a line: interpretation looks at the **words**; construction looks at the
**situation the words must be applied to**.

**Why statutes need interpreting at all.** A statute is written before the disputes it will govern
exist. It is drafted in general language by many hands, over months, in a hurry at the end. The result
is that its words fail in predictable ways:

1. **Ambiguity** — a word bears two meanings, both natural ("bank", "issue", "charge", "sanction").
2. **Vagueness** — the word has a clear core and blurred edges ("reasonable time", "vehicle",
   "obscene", "public place"). No one doubts a bus is a vehicle; a skateboard is another matter.
3. **Incomplete drafting** — the legislature did not foresee the case. Technology moves; the words
   stand still.
4. **Internal conflict** — two provisions of the same Act, read literally, pull opposite ways.
5. **Error of language** — a misplaced "or", an omitted "not", a proviso in the wrong sub-section.
6. **Changing context** — a term of art whose commercial meaning has shifted since enactment.

The court's task is **never to make the law**. It is to find what the legislature said, presuming that
the legislature knew the existing law, chose its words deliberately, and did not intend an absurdity.
Where the words genuinely bear only one meaning, the court applies that meaning **even if it dislikes
the result** and leaves the remedy to Parliament.

## 2. Classification of interpretation

Two classifications are worth carrying into the hall.

**By source — legal and doctrinal.**

| Kind | Who does it | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| **Legal (authoritative)** interpretation | the legislature itself, or a court | binding |
| — *Authentic* | the legislature, by an **interpretation clause**, an **Explanation**, or a **declaratory/amending Act** | binding, and part of the statute |
| — *Usual* | the courts, in deciding cases | binding as precedent |
| **Doctrinal** interpretation | jurists, commentators, textbook writers | persuasive only |
| — *Grammatical* | works on the **words** — their ordinary or technical sense, grammar, syntax | the starting point always |
| — *Logical* | works beyond the words — on the **intention**, the object, the setting, the mischief | reached for only when the words fail |

**Grammatical versus logical construction** is the classification the examiner most often wants
explained. **Grammatical** (also called *literal*) construction asks only what the words mean on the
page: it treats the letter of the law as the safest evidence of its intention. **Logical**
construction asks what the legislature must have meant, using the object of the Act, the state of the
prior law, the consequences of rival readings, and the scheme of the statute as a whole. The
relationship between them is **not equal**: a court begins with grammatical construction and moves to
logical construction only when the grammatical result is ambiguous, absurd, repugnant to the rest of
the Act, or defeats the statute's evident object.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Do not describe logical construction as an alternative the court may choose freely.</strong>
    It is a fallback, not a menu option. An answer that says "the court may adopt either the
    grammatical or the logical approach as it thinks fit" loses the sequencing mark. Say instead:
    <em>the grammatical construction governs unless it produces ambiguity, absurdity, repugnancy or
    defeat of the object, in which case the court moves to logical construction and departs from the
    letter only so far as is necessary.</em>
  </p>
</Callout>

## 3. The literal or grammatical rule

**Statement.** Where the words of a statute are **plain, clear and unambiguous**, they must be given
their **ordinary, natural and grammatical meaning**, whatever the consequence. The court neither adds
words nor subtracts them; it does not ask whether the legislature was wise. The letter of the law is
taken as the best evidence of its intention.

**When it is reached for.** Always first. Every exercise in construction begins here, and roughly nine
disputes in ten end here as well.

**What it carries with it.**

- Words are read in their **ordinary popular sense**, unless the statute is dealing with a particular
  trade, science or art, in which case they bear their **technical sense**.
- A word **defined by the Act** bears the defined meaning throughout, unless the context otherwise
  requires.
- The same word is presumed to bear the **same meaning throughout the statute**, and different words
  are presumed to mean different things.
- **Casus omissus** — a case the statute has omitted cannot be supplied by the court. If the
  legislature has failed to provide for a situation, the gap is for the legislature to fill.
- Hardship, inconvenience or an unwise result is **not a ground** to depart from clear words.

**The limit.** The rule applies only where the words are genuinely clear. It cannot be used to justify
a reading that produces an **absurdity or a repugnancy**, and it cannot be used to defeat the plain
object of the Act by seizing on one word out of its setting.

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 1 — the literal rule taken to an uncomfortable result">
  <p>
    A (fictitious) State enactment provides: <strong>"No person shall bring into the reading room of a
    public library any dog."</strong> Kabir arrives with a trained goat, and later with a bear cub on a
    leash. The librarian objects to both.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>Applying the literal rule:</strong> the word "dog" is plain. It has no technical sense here
    and is not defined by the enactment. A goat is not a dog, and a bear is not a dog. Neither is
    prohibited by the section, however plainly the legislature would have prohibited them had it
    thought about it. That is a <strong>casus omissus</strong>, and the court will not supply it — the
    remedy is an amendment substituting "any animal". <strong>The point to carry away:</strong> the
    literal rule is not embarrassed by an unsatisfactory result; it is embarrassed only by an
    <em>absurd</em> one. A prohibition that fails to cover bears is unsatisfactory. It is not absurd,
    because it is perfectly capable of sensible operation as far as it goes.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 4. The golden rule

**Statement.** Begin with the grammatical and ordinary sense of the words; but where that sense leads
to some **absurdity, repugnance or inconsistency with the rest of the instrument**, the ordinary sense
may be **modified so as to avoid that absurdity or inconsistency — and no further**. The golden rule
is the literal rule with a safety valve.

**When it is reached for.** Only after the literal reading has been tried and has produced a result
that (a) is absurd or manifestly unjust, (b) is repugnant to another provision of the same statute, or
(c) is inconsistent with the statute's declared scheme.

**The two limits that carry the marks.**

1. **The threshold is high.** Not "harsh", not "inconvenient", not "unfair to my client" — the result
   must be *absurd or repugnant*. A tax that falls heavily is not absurd.
2. **The departure is minimal.** The court modifies the ordinary meaning **only to the extent needed**
   to remove the absurdity, and then stops. It does not rewrite the provision to what it thinks the
   ideal rule would be.

There are two recognised applications: the **narrow** application, where a word carries two possible
meanings and the court prefers the one that avoids the absurdity; and the **wider** application, where
a word carries only one ordinary meaning and the court nonetheless modifies it because the literal
result is repugnant to the whole purpose of the Act.

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 2 — golden rule, narrow and wide">
  <p>
    A model Succession (Regulation) Act provides: <strong>"On the death of a person intestate, his
    estate shall devolve upon his issue in equal shares."</strong> Two problems arise. (i) The word
    "issue" is used elsewhere in the same Act to mean the <em>issue of a notice</em>. (ii) Nandini
    murders her father and then claims her equal share as his only child.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>(i) Narrow application.</strong> "Issue" bears two meanings — descendants, and the act of
    issuing. Reading it as "the act of issuing" in a devolution clause is unintelligible, so the court
    prefers the meaning that makes the section work: descendants. Note how little the court has done —
    it has only chosen between two meanings the word already had.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>(ii) Wider application.</strong> "Issue" plainly includes Nandini; she is the deceased's
    child, and the section admits of no exception. Yet allowing a murderer to inherit from her victim
    is repugnant to the whole scheme of a succession law. The court therefore reads the section as
    subject to an implied qualification excluding a person who feloniously caused the death.
    <strong>The limit at work:</strong> the court excludes <em>Nandini</em>. It does not go on to
    redistribute the estate on some fairer principle of its own, or to disqualify her children. It
    departs from the ordinary meaning exactly as far as the absurdity requires and not one step
    further.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 5. The mischief rule in Heydon's case

**Statement.** Where a statute has been passed to remedy a defect in the earlier law, the court
determines its meaning by asking **four questions**, and then adopts the construction that
**suppresses the mischief and advances the remedy**.

| # | The question | What you are looking for |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | What was the **common law (the prior law)** before the Act? | the legal position the Act replaced |
| 2 | What was the **mischief and defect** for which the prior law did not provide? | the gap or abuse that provoked the Act |
| 3 | What **remedy** has the legislature resolved and appointed? | the machinery the Act actually enacts |
| 4 | What is the **true reason of the remedy**? | why that machinery was chosen |

The court's duty then is to make such construction as will **suppress the mischief and advance the
remedy**, and to **suppress subtle inventions and evasions** designed to continue the mischief.

**When it is reached for.** Where the words are **ambiguous or capable of two readings**, and the Act
is plainly remedial — passed to cure an identified evil. It is the rule of choice for **welfare,
labour, tenancy, consumer-protection and anti-evasion** legislation.

**The limits.**

- It **does not licence rewriting**. The court still has to find its reading in the words used; the
  mischief tells it which of the possible readings to prefer, not which words to insert.
- It is a rule for **remedial** statutes. It does not apply with the same force to a taxing statute or
  a penal statute, where strict construction governs.
- The mischief must be identified from **legitimate sources** — the prior law, the preamble, the
  statement of objects and reasons, the setting — not from the judge's own view of social policy.

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 3 — the four questions, answered on an invented Act">
  <p>
    Before 2024, a landlord in a model State could evict a monthly tenant at will. The
    <strong>Rent Security Act 2024</strong> is then passed; its preamble recites that it is "an Act to
    protect residential tenants from arbitrary eviction". Section 6 provides:
    <strong>"No landlord shall evict a tenant from a residential building except on a ground specified
    in the Schedule."</strong> Landlord Vikram does not evict his tenant; he disconnects the water and
    electricity, removes the staircase railing and stops the lift, until the tenant leaves. He argues
    that section 6 says nothing about any of this, and that he has evicted nobody.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>1 · Prior law:</strong> eviction at will, no protection. <strong>2 · Mischief:</strong>
    residential tenants losing their homes at the landlord's pleasure. <strong>3 · Remedy:</strong> a
    prohibition on eviction save on scheduled grounds. <strong>4 · Reason of the remedy:</strong> to
    secure the tenant's continued occupation of the home.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>Result:</strong> "evict" is capable of meaning either physical removal or any act by which
    the landlord deprives the tenant of the enjoyment of the premises. The second reading suppresses
    the mischief and advances the remedy; the first leaves the mischief in place through an obvious
    evasion, and Heydon's rule expressly directs courts to defeat "subtle inventions and evasions".
    Vikram's conduct is constructive eviction and falls within section 6. <strong>Now note the
    limit:</strong> if the Schedule had listed the grounds exhaustively and one of them plainly covered
    Vikram's case, no amount of mischief reasoning could take it out. The rule chooses between
    available readings; it does not overturn a clear one.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 6. Harmonious construction

**Statement.** A statute must be read **as a whole**. Where two provisions of the same statute appear
to conflict, the court must so construe them that **effect is given to both**, and neither is reduced
to a **dead letter** or rendered otiose. A construction that makes one provision redundant is to be
avoided; the provisions must be reconciled if reconciliation is at all possible.

**When it is reached for.** Whenever an apparent conflict arises — between two sections, between a
section and a proviso, between a general provision and a special one, between the main enactment and
a Schedule, or between a rule and its parent section.

**How courts do it, in order.**

1. **Reconcile.** Read the provisions so that both operate — usually by confining each to its own
   field.
2. **Special over general.** If they cannot both operate on the same facts, the **special provision**
   governs those facts and the general provision governs the rest (*generalia specialibus non
   derogant*).
3. **Non-obstante clause.** A provision opening with "notwithstanding anything contained in..."
   overrides the provisions it names, to the extent of the inconsistency.
4. **Later prevails.** As a last resort within the same Act, the later provision may be preferred —
   but this is a counsel of despair, used only when reconciliation is truly impossible.

**The limit.** Harmonious construction is a rule of **reconciliation, not of subtraction**. A court
may not achieve harmony by simply ignoring one of the two provisions; if the only way to give one
provision meaning is to destroy the other, the exercise has failed and one of the priority rules must
be used instead.

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 4 — harmonising two sections of one Act">
  <p>
    A model Co-operative Societies Act contains two provisions. <strong>Section 20:</strong> "Every
    member of a society shall have one vote." <strong>Section 44:</strong> "A society which is a member
    of a federal society shall be entitled to vote through its delegates, in proportion to its paid-up
    share capital in the federal society." A federal society refuses a member-society's proportional
    votes, relying on section 20.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The harmonious reading:</strong> section 20 is the general rule and speaks of
    <em>individual</em> membership of a society; section 44 is a special provision governing a
    particular class of member — a <em>society</em> that is a member of a federal society. Confine each
    to its own field and both operate fully: one member one vote in the ordinary case, proportional
    delegate voting in the federal case. <strong>The reading to reject:</strong> "section 20 comes
    first and is general, so section 44 must yield" — that would leave section 44 with nothing to
    operate on, a dead letter, which is precisely what the rule forbids. <strong>The reading also to
    reject:</strong> "section 44 shows the real intention, so section 20 does not mean what it says" —
    equally a subtraction.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 7. Purposive construction

**Statement.** A provision is to be read in the light of the **purpose or object** the legislature
sought to achieve, so that the provision is made to serve that purpose rather than frustrate it. The
court asks what the statute is *for*, and prefers the reading that fits.

**Relationship with the mischief rule.** Purposive construction is best described as the **modern,
broadened successor** of the mischief rule. Three differences are worth stating:

| | Mischief rule (Heydon) | Purposive construction |
|---|---|---|
| **Focus** | the **defect** in the prior law that the Act was passed to cure | the **positive object** the Act sets out to achieve |
| **Requires a prior law?** | yes — question 1 presupposes an earlier legal position | no — it works even on a wholly new subject with no predecessor law |
| **Materials used** | prior law, preamble, historical setting | all of those, plus the scheme of the Act, its long title, the statement of objects and reasons, the constitutional context |

So every mischief-rule case is a purposive case, but not every purposive case involves a mischief. In
Indian practice the two are applied together and an answer that says "purposive construction is the
wider, modern form of the mischief rule; it looks to the object of the Act rather than only to the
defect it cured" will be marked correct.

**The limit.** Purpose cannot displace clear words. The court may choose among meanings the words can
bear; it may not attribute to the words a meaning they cannot bear, however desirable the purpose. And
the purpose must be drawn from the **statute**, not from the judge's policy preference.

## 8. The primary rules side by side

| Rule | Trigger — when a court reaches for it | Limit — what it cannot do |
|---|---|---|
| **Literal / grammatical** | always first; words plain and unambiguous | cannot supply a *casus omissus*; cannot survive absurdity or repugnancy; cannot ignore a statutory definition |
| **Golden** | the literal reading is **absurd, repugnant or inconsistent** with the rest of the Act | departs from the ordinary meaning **only so far as necessary**; hardship alone is not enough |
| **Mischief (Heydon)** | words **ambiguous** and the statute is **remedial**; four questions | cannot insert words; weaker in penal and taxing statutes; mischief must come from legitimate sources |
| **Harmonious construction** | two provisions of the **same statute** appear to conflict | must give effect to **both**; may not render either a dead letter by subtraction |
| **Purposive** | the reading must be tested against the **object** of the Act | cannot give words a meaning they cannot bear; purpose must be drawn from the statute |

<Callout kind="pointer" label="The order in which you should deploy them in an answer">
  <p>
    A descriptive answer that simply lists five rules scores less than one that shows the
    <strong>sequence</strong>. Write it in this order and the structure marks look after themselves:
    <strong>(1)</strong> start with the plain words; <strong>(2)</strong> if they are clear, apply them
    and stop, even if the result is hard; <strong>(3)</strong> if the plain reading is absurd or
    repugnant, use the golden rule and modify no further than necessary; <strong>(4)</strong> if the
    words are ambiguous and the Act is remedial, run Heydon's four questions and prefer the reading
    that suppresses the mischief; <strong>(5)</strong> if two provisions clash, harmonise so that both
    live; <strong>(6)</strong> throughout, test the chosen reading against the object of the Act.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 9. Noscitur a sociis and ejusdem generis

These two are the most heavily examined maxims in the chapter, and students routinely blur them. They
are related but not the same: **ejusdem generis is a special application of noscitur a sociis**.

**Noscitur a sociis — "a word is known by the company it keeps".** The meaning of a doubtful word is
gathered from the words associated with it. Where words are grouped together in a statute, they are
presumed to be used in a related sense and to take colour from one another. The maxim applies to any
**string of associated words**; there is no requirement that general words follow specific ones.

*Illustration.* A licensing provision speaks of **"any place of public resort, dance hall, cinema,
theatre or circus"**. Is a public library a "place of public resort"? Its companions are all places of
**entertainment**. The doubtful expression takes colour from them, and a library — a place of study —
falls outside.

**Ejusdem generis — "of the same kind or nature".** Where **general words follow an enumeration of
specific words**, the general words are confined to things of the **same genus** as the specific words
that precede them.

**The four conditions, all of which must be satisfied:**

1. The statute contains an **enumeration of specific words**.
2. The subjects of the enumeration **constitute a genus or category** — they have some common
   characteristic beyond simply being listed together.
3. That genus or class is **not exhausted** by the enumeration — there must be something left for the
   general words to cover.
4. The **general words follow** the specific words (a general word *preceding* an enumeration is not
   restricted by it), and there is **no contrary legislative intention**.

**When the maxim does NOT apply** — this is the mark-winning half:

- Where the specific words **do not form a genus**. "Books, pamphlets, houses, motor cars and other
  things" has no common category; the general words stay general.
- Where the enumeration **exhausts the genus**, leaving the general words nothing to do — applying the
  maxim would make them redundant, and *ut res magis valeat quam pereat* forbids that.
- Where the statute shows a **contrary intention** — for example, by using "or any other thing
  **whatsoever**", or "whether or not of the same kind as the foregoing", or by defining the general
  expression.
- Where the general words are **not preceded** by specific words but precede them, or stand alone.
- Where confining the general words would **defeat the object** of a remedial provision.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Ejusdem generis needs a genus — find it before you use the maxim.</strong> The single most
    common error is to apply the maxim to any list at all. Ask first: <em>what is the common category
    of the specific words?</em> If you cannot state it in a phrase, the maxim does not apply and the
    general words must be read at their natural width. Second most common error: applying the maxim
    where the general words come <strong>first</strong>. In "any vehicle, including a car, truck or
    bus", the word "vehicle" is not cut down to the three examples; the examples illustrate it. Third:
    forgetting that "and other things <strong>whatsoever</strong>" is a standard drafting signal that
    the legislature meant the general words to be unrestricted.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 5 — the same list, two maxims, and Meera's cheese">
  <p>
    Return to the levy in the opening: <strong>"Every dealer who sells vegetables, fruits, flowers and
    other perishable articles in the notified market shall pay a market fee of one per cent of
    turnover."</strong> Meera sells cut flowers and packaged cheese.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>Step 1 — is there a genus?</strong> Vegetables, fruits and flowers share a category:
    unprocessed horticultural produce. Yes, there is a genus. <strong>Step 2 — is the genus
    exhausted?</strong> No — herbs, leaves, saplings and green fodder remain, so the general words have
    work to do. <strong>Step 3 — do the general words follow?</strong> Yes. <strong>Step 4 — contrary
    intention?</strong> The clause does not say "whatsoever" and does not define "perishable articles".
    All four conditions are met, so "other perishable articles" is confined to the genus, and
    <strong>packaged cheese is outside the levy</strong> — it is perishable, but it is not
    horticultural produce. Meera pays on the flowers only.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>The variations that flip the answer.</strong> (a) If the clause had read "vegetables,
    fruits, flowers and all other perishable articles <em>whatsoever</em>", the contrary intention is
    manifest and the cheese is taxed. (b) If it had read "sugar, cement, motor spares and other
    articles", there is no genus at all, the maxim fails, and the general words carry full width.
    (c) If the question were instead whether an unlit market shed is a "notified market" alongside
    "auction platform, weighing yard and notified market", that is a <strong>noscitur a sociis</strong>
    problem, not an ejusdem generis one — no general words follow an enumeration; a doubtful expression
    simply takes colour from its companions.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 10. Other subsidiary rules of construction

**Expressio unius est exclusio alterius — "the express mention of one thing excludes another".** Where
a statute expressly mentions certain things, the inference is that things not mentioned were
deliberately left out. A power to appoint "a chartered accountant or a cost accountant" as auditor
excludes an advocate. **Use it with care**: it is a maxim of *inference*, not a rule of law, and it
fails where the enumeration is plainly illustrative ("such as", "including"), where the omission is
explicable as an oversight, or where the mention was made *ex abundanti cautela* — out of abundant
caution.

**Ut res magis valeat quam pereat — "let the thing have effect rather than perish".** A construction
that makes the provision **effective and workable** is preferred to one that renders it useless,
nugatory or unconstitutional. Two corollaries: a court leans against a reading that makes a section
unworkable in practice, and it leans against a reading that would make part of the statute
**redundant** — every word is presumed to have been used for a purpose (*surplusage is not to be
presumed*).

**Generalia specialibus non derogant — "general provisions do not derogate from special ones".** Where
a general provision and a special provision cover the same ground, the **special prevails** on the
matter it specifically addresses, and the general governs the rest. The maxim works both **within one
statute** (part of harmonious construction) and **between two statutes**: a later general Act is not
presumed to repeal an earlier special Act by implication, unless the intention to do so is clear.

**Contemporanea expositio est optima et fortissima in lege — "contemporaneous exposition is the best
and strongest in law".** The meaning given to a statute by those who administered it **at or near the
time of its enactment**, and the long usage that followed, is strong evidence of its meaning. Three
limits: it applies mainly to **ancient or old statutes**; it yields at once to **clear words**; and it
has **little or no application to a modern statute** whose language the court can read for itself.
Departmental circulars and long-settled administrative practice are its usual vehicles.

**Reddendo singula singulis — "rendering each to each".** Where a sentence contains several subjects
and several objects, each is to be referred to its appropriate counterpart, rather than every phrase
being applied to everything. In "I devise and bequeath all my real and personal property to X", the
technical word *devise* attaches to the real property and *bequeath* to the personal property. In a
clause reading "the Registrar or the Tribunal may, on an application or of its own motion, cancel the
registration or wind up the company", the maxim prevents the absurd result that the Registrar may wind
up the company; each authority is matched with the power that belongs to it.

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 6 — four maxims in one clause">
  <p>
    A model <strong>Market Regulation Act</strong> contains: <strong>Section 12:</strong> "The Board
    may, by order, suspend or cancel the licence of a trader for a period not exceeding six months."
    <strong>Section 13:</strong> "The Board or the Collector may, on a complaint or on inspection,
    suspend the licence or prosecute the trader." <strong>Section 40:</strong> "An appeal shall lie to
    the Tribunal from an order under section 12."
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>(a) Reddendo singula singulis.</strong> Section 13 read distributively means the Board acts
    on a complaint and the Collector on inspection, each doing what belongs to it — it does not mean
    every authority may do everything by every route.
    <strong>(b) Ut res magis valeat quam pereat.</strong> Section 12 says "suspend or cancel ... for a
    period not exceeding six months". Reading the six-month limit as attaching to cancellation too
    would make cancellation identical to suspension and leave the word "cancel" with no independent
    work. The workable reading attaches the period to suspension alone.
    <strong>(c) Expressio unius.</strong> Section 40 gives an appeal from an order under section 12
    only; the express mention supports the inference that <em>no</em> appeal lies from a section 13
    suspension. But note the caution — if the Act elsewhere confers a general right of appeal from "any
    order of the Board", the inference collapses.
    <strong>(d) Generalia specialibus.</strong> If a later general Licensing Act says every licence
    suspension shall not exceed three months, the special six-month provision in section 12 of this Act
    is not impliedly repealed; it continues to govern market traders' licences.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 11. Presumptions in interpretation

Presumptions are the court's starting assumptions about what the legislature must have intended. Each
is **rebuttable** — displaced by clear words or necessary implication — and stating that in your answer
is worth a mark on its own.

| Presumption | What it means | How it is rebutted |
|---|---|---|
| **Against exceeding legislative jurisdiction** | the legislature is presumed to have legislated within its competence — its List, its territory | if the words admit of no reading within competence, the provision is struck down, not read down |
| **Of constitutionality** | a statute is presumed **valid**; the burden lies on the person challenging it, and where two readings are possible the one that keeps the Act constitutional is preferred (**reading down**) | clear unconstitutionality on any possible reading |
| **Against retrospective operation** | a statute is presumed to operate **prospectively** and not to take away vested rights or impose new obligations for past acts | express words, or necessary intendment; and the presumption **does not apply to purely procedural provisions**, which are presumed retrospective |
| **In favour of mens rea in penal statutes** | a criminal offence is presumed to require a **guilty mind**, even where the section is silent | express exclusion, or the clear implication of a regulatory or welfare statute creating strict liability |
| **Against ousting the jurisdiction of courts** | a provision excluding the ordinary civil court's jurisdiction is **construed strictly**; ouster is not inferred | express and clear words of exclusion |
| **Against altering the existing law more than necessary** | the legislature is presumed to know the existing law and not to change it beyond what the Act says | clear inconsistency with the existing law |
| **Against implied repeal** | an earlier Act is not presumed repealed by a later one unless they are irreconcilable, or the later Act covers the whole field | manifest inconsistency, or a clear intention to occupy the field |
| **Against injustice or absurdity** | the legislature is presumed not to intend an unjust, absurd or inconvenient result | plain words to the contrary — this is the golden rule's foundation |
| **In favour of territoriality** | an Act is presumed to apply within the territory of the enacting legislature only | express extra-territorial operation |
| **That the Crown / State is not bound** unless named | a statute is presumed not to bind the State unless it says so or the implication is necessary | express words or necessary implication |

**Retrospectivity — the distinction that is examined.** A **substantive** provision — one that creates,
takes away or alters a **right or liability** — is presumed prospective. A **procedural** provision —
limitation for filing, mode of proof, forum, form of an application — is presumed **retrospective**,
because no one has a vested right in a procedure. The qualification: a procedural change that
**destroys an accrued right** (for example, one that shortens a limitation period so as to bar a suit
already ripe) is not treated as merely procedural.

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>"Presumed retrospective" is not the same as "retrospective".</strong> Even a procedural
    amendment does not reopen proceedings already concluded, and it does not revive a remedy already
    barred. Equally, do not write that a beneficial statute is "always retrospective" — the correct
    statement is that <em>where a beneficial or curative provision is capable of two readings, the
    court may give it retrospective effect to advance the benefit</em>, which is a very different claim.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 12. Strict and liberal construction

| | **Strict construction** | **Liberal (beneficial) construction** |
|---|---|---|
| **Applies to** | penal statutes; taxing statutes; statutes taking away rights, imposing burdens, or ousting jurisdiction | welfare, labour, social-security and consumer legislation; remedial and curative statutes |
| **Approach** | the subject is not to be brought within the provision unless the words **clearly** cover him | the words are given the **fuller** of two possible meanings, so that the benefit reaches the class protected |
| **Effect of ambiguity** | resolved **in favour of the subject** — the accused, the assessee | resolved **in favour of the person the Act was passed to protect** |
| **Words may be added?** | never | never — but the words used are read at their widest legitimate reach |

**Penal statutes.** A penal provision is construed strictly. The consequences: an act is not an offence
unless it falls **clearly within the words**; the definition of the offence is not extended by analogy
or by considerations of policy; where the section is genuinely ambiguous, the construction **favourable
to the accused** is adopted; and **mens rea** is presumed unless excluded. Strict construction does not
mean a perverse or unnatural reading — where the words plainly cover the conduct, the accused is
convicted, and the fact that the penalty is severe is no reason to read the section narrowly.

**Taxing statutes.** The classic position: in a taxing statute one looks simply at what is **clearly
said**; there is **no room for any intendment**, **no equity about a tax**, **nothing to be read in and
nothing to be implied**. The consequences worth writing:

- A **charging provision** is construed **strictly**: the subject can be taxed only by clear words, and
  if he is not caught by the letter he goes free however plainly he is within the spirit.
- An **exemption or deduction provision** is also construed **strictly**, and the burden of showing
  that he is within it lies on the **assessee** — but once he is shown to be within it, the exemption
  is given full effect.
- A **machinery or procedural provision** (assessment, recovery, appeal) is construed **liberally**, so
  as to make the charge workable.
- **Tax planning** within the law is legitimate; a **colourable device** or sham is not, and an
  anti-avoidance provision is read purposively to defeat evasion.

**Beneficial or welfare legislation.** Where a statute is passed for the benefit of a class — workers,
tenants, consumers, women, the disabled — and a provision is capable of two constructions, the court
adopts the one **more favourable to the class** the Act protects, so that the benefit is not defeated
by a narrow reading. This is the mischief rule and purposive construction working together. **Two
limits:** the liberal approach operates only **where the provision is capable of two constructions** —
it cannot rewrite an eligibility condition the legislature laid down; and a **penal provision inside a
welfare Act** is still construed strictly, because a person is not to be punished by a generous
reading.

**Remedial versus penal — the distinction.** A **remedial** provision gives a **remedy to an
individual** for a wrong — compensation, restitution, reinstatement, recovery. A **penal** provision
imposes a **punishment for a public wrong** — fine, imprisonment, forfeiture, disqualification. The
same statute commonly contains both, and each part carries its own canon: liberal construction for the
remedial part, strict construction for the penal part. Where a provision is doubtful, ask **who
benefits from the sanction** — if the money goes to the injured person, it is remedial; if it goes to
the State as punishment, it is penal.

## 13. Mandatory and directory provisions

**The question.** A statute prescribes that something "shall" be done in a particular manner or within
a particular time. It is not done, or not done exactly. **Is the act invalid?** If the provision is
**mandatory** (imperative), non-compliance **invalidates** the act. If it is **directory**, substantial
compliance suffices and non-compliance leaves the act **valid**, though it may attract some other
consequence.

**"Shall" and "may" are not conclusive.** This is the heart of the topic. "Shall" ordinarily indicates
a mandatory provision and "may" a discretionary one, but the words are constantly used loosely by
draftsmen. A "shall" may be read as directory, and a "may" may be read as mandatory where a **duty**
is cast or a **right** is conferred on someone who can demand its exercise. The court decides by
looking at the **object and scheme of the statute**, not the auxiliary verb.

**The tests courts apply:**

1. **Object of the provision.** Does the object of the enactment require exact compliance, or is the
   provision merely a mode of achieving an object that can be achieved otherwise?
2. **Consequence of non-compliance stated?** If the statute itself declares the act **void**, invalid
   or of no effect on breach, the provision is **mandatory**. If it prescribes only a **penalty** for
   breach, that usually indicates it is **directory** — the legislature has provided its own
   consequence and did not intend invalidity in addition.
3. **Prejudice.** Does non-compliance cause **prejudice or injustice** to a person the provision was
   meant to protect? Prejudice points to mandatory; absence of prejudice points to directory.
4. **Serious general inconvenience.** Would treating the provision as mandatory cause serious general
   inconvenience or injustice to persons who have no control over the default (typically a default by a
   public officer)? Then it is read as directory.
5. **Public duty versus private right.** A provision imposing a duty on a **public officer** for the
   benefit of the public, with a time limit, is often directory; a provision conferring a **right or a
   safeguard on an individual** — notice, hearing, consent — is usually mandatory.
6. **Negative or prohibitory language.** "No order shall be made unless...", "shall not be valid
   unless...", "it shall be lawful only if..." is a strong indicator of a mandatory provision.

<Callout kind="example" label="Scenario 7 — one Act, three provisions, three different answers">
  <p>
    A model <strong>Municipal Assessment Act</strong> provides: <strong>Section 8:</strong> "The
    Assessor shall complete the annual assessment list on or before 30 June each year."
    <strong>Section 9:</strong> "No assessment shall be revised to the prejudice of an owner unless he
    has been given a notice of not less than fifteen days and an opportunity of being heard."
    <strong>Section 10:</strong> "Every notice under this Act shall be served by registered post; any
    officer failing to do so shall be liable to a penalty of five hundred rupees." The Assessor
    completes the list on 12 July; revises Rahul's assessment upwards after a 4-day notice; and serves
    Sana's notice by courier, which she admits she received and acted on.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>Section 8 — directory.</strong> The time limit is a public duty cast on an officer for the
    orderly conduct of administration. Owners have no control over the Assessor's diary, and holding
    every late list void would cause serious general inconvenience and deprive the municipality of a
    whole year's revenue for the officer's default. The list made on 12 July is valid.
    <strong>Section 9 — mandatory.</strong> Negative language ("No assessment shall be revised ...
    unless"), a safeguard conferred on an identified individual, and clear prejudice from the shortfall.
    A 4-day notice where fifteen are required invalidates the revision of Rahul's assessment.
    <strong>Section 10 — directory.</strong> The section prescribes its own consequence — a penalty on
    the officer — which indicates the legislature did not intend invalidity as well; and the object of
    service, namely actual notice, was achieved without prejudice to Sana. The notice stands, and the
    officer pays the penalty.
  </p>
  <p>
    <strong>Notice what did the work:</strong> all three sections say "shall". The auxiliary verb
    decided nothing. Object, prejudice and the stated consequence decided everything.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 14. Internal aids to interpretation

**Internal aids** are found **inside the statute itself**. They are always available to a court and
some of them are part of the enactment. The examiner wants each one **defined** and, more importantly,
its **weight** stated.

| Internal aid | What it is | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| **Long title** | the full descriptive title following the short title | **part of the Act**; a legitimate guide to its **general scope and object**, but cannot cut down clear words |
| **Short title** | the name for citation ("the Contract Act, 1872") | **identification only**; of no interpretative value |
| **Preamble** | the recital of the reason and object of the Act, opening "Whereas..." | **part of the Act**; the key to the **object and policy**; may be used where words are ambiguous, **never against plain words** |
| **Headings / chapter titles** | prefixed to a group of sections | may be referred to where the section is ambiguous; a heading **cannot control** the plain meaning of a section |
| **Marginal notes / side notes** | the note printed beside a section | **weakest** internal aid; generally **not part of the Act** and not inserted by the legislature; used only as a rough indication (marginal notes in the **Constitution** are an exception and have been used) |
| **Definition / interpretation clause** | "In this Act, unless the context otherwise requires..." | **binding**; the defined meaning governs throughout — subject only to the "context otherwise requires" escape |
| **Proviso** | "Provided that..." — appended to a section | **carves an exception out of the main provision**; operates only on the provision it is attached to; **not to be read as an independent enactment** |
| **Explanation** | "Explanation.— For the purposes of this section..." | **clarifies, and may widen or narrow** the main provision; it does not stand apart from it |
| **Illustrations** | examples appended to a section (Contract Act, Evidence Act, IPC) | valuable as showing how the legislature intended the section to work; **cannot modify or extend** the section, and where an illustration conflicts with the section, the **section prevails** |
| **Schedules** | tables, forms and lists at the end of the Act | **part of the Act**, given effect as such; where a Schedule conflicts with the body of the Act, the **enacting section prevails** |
| **Non-obstante clause** | "Notwithstanding anything contained in..." | an **overriding** provision — it prevails over the provisions it displaces, to the extent of the inconsistency |
| **"Subject to" clause** | "Subject to the provisions of section X..." | the **opposite** of a non-obstante clause — the provision **yields** to what it is made subject to |
| **Punctuation** | commas, semicolons, brackets | of **minor** value; a modern statute's punctuation may be looked at, but punctuation **cannot control** the meaning of the words, and old statutes were often enrolled without it |
| **Saving clause** | "Nothing in this Act shall affect..." | **preserves** existing rights, pending proceedings or an earlier law from the operation of the Act; construed so as not to conflict with the main enactment |

**Definition clauses — the three drafting styles.** Recognising which style is used is a standing MCQ.

| Formula | Nature | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **"means"** | **restrictive / exhaustive** | the definition is **hard-edged and complete**; nothing outside it is included |
| **"includes"** | **extensive / inclusive** | the word keeps its **ordinary meaning and gets more**; the list is illustrative and not exhaustive |
| **"means and includes"** | **exhaustive** | the definition is complete; the "includes" limb enumerates what the "means" limb covers and **does not add** an open-ended residue |
| **"is deemed to be"** | **legal fiction** | treats as true something that is not; carried to its logical conclusion **for the purpose stated**, and no further |
| **"unless the context otherwise requires"** | **escape hatch** | the defined meaning applies **unless** a particular section's context is incompatible with it |

<Callout kind="mistake">
  <p>
    <strong>Proviso, exception and Explanation are three different creatures — do not swap them.</strong>
    A <strong>proviso</strong> takes something <em>out</em> of the main provision that would otherwise
    have been within it, and it is limited to the provision it is attached to; it is not a substantive
    enactment of its own and it does not travel to other sections. An <strong>exception</strong>
    similarly exempts, but is usually drafted as a separate clause of general application. An
    <strong>Explanation</strong> neither excepts nor exempts — it <em>explains</em>, and may widen or
    narrow the main provision to make its true intent clear. Two consequences that get examined: a
    proviso cannot be used to <em>enlarge</em> the section it qualifies, and an Explanation cannot be
    read as an independent provision divorced from its section.
  </p>
</Callout>

<Callout kind="pointer" label="Non-obstante and 'subject to' — read the opening words first">
  <p>
    When two provisions clash, look at how each <strong>begins</strong> before doing anything else. A
    section opening "<strong>Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act</strong>" is declaring
    itself the winner over everything it names. A section opening "<strong>Subject to the provisions of
    section 40</strong>" is declaring itself the loser to section 40. Where <em>both</em> provisions
    carry non-obstante clauses, harmonise them first by confining each to its own field; if that fails,
    the provision that is <strong>more special</strong>, or that deals with the matter more
    specifically, prevails.
  </p>
</Callout>

## 15. External aids to interpretation

**External aids** lie **outside the statute**. They are admissible where the language is **ambiguous**,
and every one of them is subordinate to the words actually enacted.

| External aid | Use | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| **Dictionaries** | to find the ordinary meaning of an undefined word | a dictionary gives **all** possible meanings; the court must pick the one that fits the **context** — a dictionary cannot override a statutory definition or a trade meaning |
| **Textbooks and commentaries** | persuasive guidance on settled doctrine | never binding; the court forms its own view |
| **Historical setting** | the circumstances, state of society and prior law at enactment | establishes the mischief; cannot displace clear words |
| **Statement of Objects and Reasons** | the note accompanying a **Bill** | admissible to understand the **background, the mischief and the purpose**; **not** to construe the section, because the Bill may have been amended in passage |
| **Committee, commission and Law Commission reports** | the report that preceded the Bill | good evidence of the **mischief** the Act addresses; not evidence of the meaning of the enacted words |
| **Parliamentary debates** | speeches in the House | **limited use**; the speech of the mover of the Bill may be looked at to identify the object and the mischief, but individual members' opinions do not decide meaning — what was enacted is the text, not the speeches |
| **Earlier statutes on the same subject (*in pari materia*)** | statutes dealing with the same subject matter are read together as one system | applies only where the statutes truly deal with the same subject; a definition in one Act does not automatically govern another |
| **Later statutes** | a later Act may show how the legislature understood the earlier one, and a **declaratory or amending** Act may settle it | a later Act cannot change the meaning the earlier one bore, unless it is expressly declaratory or retrospective |
| **Foreign decisions** | decisions on similarly worded statutes in other jurisdictions | **persuasive only**; weight depends on similarity of language and of social conditions, and they yield to Indian statutory language and constitutional context |
| **Usage and practice** | long, uniform practice under an old statute (*contemporanea expositio*) | strong for **old** statutes; weak for modern ones; always yields to clear words |
| **Government / departmental circulars** | show contemporaneous official understanding | not binding on courts; they cannot add to or subtract from the statute |

**Internal versus external aids — the comparison the examiner asks for:**

| | **Internal aids** | **External aids** |
|---|---|---|
| **Where found** | inside the statute | outside the statute |
| **Examples** | long title, preamble, headings, marginal notes, definition clauses, provisos, Explanations, illustrations, Schedules, non-obstante clauses, punctuation, saving clauses | dictionaries, textbooks, historical setting, Statement of Objects and Reasons, committee reports, debates, statutes *in pari materia*, foreign decisions, usage |
| **When used** | first — the statute is read as a whole before anything outside it is opened | only when internal aids leave the meaning **ambiguous** |
| **Status** | several are **part of the Act** (preamble, Schedules, definition clauses, provisos, Explanations) | none is part of the Act |
| **Weight** | ranges from binding (definition clause) to slight (marginal note, punctuation) | persuasive at best; never overrides the enacted words |

<Callout kind="pointer" label="The single sentence that governs every aid, internal or external">
  <p>
    <strong>No aid — however weighty — can be used against the plain and unambiguous words of the
    statute.</strong> Aids resolve ambiguity; they do not create it. If an answer states this once, in
    terms, before listing the aids, it has secured the conceptual mark that most scripts miss. The
    corollary is equally examinable: because aids are for ambiguity, the <em>order of operations</em> is
    fixed — plain words first, then the statute read as a whole with its internal aids, and only then
    the material outside it.
  </p>
</Callout>

## Common mistakes &amp; examiner traps

1. **Reading "shall" as automatically mandatory.** It is a strong indicator, not a rule. Object,
   prejudice, and whether the statute prescribes its own consequence decide the question. A section
   that prescribes a **penalty** for breach is usually **directory**; one that declares the act
   **void** is mandatory.
2. **Applying ejusdem generis without finding a genus.** State the genus in a phrase before you use the
   maxim. No genus, no restriction. Also check that the genus is **not exhausted**, that the general
   words **follow** the specific ones, and that there is no "whatsoever" signalling a contrary
   intention.
3. **Confusing noscitur a sociis with ejusdem generis.** *Noscitur* colours a doubtful word from its
   companions and needs no general-words-after-specific-words structure. *Ejusdem generis* is the
   narrower, specialised application and does.
4. **Using the golden rule for hardship.** The trigger is **absurdity or repugnancy**, not harshness;
   and the departure is **only so far as necessary**.
5. **Treating the mischief rule as licence to rewrite.** It selects between readings the words can
   bear. It does not add words, and it has little force in penal or taxing statutes.
6. **Achieving "harmony" by ignoring a section.** Harmonious construction must leave **both**
   provisions with work to do. Reducing one to a dead letter is the failure the rule exists to prevent.
7. **Saying a preamble or a marginal note can override a section.** Nothing overrides plain words. The
   preamble is a key to the object; a marginal note is the weakest aid of all.
8. **Mixing up "means", "includes" and "means and includes".** *Means* is exhaustive; *includes*
   extends the ordinary meaning; *means and includes* is exhaustive despite the word "includes".
9. **Calling a proviso an independent enactment.** A proviso carves an exception out of the provision
   it is attached to, cannot enlarge that provision, and does not travel elsewhere in the Act.
10. **Getting the taxing-statute canons half right.** *Charging* provision — strict. *Exemption* —
    strict, burden on the assessee. *Machinery / procedural* provision — **liberal**, so the charge
    works. And there is **no equity about a tax**.
11. **Saying beneficial legislation is construed liberally in all circumstances.** Only where the
    provision is **capable of two constructions**; and a **penal provision inside a welfare Act** is
    still strictly construed.
12. **Confusing the retrospectivity presumptions.** **Substantive** provisions — presumed
    **prospective**. **Procedural** provisions — presumed **retrospective**, but never so as to destroy
    an accrued right or reopen a concluded proceeding.
13. **Forgetting that "subject to" is the mirror of "notwithstanding".** One yields; the other
    overrides.
14. **Quoting parliamentary debates as decisive.** Their use is limited — the mover's speech may show
    the object and the mischief; it does not fix the meaning of the enacted words.

## One-page revision summary

**The five primary rules, in sequence.** **Literal / grammatical** — plain words, ordinary meaning,
consequences irrelevant; no *casus omissus*. **Golden** — depart from the literal meaning only to avoid
**absurdity or repugnancy**, and only **so far as necessary** (narrow application: choose between two
meanings; wider application: modify the only meaning). **Mischief (Heydon)** — four questions: prior
law, mischief/defect, remedy appointed, true reason of the remedy; then **suppress the mischief and
advance the remedy**, defeating subtle evasions; for **ambiguous, remedial** statutes.
**Harmonious construction** — read the Act as a whole, give effect to **both** provisions, no dead
letter; if irreconcilable, special over general, then the non-obstante clause. **Purposive** — the
modern, wider successor of the mischief rule; looks to the **object**, needs no prior law, uses the
whole scheme; cannot give words a meaning they cannot bear.

**Subsidiary maxims.** *Noscitur a sociis* — a word is known by its companions. *Ejusdem generis* —
general words after specific words of a **genus** are confined to that genus; **four conditions**
(enumeration; a genus; genus not exhausted; general words follow, no contrary intention); it fails
where there is no genus, where the genus is exhausted, where "whatsoever" or a definition shows a
contrary intention, or where the general words come first. *Expressio unius est exclusio alterius* —
express mention of one excludes another; an inference only, defeated by "including", by oversight, or
by *ex abundanti cautela*. *Ut res magis valeat quam pereat* — prefer the workable, effective reading;
avoid redundancy. *Generalia specialibus non derogant* — the special prevails over the general, within
an Act and between Acts; no implied repeal of a special Act by a later general one. *Contemporanea
expositio* — contemporaneous official understanding and long usage are strong for **old** statutes,
weak for modern ones, and always yield to clear words. *Reddendo singula singulis* — refer each subject
to its own object.

**Presumptions (all rebuttable).** Legislating within jurisdiction · **constitutionality** (read down
where possible) · **against retrospectivity** — substantive **prospective**, procedural
**retrospective**, but never destroying an accrued right · **mens rea** in penal statutes · **against
ouster** of the civil court's jurisdiction · against changing existing law more than necessary ·
against implied repeal · against absurdity and injustice · territoriality · State not bound unless
named.

**Strict versus liberal.** **Penal** — strict; the accused is not caught unless clearly covered;
ambiguity favours the accused; mens rea presumed. **Taxing** — strict; **no equity about a tax**;
nothing read in, nothing implied; charge and exemption both strict (exemption's burden on the
**assessee**); **machinery provisions liberal**. **Beneficial / welfare** — liberal, in favour of the
class protected, but only where **two constructions are possible**, and penal provisions inside such an
Act stay strict. **Remedial** gives an individual a remedy; **penal** punishes a public wrong — ask who
receives the sanction.

**Mandatory versus directory.** "Shall" and "may" are **not conclusive**. Mandatory →
non-compliance **invalidates**; directory → substantial compliance suffices. Tests: **object** of the
provision; whether the statute states the **consequence** (void → mandatory; penalty only → usually
directory); **prejudice** to a protected person; serious **general inconvenience** from a public
officer's default; **public duty** (often directory) versus an **individual right or safeguard**
(usually mandatory); **negative or prohibitory** language ("no ... unless") → mandatory.

**Internal aids and their weight.** **Long title** — part of the Act, shows scope. **Short title** —
identification only. **Preamble** — object and policy; never against plain words. **Headings** —
ambiguity only; cannot control a section. **Marginal notes** — weakest; generally not part of the Act.
**Definition clause** — binding, subject to "unless the context otherwise requires"; **means**
(exhaustive) · **includes** (extensive) · **means and includes** (exhaustive) · **deemed** (legal
fiction, for the stated purpose only). **Proviso** — carves an exception out of its own provision;
never an independent enactment; cannot enlarge. **Explanation** — clarifies, may widen or narrow;
not free-standing. **Illustrations** — show intended working; the **section prevails** on conflict.
**Schedules** — part of the Act; the **enacting section prevails** on conflict. **Non-obstante** —
overrides; **"subject to"** — yields. **Punctuation** — minor. **Saving clause** — preserves existing
rights and proceedings.

**External aids and their limits.** Dictionaries (context governs; never over a statutory definition) ·
textbooks (persuasive) · historical setting · **Statement of Objects and Reasons** (background and
